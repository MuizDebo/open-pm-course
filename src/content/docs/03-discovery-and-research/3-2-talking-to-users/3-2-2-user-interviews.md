## Introduction

A user interview is the cheapest, fastest research tool a product manager has, and most of
them are wasted. The interviewer asks whether the user would like a feature, the user says
yes to be polite, the interviewer writes down "validated", and the team builds something
nobody uses. The problem is not the method. It is that people confuse asking questions with
learning something.

This lesson teaches the version that works. It is built on one rule, from Rob Fitzpatrick's
short book The Mom Test: ask about the person's life, not about your idea. Everything else,
from the script to the notes, follows from that rule. By the end you should be able to run a
thirty-minute conversation with a stranger and come out with facts you did not have going in.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The one rule: talk about their life, not your idea.
- Questions that produce facts and questions that produce compliments.
- Recruiting the right people and getting them to show up.
- A thirty-minute script you can use tomorrow.
- Taking notes so that the interview is still useful a month later.

## The one rule

The Mom Test takes its name from a thought experiment: could you ask your mother about your
business idea and learn the truth? Not if you ask her whether it is a good idea. She loves
you. But if you ask her what she did the last time she tried to do the thing your idea
addresses, how long it took, what annoyed her and what she paid, you get facts, and your
mother cannot lie about facts even to be kind.

That is the whole method. Questions about the person's past behaviour and current problems
produce information. Questions about your idea, the future, or hypotheticals produce
opinions, and opinions in an interview are worth nothing, because the person has every
reason to be nice and no stake in being right.

Three practical consequences:

1. Do not pitch. If the user does not know what you are building, they cannot tell you what
   you want to hear.
2. Ask about specifics. "Tell me about the last time" beats "do you usually".
3. Listen for what they did, not what they say they would do. Someone who complains about a
   problem but has never tried to solve it does not have the problem badly enough to pay for
   a solution.

## Questions that work and questions that do not

Questions that work:

- "Walk me through the last time you did X."
- "What was the hardest part of that?"
- "Why was that hard?" (and then again: "why?")
- "What did you do about it?" "What else have you tried?"
- "How much did that cost you, in time or money?"
- "Who else was involved?"
- "What happened next?"

Questions that do not:

- "Would you use a product that did X?" (Everyone says yes.)
- "Would you pay for it?" (Everyone says yes, and then does not.)
- "Do you think this is a good idea?" (See your mother.)
- "How often do you X?" (People are terrible at estimating. Ask about the last time
  instead and count.)
- Any question with the answer in it: "Is the checkout confusing?"

If you notice you have been talking for more than a minute at a stretch, you are pitching.
Stop and ask what they did last Tuesday.

## Recruiting

Who to talk to matters more than how well you interview. Five people who have the problem
will teach you more than fifty who do not. Define the profile first, in behavioural terms:
not "small business owners" but "people who have sent an invoice in the last month and
chased payment on at least one of them".

Where to find them: your existing users, filtered by behaviour; support tickets about the
problem; sales conversations; communities where the problem is discussed; your network, at a
pinch. Offer something for their time. A voucher, a donation to a charity they pick, early
access. Not because they need it but because it signals that you take their time seriously,
and it roughly doubles the show-up rate.

Expect a third of booked interviews not to happen. Book eight to get five.

## A thirty-minute script

You do not read a script aloud. You hold it as a map so you know where you are.

**Minutes 0 to 3: open.** Who you are, what this is for ("we're trying to understand how
people handle X, not testing you"), permission to take notes or record, and one easy warm-up
question about their role or day.

**Minutes 3 to 20: their story.** "Tell me about the last time you did X." Then follow
them. Your job is to keep them in the specifics and keep asking why. Have four or five
prompts ready for the areas you care about, but let the order come from them. Silence is
fine. Count to five before you fill it.

**Minutes 20 to 27: the edges.** What have they tried? What do they use now? What would have
to be true for them to change? What do they wish someone would fix? Still no pitching.

**Minutes 27 to 30: close.** "Is there anything I should have asked?" Then, if they fit the
profile, "would you be willing to talk again?" and "is there anyone else I should speak
to?" A referral from an interviewee is the best recruiting channel there is.

If you are new to this, run the first one with a colleague taking notes so you can give the
user your whole attention. Alternate roles.

## Notes that survive

An interview that lives only in your memory is gone within a week. Write up each one within
an hour, while you remember the tone. Two pages at most:

- Who they were, in profile terms, anonymised.
- The three to five things they said that surprised you, as close to their words as you can
  get. Quotes are the most persuasive artifact you will bring back to the team.
- What they actually do today, step by step.
- The pain, ranked by how much it seemed to cost them, not by how loudly they said it.
- What you would ask next time.

Keep every write-up in one place where the team can read them. Lesson 3.2.4 covers how to
turn a stack of these into decisions.

1. Read the [summary and sample chapter](https://www.momtestbook.com/) of The Mom Test on
   its own site, fifteen minutes. If you buy one book for this course, this is a strong
   candidate. It is short and almost every page is usable.
2. Read Nielsen Norman Group's [User Interviews 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/),
   fifteen minutes. The research-practice view of the same method, with more on preparation
   and ethics than the founder-facing sources give you.
3. Read Jakob Nielsen's [Interviewing Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interviewing-users/),
   ten minutes. A short, sceptical piece on what people can and cannot tell you about
   themselves, and why watching beats asking for some questions. It explains the limits of
   the method before you lean on it.
4. Write your interview guide for project 3.2.5: the profile you are recruiting, the opening,
   five prompts, and the close. One page. Then run a ten-minute practice interview on a
   friend about any everyday task, using only past-behaviour questions. Notice how hard it
   is not to pitch.
