## Introduction

Every product team says it is customer-focused. Ask how many users anyone on the team spoke
to last week and the room goes quiet. The gap between what teams say about users and what
they actually know about them is the single most common reason products fail, and it is not
a knowledge gap. Everyone knows research matters. It is a habit gap. Research is treated as a
project you do before building, when it should be a thing you do every week for as long as
the product exists.

This lesson is about closing that gap. It gives you the argument for why no amount of data,
cleverness or experience substitutes for talking to the people you are building for, where
the "get out of the building" idea came from, and a practical way to make contact with users
a fixed part of your week rather than a phase that ends when the roadmap is written.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why facts about users live outside the building and cannot be reasoned into existence.
- Customer development, the idea that made user contact a discipline rather than a nicety.
- The difference between research as a phase and research as a habit.
- What a weekly touchpoint looks like and how to set one up.
- The usual reasons teams stop talking to users, and what to do about each.

## The facts are outside

Inside the building you have opinions, data and documents. Opinions are cheap and everyone
has some. Data tells you what people did, in aggregate, in the past, inside the product you
already built. Documents tell you what someone once decided. None of these can tell you why a
user does what they do, what they are trying to accomplish, what they do instead of using
your product, or what they would pay to make a problem go away. Those facts exist only in
users' heads and in the places they live and work. The only way to get them is to go and
ask.

This sounds obvious and is routinely ignored, because asking is slow, awkward and humbling.
A planning meeting feels productive. A user interview that overturns the plan feels like a
setback. It is the opposite. The interview saved you three months.

## Customer development

The phrase "get out of the building" comes from Steve Blank, a serial founder who in the
early 2000s noticed that startups failed not for lack of product development but for lack of
customer development. They built what they imagined customers wanted, launched, and
discovered the truth too late to survive it. His fix was to treat finding customers as a
process as rigorous as building the product: form hypotheses about who the customer is and
what they need, then leave the office and test those hypotheses in conversation before
writing code.

Customer development became the foundation of the lean startup movement and, through it, of
modern product discovery. The vocabulary has changed. The core has not. You have guesses.
Users have facts. Go and get them.

For a product manager inside an established company the stakes are lower than for a founder,
and the principle is identical. A roadmap built on internal opinion is a set of untested
hypotheses with a budget attached.

## Research as a phase versus research as a habit

Most teams do research the way they do a kitchen renovation: a concentrated burst, then
years of living with the result. A discovery sprint, a round of interviews, a report, and
then the team builds for a year on what it learned in a fortnight. By month six the findings
are stale, the market has moved, and the team is making decisions on memory.

Teresa Torres, whose work on continuous discovery has shaped how the best teams operate,
argues that the unit of research should be the week, not the project. Her benchmark is
simple: the team that owns a product should talk to at least one customer every week, as a
standing habit, without needing a reason. The interviews are short. The purpose is not to
answer a specific question but to keep the team's model of the user current, so that when a
specific question comes up, there is already a bank of recent conversations to draw on and a
list of people to call.

This is the habit this course wants you to build. The project at the end of this section
starts it.

## Setting up a weekly touchpoint

The practical obstacle is never the conversation. It is the recruiting. Teams that talk to
users weekly have solved recruiting once and automated it. Options, in rough order of effort:

- **Piggyback on support and sales.** Ask to sit in on three support calls or one sales demo
  a week. You are not running the call. You are listening and taking notes. This costs you
  an hour and needs nobody's permission beyond the person on the call.
- **A standing invitation in the product.** A one-line prompt after a key action: "Have
  fifteen minutes to tell us how this went? We'll send a voucher." Route responses to a
  calendar link. Once set up, this produces interviews without you lifting a finger.
- **A customer panel.** Twenty users who have agreed to be contacted. Rotate through them.
  Refresh it every quarter so you are not only hearing from your fans.
- **Your own network, for early-stage work.** Friends of friends who fit the profile. Worse
  sample, zero cost, fine for a first pass.

Then block the slot. Same time every week, thirty minutes, with a second person from the
team present if you can get one. Engineers and designers who sit in on interviews build
better products than ones who read your summary, and they stop treating your conclusions as
opinion.

## Why teams stop, and what to do

- **"We already know our users."** You know your users as of the last time you spoke to
  them. Ask when that was.
- **"We have the data."** Data tells you what. Interviews tell you why. You need both and
  the second is the one teams skip.
- **"Sales owns the customer relationship."** Sales owns the commercial relationship. Ask
  to join their calls as an observer. Most will say yes if you stay quiet.
- **"We don't have time."** One hour a week. If the team genuinely cannot find one hour a
  week to speak to the people it is building for, that is the problem to solve first.
- **"Users don't know what they want."** True, and irrelevant. You are not asking them what
  to build. You are asking what they do, what is hard and what they have tried. The next
  lesson is about how.

   [Why the Lean Start-Up Changes Everything](https://hbr.org/2013/05/why-the-lean-start-up-changes-everything)

   development from the person who named it, and of why building first and asking later

   [overview of continuous discovery](https://www.producttalk.org/2021/08/product-discovery/),
   fifteen minutes. The argument for weekly contact over periodic projects, and the habits
   that make it stick. This course adopts her weekly benchmark.
3. Read Marty Cagan's [Product Discovery](https://www.svpg.com/product-discovery/), ten
   minutes. Where user contact sits inside the larger discovery process, and the four risks
   it is there to reduce.

   thirty-five minutes. A founder-facing talk, and the best single introduction to what you
   are trying to learn in a conversation and the mistakes that stop you learning it. The
   next lesson builds on it.
5. Set up one recruiting channel from the list above. Not the best one, the one you can do
   this week. Write down what it is and when your first conversation is booked. You will
   need five interviews for project 3.2.5.
