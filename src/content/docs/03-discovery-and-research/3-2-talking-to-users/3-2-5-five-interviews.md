## Introduction

Everything in this section has been preparation for this. You will recruit five people who
have the problem you framed in project 3.1.4, interview each of them for thirty minutes
using the method from lesson 3.2.2, and synthesise what you learned into one page using
lesson 3.2.4. The artifact is the script, the anonymised notes and the synthesis.

This is the project most learners find hardest, because the hard part is not intellectual.
It is sending the messages, chasing the no-shows and sitting through the first awkward
conversation. It is also the project that most changes how you think. Nobody who has done
five real interviews on a problem goes back to guessing.

## Before you start

You need a problem and a profile. The problem is the one from your audience map in project
3.1.4; if you did not do that project, pick any problem you have personally and can find
other people who share. The profile is behavioural: what someone must have done recently to
count as having the problem. Write it in one sentence before you recruit anyone.

Expect the whole project to take six to ten hours across two weeks: an hour to set up
recruiting, two and a half hours of interviews, two hours of write-ups, two hours of
synthesis. Book the interviews before you do anything else, because they set the schedule.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. **Write the interview guide.** One page: the profile in one sentence, your opening
   (who you are, what this is for, permission to take notes), five prompts about past
   behaviour and current workarounds, and your close (anything I should have asked, anyone
   else I should speak to). No question about your idea and no question about the future.
   Finish this before recruiting; it tells you whom to recruit.
2. **Recruit and book eight people.** Use the channel you set up in lesson 3.2.1. Offer
   something for their time. Eight bookings gets you five interviews. Keep a short log of
   where each person came from and whether they showed, because recruiting is a skill you
   are also building.
3. **Run five interviews of about thirty minutes each.** Record if the person agrees; take
   notes either way. Stay on past behaviour. Count to five before filling a silence. Ask
   why at least three times per interview. If you can, have a second person take notes for
   at least two of them.
4. **Write up each interview within a day**, using the structure from lesson 3.2.2: profile
   (anonymised), what surprised you, what they actually do today, the pain ranked by cost,
   what to ask next time. Two pages each at most. Replace names and identifying details
   before anything goes in your repository.
5. **Synthesise.** Break all five write-ups into observations, affinity-map them, and write
   three to seven insight statements in the shape from lesson 3.2.4, each with a quote and
   the interviews that support it. Keep "what they said", "what it means" and "what we might
   do" in separate sections. One page.
6. **Reflect.** Add a short section: what you believed about the problem before the
   interviews, what you believe now, and the one finding you least expected. If nothing
   surprised you, say so and say what you would change about the guide.

#### Extra credit

- Run a sixth and seventh interview with people who do not fit the profile but are adjacent
  to it. Note what changes. This is the fastest way to find the edge of your market.
- Turn your three strongest quotes into thirty-second audio clips (with permission) and
  include them. Then play one to someone who has not read your synthesis and ask what they
  think the problem is.
- Draft the survey you would send to measure how widely your top insight holds, using the
  rules from lesson 3.2.3. Five questions at most. Do not send it yet.

</div>

## Submitting your work

Post a link to your artifact in this project's thread in
[GitHub Discussions](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/discussions). Review at
least two other submissions while you are there. In your review, check whether the insight
statements are actually supported by the quotes, and whether the guide avoided asking about
the idea.
