## Introduction

Five interviews produce ten pages of notes, a hundred quotes, and a feeling that you learned
something. That feeling is not an insight. Synthesis is the work of turning raw conversation
into a small number of clear, evidenced statements about users that a team can build on,
argue with, and remember next quarter.

It is the step most teams skip. The interviews happen, the PM comes back and says "users
want X", and the notes sit in a folder. Without synthesis, research becomes one more opinion
in the room, the PM's, with a slightly better anecdote attached. With it, research becomes
the thing the team's decisions rest on. This lesson gives you a method that takes an
afternoon.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why a summary is not a synthesis.
- Affinity mapping: finding the patterns in a pile of observations.
- Writing insight statements that are specific, evidenced and surprising.
- Separating what users said from what it means and what to do about it.
- Sharing findings so the team acts on them instead of nodding.

## Summary versus synthesis

A summary tells you what each person said. A synthesis tells you what is true across all of
them and why it matters. The test: a summary gets longer with every interview; a synthesis
gets shorter and sharper.

"Three of five users mentioned the export feature" is a summary. "Users export data to a
spreadsheet because they do not trust the in-app totals, and they do it before every client
meeting" is a synthesis. The second one tells the team what the problem actually is (trust,
not export), when it bites (before meetings), and points at a fix nobody asked for
(make the totals trustworthy). It also makes a claim that could be wrong, which is what
makes it useful.

## Affinity mapping

The standard method for finding patterns across interviews is the affinity map. It is
physical, slightly tedious, and works.

1. **Break the notes into observations.** Go through every write-up and pull out each
   distinct thing a user did, said, or felt, one per sticky note or card, with a code for
   which interview it came from. A thirty-minute interview yields twenty to forty. Quote
   where you can. Do not interpret yet.
2. **Put them all up.** A wall, a whiteboard, a digital board. All interviews mixed together.
3. **Group by similarity, without labels.** Move notes that seem to be about the same thing
   next to each other. Do this in silence if there is more than one of you; talking turns it
   into a debate about categories before the categories exist. Groups will form, split and
   merge. Let them.
4. **Name the groups.** Only once the groups are stable, write a label for each that
   describes the pattern, not the topic. "Export" is a topic. "Workarounds for not trusting
   the numbers" is a pattern.
5. **Look at the shape.** Which groups are large? Which came from every interview and which
   from one person? Which surprised you? A group with notes from four of five interviews is
   a finding. A group from one interview is a lead.

Doing this with the engineers and designer who sat in on the interviews is better than
doing it alone. They saw different things and they will trust findings they helped find.

## Insight statements

Each major group becomes an insight statement: one or two sentences that say what is true
about users, with the evidence attached. A useful shape:

> [Who] [does or experiences what] because [underlying reason], which means [consequence
> for them]. (Evidence: interviews 1, 3, 4, 5; quotes below.)

Good insight statements are specific enough to be wrong, evidenced enough to be believed,
and surprising enough to be worth saying. "Users want it to be faster" fails all three.
"Freelancers chase late invoices by hand because they do not know a client is late until
they check their bank, so the problem is noticing, not chasing" passes.

Aim for three to seven. If you have fifteen, you have summarised again. Rank them by how
many interviews support them and how much the problem seemed to cost the user.

## Said, means, do

Keep three columns apart in your head and on the page:

- **What they said.** The quotes. Unarguable.
- **What it means.** Your interpretation. Arguable, and should be argued.
- **What we might do.** Options, not decisions. Belongs in Course 4, not in the research
  write-up.

Teams go wrong when the columns collapse: a quote becomes a requirement without anyone
noticing the interpretation in between. Writing them separately makes the leap visible and
lets someone challenge it, which is exactly what you want before anything gets built.

## Sharing findings

The synthesis only matters if it changes what the team does. Three habits that make that
likely:

- **One page.** The insight statements, ranked, with one quote each. Everything else is an
  appendix. Nobody reads a twenty-page research report; everybody reads one page with a
  surprising quote on it.
- **Lead with what surprised you.** Findings that confirm what the team believed get a nod.
  Findings that contradict it get attention. Put those first.
- **Bring the users into the room.** Short audio clips or verbatim quotes in the user's own
  words are more persuasive than any chart. A team that hears a real person describe the
  problem stops arguing about whether it exists.

Then keep the one-pager where the team plans. When a roadmap discussion starts drifting
toward opinion, point at it.
