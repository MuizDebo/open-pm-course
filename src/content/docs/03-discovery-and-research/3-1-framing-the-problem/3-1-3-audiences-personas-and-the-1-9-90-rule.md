## Introduction

"Everyone" is not an audience. A product built for everyone is built for nobody in
particular, and nobody in particular is the person who does not show up. Discovery starts
with deciding who you are talking about, which means dividing a market into groups that
behave differently, picking the one to serve first, and describing them in a way the whole
team can hold in their heads.

This lesson covers those three moves: segments, early adopters, and personas. It also
covers the 1/9/90 rule, which explains why most of your users will never do the thing your
product is designed around, and why that is fine.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Segments: dividing a market by behaviour, not by demographics.
- Early adopters, and why the first audience is not the biggest one.
- The 1/9/90 rule of participation and what it means for your plans.
- Personas: what they are for, how they go wrong, and how to keep them honest.
- The wedge: picking one segment to win before the others.

## Segments

A segment is a group of people who share a circumstance and therefore behave the same way
toward your product. The word "circumstance" is doing the work. Age, income, location and
job title are easy to collect and usually poor predictors of behaviour. What someone is
trying to do, how often, under what constraints and with what alternatives predicts it far
better.

For a tool that helps people send invoices, "freelancers" is a demographic. "People who
invoice fewer than five clients a month and are paid late by at least one of them" is a
segment. The second group shares a job, a pain and a likely reaction to your product. The
first group includes people who will never have the problem.

Build your segments from the jobs in the previous lesson. For each job, ask: who has it most
often, most painfully, with the fewest good alternatives? Each distinct answer is a
candidate segment. Three to five is usually plenty at the start; more than that and you are
describing individuals.

## Early adopters

Every new product's first users are unusual. They have the problem worse than most, they
are willing to try something unfinished, and they will forgive rough edges in exchange for
being helped first. They are also willing to talk to you, which is the most valuable thing a
user can do for a product that does not exist yet.

The technology adoption curve describes a sequence: innovators, early adopters, the early
majority, the late majority, laggards. The important part for a PM is that the groups want
different things. Early adopters want a solution to a painful problem and will tolerate
incompleteness. The early majority want something proven, polished and safe, and will not
try it until people like them already have. A product that tries to please the majority on
day one has no users to prove anything with.

So the first audience is deliberately small and deliberately strange. You are not looking
for the biggest segment. You are looking for the segment that needs you most, can be
reached, and will tell you the truth. Win them, learn from them, then expand.

## The 1/9/90 rule

In almost any product with a community or a creative component, roughly one percent of
users create most of the content, about nine percent contribute occasionally, and ninety
percent only consume. The numbers vary by product; the shape does not. It was first
documented in online forums and holds for review sites, marketplaces, social products and
most things with a "post" button.

Why it matters for discovery:

- **Your loudest users are the one percent.** The people who answer surveys, join your
  Discord and file bug reports are not representative. Design for them alone and you build
  a product for power users that the ninety percent find bewildering.
- **The ninety percent are not failing.** A user who reads and never posts is getting value.
  A plan that depends on converting them to creators is usually a plan to be disappointed.
- **Plan the numbers honestly.** If your product needs a thousand active contributors, you
  need something like a hundred thousand users. That changes the acquisition problem
  entirely.

When you draw your audience map in the project, mark which segments are likely to be
creators and which consumers. They are different audiences with different jobs even when
they use the same product.

## Personas

A persona is a short, concrete description of a typical member of a segment: a name, a
circumstance, a job to be done, the alternatives they use now, what they fear and what
would make them switch. Its purpose is to give the team one person to argue about instead of
a statistical abstraction. "Would Amara use this?" is a better design question than "would
segment B use this?"

Personas go wrong in predictable ways:

- **They are invented.** A persona with no interviews behind it is fiction. Fiction that
  gets printed and pinned to a wall becomes a fact nobody checks.
- **They are demographic.** "Amara, 34, Lagos, marketing manager, likes yoga" tells you
  nothing about what she is trying to do or why she might hire your product. The yoga is
  decoration.
- **They are flattering.** Teams write personas who would love the product. Real segments
  include people who are sceptical, busy, and currently fine with a spreadsheet.
- **They never change.** A persona written at the start and never revised becomes a
  monument to what the team used to believe.

Keeping them honest is simple: every claim in a persona should trace to something a real
person said or did, with a date. Rewrite them after every round of research. Keep them
short enough to reread in a minute. And write one for the person who does not want your
product, because that persona is the one that predicts churn.

## The wedge

With segments drawn and early adopters identified, you choose a wedge: the one segment you
will win first, so thoroughly that they tell others. The wedge is small on purpose. A
product that is the obvious choice for three-person plumbing firms can grow into one for all
trades, then all small businesses. A product that is a reasonable choice for all small
businesses is nobody's obvious choice and grows into nothing.

The test of a good wedge:

- The segment has the problem painfully and often.
- You can reach them, meaning you know where they gather and someone will introduce you.
- They can pay, or the business model does not need them to yet.
- Winning them creates a path to the next segment, through shared tools, shared people or
  shared reputation.

Choosing the wedge is a decision, and like all decisions in this course it is a hypothesis.
The interviews in section 3.2 will tell you whether you picked right.

   alternative, and a guess at whether they are early adopters. Pick the one you would
   wedge with. You will formalise this in the project.
