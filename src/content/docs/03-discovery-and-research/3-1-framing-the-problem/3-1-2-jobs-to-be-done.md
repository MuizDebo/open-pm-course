## Introduction

People do not buy products. They hire them to do a job. That sentence is the whole of jobs
to be done, and it is more useful than it sounds, because it moves your attention from what
the product is to what the person is trying to get done, in what circumstance, and what
they would do instead.

The framework has a famous story attached, a small set of ideas worth knowing, and a lot of
consultancy built on top. This lesson gives you the ideas, tells the story once, and is
honest about where the framework stops helping.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The core idea: a job, a circumstance, and the progress someone is seeking.
- The milkshake story and what it actually teaches.
- Functional, emotional and social dimensions of a job.
- The forces that make people switch, or not.
- The limits of the framework and the ways it gets misused.

## The core idea

A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance. Not a task
on a list, and not a demographic attribute. A commuter with twenty minutes to kill and one
free hand has a job. A parent at the end of a long day with a child who wants a treat has a
different job. The same product can be hired for both, and it will be judged on different
things each time.

Three parts to hold onto:

- **The circumstance.** When does the job arise? Where, with whom, under what pressure?
  Circumstance explains behaviour far better than attributes do. Two people with identical
  profiles behave differently in different situations; one person behaves the same way as a
  stranger in the same situation.
- **The progress sought.** What does "done" look like to them? Usually it is not the thing
  the product does but what that makes possible.
- **The competing hires.** What else do they hire for this job, including nothing? Your
  competition is whatever the person does instead, and it is often a workaround, a habit or
  simply putting up with it.

## The milkshake story

A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes. It improved the shakes according to what
customers said they wanted, and sales did not move. Researchers then watched who bought
shakes and when. A large share were sold before eight in the morning, to people alone in
cars, who drove away with them. Interviewed, those buyers described the job: a long, boring
commute, a free hand, and the need for something that would last the whole drive and keep
them full until lunch. The shake was competing with bananas, doughnuts and bagels, and
winning because it was thick and took twenty minutes to finish.

The same product was hired in the afternoon by parents for a completely different job:
saying yes to a child without guilt. For that job the shake was too thick and took too long.

What the story teaches is not "make the shake thicker". It is that asking customers what
they want produced nothing, while watching when they bought and asking about the
circumstance produced two jobs with opposite requirements. The method is observation and
circumstance, not surveys and segments.

## Three dimensions

Every job has more than one dimension, and products that nail one while ignoring the others
get hired and then fired.

- **Functional.** The practical task. Get to work. Send the invoice. Store the photo.
- **Emotional.** How the person wants to feel while doing it, and afterwards. In control.
  Not stupid. Safe.
- **Social.** How they want to be seen by others. Competent. Generous. Not the kind of
  person who uses that other product.

A budgeting app that gets the functional job right and makes the user feel ashamed every
time they open it will lose to one that is slightly worse at the sums. A PM who only writes
functional requirements will keep being surprised by this.

## The forces of switching

People stay with inadequate solutions far longer than product teams expect. Jobs theory
describes four forces acting on a decision to switch:

- **Push** of the current situation: what is frustrating about the thing they do now.
- **Pull** of the new solution: what they imagine the new thing will give them.
- **Anxiety** about the new solution: will it work, what if I lose my data, what will it
  cost me to learn.
- **Habit** of the present: the sunk effort, the known workarounds, the comfort.

Push and pull have to outweigh anxiety and habit, or nothing happens. Most product teams
work entirely on pull, adding features, when the thing holding the customer back is anxiety,
which features do not touch. Discovery interviews, next section, are where you find out
which force is actually in play.

## Limits and misuse

The framework is good at one thing: forcing you to look at circumstance and progress instead
of features and demographics. It is not a complete method, and it has been stretched into
one by people selling it. Watch for:

- **Job statements so abstract they explain nothing.** "Help me live a better life" is not a
  job you can design for. If the statement would fit any product, it is too high.
- **Jobs invented in a conference room.** The milkshake jobs came from standing in a
  restaurant for a day. If nobody has watched or interviewed real people, you have a guess,
  not a job.
- **Treating it as a replacement for everything else.** Jobs tell you why someone might
  hire you. They do not tell you how big the market is, what it costs to serve, or whether
  the person will pay. Those come from the rest of this course.
- **Arguing about definitions.** There are at least two schools of jobs theory that
  disagree about what a job is. Neither disagreement has ever shipped a product. Pick the
  framing that helps and move on.

Used with those cautions, jobs to be done is the best single question to carry into a user
interview: what were you trying to get done, and what did you do before?

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Clayton Christensen and colleagues'
   [Know Your Customers' "Jobs to Be Done"](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done),
   twenty minutes. The canonical statement of the framework, with the milkshake story and
   several others. Read it for the argument that correlation in customer data is not the
   same as causation in customer behaviour.
2. Watch Christensen tell the milkshake story himself in
   [Understanding the Job](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sfGtw2C95Ms), six minutes.
   Short, and the best version of it.
3. Read Lenny Rachitsky's interview with Bob Moesta,
   [The ultimate guide to JTBD](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-ultimate-guide-to-jtbd-bob-moesta),
   twenty-five minutes. Moesta ran the original milkshake research and is the clearest
   voice on how to actually interview for jobs. Pay attention to the four forces.
4. Take the problem statement you wrote in lesson 3.1.1 and rewrite it as a job: who, in
   what circumstance, trying to make what progress, currently hiring what instead. Add the
   emotional and social dimensions. Note which of the four forces you think is strongest,
   and admit that you are guessing until the interviews in section 3.2.

</div>

  for the context: jobs theory grew out of his work on disruptive innovation, and the two
  make more sense together.
