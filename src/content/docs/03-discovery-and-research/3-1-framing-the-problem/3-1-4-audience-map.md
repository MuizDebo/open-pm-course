## Introduction

This project produces the first artifact for a product of your own: an audience map. It
lists who might use the product, divides them into segments by circumstance, estimates
roughly how many there are, identifies the early adopters, and chooses the wedge you would
win first.

It is also the moment you choose the product idea itself. That choice matters more than
usual, because the same product carries through most of the projects that follow: the
interviews in 3.2, the opportunity tree in 3.3, the strategy and roadmap in Course 4, the
PRD and launch plan in Course 5, the metrics in Course 6, and the AI feature in Course 7.
By the capstone you will have a full portfolio built around it. Choose something you can
stay interested in for six months.

## Choosing your product

Any of these works:

- **A product you want to build.** The best option if you have one. Real stakes, real
  motivation, and the research might turn into something.
- **A product you use and would change.** You already know the domain and can find other
  users easily.
- **A product that exists and you would compete with.** Pick a known product and imagine
  entering its market. You will have to find the gap, which is good practice.

What does not work: a product whose users you cannot reach. Section 3.2 asks you to
interview five of them. If you cannot picture how you would find five people in the
audience, pick a different product. A product for nurses is a fine idea if you know nurses.
A product for airline pilots is a fine idea if you do not mind that the interviews will not
happen.

Avoid, for now, a product whose users are mostly other businesses' procurement departments.
B2B is a fine career; it is a hard place to do your first discovery, because the people who
buy are not the people who use, and both are difficult to reach cold.

Write the idea down in two sentences: what it is and who it is for. Expect both to change.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. **State the product and the problem.** Two sentences for the product. Then a problem
   statement in the template from lesson 3.1.1, and the job it serves in the form from
   lesson 3.1.2. These three things go at the top of your map.
2. **List candidate segments.** Brainstorm every group who might have the problem, then
   group them by circumstance using the rules from lesson 3.1.3. Aim for four to six
   segments. For each, write:
   - A one-line description by circumstance, not demographics.
   - What they do about the problem today, including nothing.
   - How often and how painfully the problem occurs for them, on a rough low, medium, high
     scale.
   - Whether they are likely creators or consumers, if your product has that split.
3. **Size each segment roughly.** Order-of-magnitude estimates are enough: tens, hundreds,
   thousands, hundreds of thousands. Say how you arrived at each number in one line. Public
   statistics, a government census, an industry body's figures, a count of a relevant online
   community, or a chain of reasoning from a number you do know. Precision is not the point;
   showing your working is.
4. **Identify early adopters.** Within each segment, who has it worst and is most willing
   to try something new? Mark them. If a segment has no plausible early adopters, note
   that; it is a sign the segment is for later.
5. **Choose the wedge.** Pick one segment to win first and defend the choice against the
   four tests in lesson 3.1.3: pain, reach, ability to pay, path to the next segment. Name
   the segment you would expand to second, and why the wedge leads there.
6. **Draw it.** The map itself is a single page: the product at the centre, the segments
   around it sized roughly by your estimates, early adopters marked, the wedge highlighted,
   and an arrow to the second segment. Hand-drawn and photographed is fine. Excalidraw is
   fine. It should be readable by someone who has not seen your notes.
7. **Write the honesty section.** A short list of the claims on the map that you have no
   evidence for yet. Most of them, at this stage. This list becomes the interview plan in
   section 3.2.
8. **Submit.** Put the written sections and the drawing in a folder called
   `3-1-4-audience-map` in your work repository. Post the link in the project's Discussions
   thread using the template from lesson 0.3, and review two other maps. When reviewing,
   the most useful question is whether you could find five members of the chosen wedge if
   you had to.

#### Extra credit

- Run the five whys on your problem statement with someone who disagrees that it is a
  problem. Record where the chains diverge.
- Find one existing product that serves your wedge today, even badly, and write three lines
  on why its users put up with it. That is the habit force from lesson 3.1.2, and you will
  need to overcome it.
