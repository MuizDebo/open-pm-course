## Introduction

You have an outcome you care about, an audience map, five interviews and a synthesis. This
project puts them into one structure: an opportunity solution tree. It is the artifact that
turns research into a decision about what to build next, and the one a product trio keeps
coming back to every week.

A tree does something a list of insights cannot. It forces you to say which outcome you are
driving, which opportunities would move it, which of those you are choosing to pursue and
why, and what you would need to see to believe a solution works. Done honestly, it exposes
the gap between what you learned and what you had already decided to build.

## What goes in a tree

Four levels, top to bottom:

- **Outcome.** One product outcome, stated as a measurable change in behaviour. "Increase
  the share of retailers who record a sale on the day it happens," not "launch bookkeeping".
  Lesson 3.3.3 explains why an outcome and not a feature.
- **Opportunities.** Needs, pain points and desires you heard in the interviews, phrased in
  the customer's terms, grouped from specific to general. Each one should trace back to a
  moment in a transcript.
- **Solutions.** Ideas that could address a chosen opportunity. Several per opportunity, so
  you are comparing rather than confirming.
- **Assumption tests.** The cheapest thing you could do to learn whether a solution is
  worth building. A fake door, a concierge version, a five-minute usability test.

The shape should be wide at the opportunity level and narrow below it. A tree with one
opportunity and six solutions is a roadmap wearing a disguise.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. **State the outcome.** One line at the top of the tree. If you are tempted to put a
   feature there, go back to lesson 3.3.3 and the product outcomes post linked from it.
2. **Build the opportunity space from your interviews.** Go through the synthesis from
   project 3.2.5 and the anonymised notes. Pull out every need, pain or desire you heard
   and write each as a short sentence in the customer's words. Aim for ten to twenty. Then
   group them: specific opportunities under broader parent opportunities, two or three
   levels deep. Mark the ones you heard from more than one person.
3. **Choose a target opportunity.** Pick one, and write a short paragraph on why: how many
   people have it, how painful it seemed, whether it is reachable with what your team can
   build, and what you would be giving up by not choosing the others. Note where your
   interview data was too thin to decide and what you would ask next time.
4. **Generate solutions.** At least three for the target opportunity, briefly described.
   Include one you think is bad; it helps the comparison.
5. **Write the assumption tests.** For each solution, list the riskiest assumption (will
   people do it, can we build it, will it move the outcome) and the smallest test that would
   tell you. Put a time estimate on each test.
6. **Draw it.** Any tool is fine. Excalidraw, FigJam, a whiteboard photo. Export a PNG and
   write a one-page companion note that walks a reader from the outcome to your first test.
   Submit both in a folder called `3-3-4-opportunity-solution-tree`.

#### Extra credit

- Run one of the assumption tests this week and add the result to the tree.
- Rebuild the tree with a different target opportunity and compare the two in a paragraph.
  Which would you choose now, and did the exercise change your mind?
