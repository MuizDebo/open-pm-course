## Introduction

Every product has competitors, including the ones that claim not to. If nobody else is
solving the problem, the competitor is whatever people do today: a spreadsheet, a WhatsApp
group, a notebook, nothing. A product manager who does not know what the alternatives are
cannot explain why anyone should switch, and cannot see the attack coming.

Competitive analysis is also the fastest way to ruin a roadmap. Teams that watch competitors
too closely end up building the same thing a year later with fewer resources. This lesson
is about doing the work well: learning what competitors reveal about the market and about
customers, deciding where you will be different on purpose, and then putting the analysis
away.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Who your competitors actually are, including the non-obvious ones.
- Teardowns: what to look for and how to record it.
- Positioning: choosing the frame of reference and the difference that matters.
- Finding where incumbents are structurally weak.
- The copying trap and how to stay out of it.

## Who you are really up against

List competitors in four rings, from the inside out:

1. **Direct.** Products that solve the same problem for the same people in roughly the same
   way. These are the ones everyone lists.
2. **Indirect.** Products that solve the same problem differently. A bookkeeping app's
   indirect competitors include an accountant, a POS terminal's reports and a bank's
   statement.
3. **Substitutes.** What people do instead, including nothing. The notebook, the memory, the
   trusted relative who "handles the money".
4. **Future.** Who could enter easily. The bank, the telco, the payments company that
   already has the retailer's transactions.

The second and third rings usually matter most, because they are what your customers are
actually using today. Your interviews from section 3.2 should have told you what those are.
If they did not, that is a gap to close before you go further.

## Teardowns

A teardown is a structured walk through a competitor's product, recorded so that the team
can see it without each doing it themselves. For each product, capture:

- **Who it is for.** The homepage headline, the pricing tiers and the case studies tell you
  who they have chosen, and who they have ignored.
- **The core loop.** Sign up, do the main thing, come back. Screenshot every step. Note where
  it is slow, confusing or delightful.
- **What they charge and how.** Pricing is the clearest statement of a company's beliefs
  about its customers.
- **What they say about themselves.** Positioning words, the category they claim, the
  comparison they invite.
- **What their users say.** App store reviews, forum threads, support communities. Sort by
  one star and read twenty. Then sort by five stars and read twenty. The gap between the two
  is the product.

Keep teardowns in a shared folder with a date. Redo the important ones every six months.
A teardown older than a year is a historical document.

## Positioning

Positioning is the decision about how customers should think about your product: what it
is, who it is for, what it is an alternative to, and why it is better for those people at
that job. April Dunford's work is the clearest practical treatment and is in the
assignment. The pieces that matter most for a PM:

- **Competitive alternatives.** What would the customer do if your product did not exist?
  That, not your category label, is the real frame of reference.
- **Unique attributes.** What you have that the alternatives do not. Be specific. "Easy to
  use" is not an attribute; "works without data for a week" is.
- **Value.** What those attributes let the customer do or avoid.
- **Who cares most.** The segment for whom that value is decisive, not just nice.
- **Market category.** The frame that makes the value obvious. Sometimes that is the
  obvious category, sometimes a neighbouring one, occasionally a new one.

Positioning is not a marketing exercise bolted on at launch. It is a product decision made
early, and every feature either reinforces it or dilutes it.

## Where incumbents are weak

Large competitors are rarely beaten head-on. They are beaten where their structure stops
them responding. Look for:

- **Customers they cannot afford to serve.** Too small, too far away, too low-margin. The
  classic entry point.
- **Jobs they treat as secondary.** A feature buried three menus deep that a segment uses
  every day.
- **Business models that constrain them.** A company paid per transaction struggles to
  launch a flat-fee product. One paid by advertisers struggles to sell privacy.
- **Channels they have outgrown.** Direct sales teams cannot profitably sell to people who
  buy from an app store.
- **Trust they have lost.** Read the one-star reviews again.

Michael Porter's framework for industry forces, linked below, is the classic lens for this.
Ben Thompson's aggregation theory is the modern one for internet markets, where the
incumbent's strength is often the demand side rather than supply.

## The copying trap

Teams that study competitors carefully tend to build what competitors have. It feels safe:
the feature exists, customers mentioned it, the gap is on a slide. Three problems:

- **You are seeing the output, not the reasoning.** You do not know why they built it, for
  whom, or whether it worked. Half of what shipped at any company was a mistake someone
  has not admitted yet.
- **You will arrive late with less.** Matching a feature a year after a better-resourced
  competitor shipped it earns you parity at best.
- **It erases your positioning.** Every copied feature moves you toward the category centre,
  where the biggest player wins by default.

The discipline is to use competitors as evidence about the market and the customer, and then
decide your own product from your own outcome, your own interviews and your own
positioning. If a competitor's feature is genuinely table stakes, build it quickly, make it
boring, and spend your creativity elsewhere.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read the introduction to April Dunford's
   [Obviously Awesome](https://www.aprildunford.com/obviously-awesome) on her site, and
   browse [her writing](https://www.aprildunford.com/) on positioning, thirty minutes in
   total. Her five-component model above comes from this work. Read it for the idea that
   the competitive alternative is what the customer would do, not what the analyst lists.
2. Read Michael Porter's [What Is Strategy?](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy) in
   HBR, forty minutes. Long and worth it. The argument that strategy is choosing what not
   to do is the antidote to the copying trap, and the distinction between operational
   effectiveness and strategic position explains why feature parity does not win.
3. Read Ben Thompson's [Aggregation Theory](https://stratechery.com/2015/aggregation-theory/),
   fifteen minutes. It explains why some incumbents are nearly impossible to attack on
   supply and must be attacked on a different axis entirely. Decide which of your
   competitors, if any, is an aggregator.
4. Do a teardown of your product's two most important competitors, including at least one
   indirect competitor or substitute, using the structure above. Then write your own
   positioning in Dunford's five parts. Keep both; the strategy pack in Course 4 builds on
   them.

## Additional resources

- The Wikipedia entries on
  [Porter's five forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis) and
  [SWOT analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SWOT_analysis) are fine ten-minute refreshers
  on the two frameworks you will be asked about in interviews. Use them as checklists, not as
  the analysis itself.
- [Playing to Win](https://www.playingtowin.com/), Roger Martin and A.G. Lafley's strategy
  framework, for the "where to play, how to win" questions that competitive analysis should
  feed. Course 4 returns to it.
- Basecamp's [Getting Real](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal), a short free book from 2006,
  has a chapter on why you should not copy competitors that has aged well.
