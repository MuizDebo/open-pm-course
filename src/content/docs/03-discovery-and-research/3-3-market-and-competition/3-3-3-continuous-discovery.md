## Introduction

Everything in this course so far has treated discovery as something you do before you
build: frame the problem, run interviews, size the market, then decide. That is how most
teams do it, and it is why most teams stop learning the moment the roadmap is agreed. Six
months later the interviews are stale, the assumptions have hardened into facts, and the
product has drifted from the people it was for.

Continuous discovery is the alternative: a small, steady stream of customer contact and
cheap experiments that runs alongside delivery, every week, for as long as the product
exists. Teresa Torres gave it its name and its best-known tools, and this lesson is built
on her work. It is the practice that makes the rest of Course 3 a habit instead of a
project.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What "continuous" means in practice: a weekly touchpoint, not a quarterly study.
- The product trio and why discovery is not the PM's job alone.
- Outcomes over outputs, and the difference between a business outcome and a product outcome.
- The opportunity solution tree as the map of what you have learned and decided.
- Assumption testing: the smallest experiment that could change your mind.

## Weekly, not quarterly

The core commitment is simple and most teams fail it: talk to at least one customer every
week. Not a research project, not a survey, a conversation. The point is not any single
interview. It is that the team never goes more than a few days without hearing a real
person describe their life, which keeps the roadmap honest in a way no document can.

Weekly contact only works if it is cheap. That means automating recruitment (a prompt in
the product, a standing invitation to customers, a rota with customer success), keeping
interviews short and story-based, and sharing notes in a form that takes five minutes to
read. Lesson 3.2.2 covered the interview itself; this is about making it routine.

## The product trio

Discovery is done by the product manager, the designer and the engineering lead together,
not by the PM alone reporting back. Each sees something the others miss: the designer sees
the interaction, the engineer sees what is possible and what it costs, the PM sees the
business and the customer. When all three hear the same interview, the argument about what
it meant happens in the room instead of a week later over a document.

If your team does not have a designer, or the engineering lead cannot spare the time, the
trio is still the goal. Invite them to one interview a month and watch what changes.

## Outcomes over outputs

Most roadmaps list outputs: features, launches, projects. Continuous discovery insists on
starting from an outcome, a measurable change in customer behaviour that the business
cares about. "Increase the share of retailers who record a sale on the same day" is an
outcome. "Build a quick-entry screen" is an output that might or might not move it.

Torres draws a line that matters for a PM's accountability. A **business outcome** is what
the company measures: revenue, retention, cost. A **product outcome** is the customer
behaviour the team can actually influence and that is believed to drive the business
outcome. Teams are set product outcomes, not business ones, because nobody can be
accountable for a number they cannot move. If you are being measured on revenue directly,
Course 2's lesson on owning what you do not control applies, and the fix is to negotiate a
product outcome you can own.

## The opportunity solution tree

The tree is the map that keeps all of this straight. At the top, the outcome. Beneath it,
the opportunities: customer needs, pains and desires, in their words, drawn from the weekly
interviews and grouped so that the structure of the problem space is visible. Beneath a
chosen opportunity, several candidate solutions. Beneath each solution, the assumption tests
that would tell you whether to build it.

Three things the tree does that a backlog cannot:

- **It shows the shape of what you know.** A lopsided tree is a visible warning that you
  have been hearing from one kind of customer.
- **It makes the choice explicit.** Picking a target opportunity is a decision with
  reasons, written down, that the team can revisit when the evidence changes.
- **It compares solutions instead of defending one.** With three solutions under an
  opportunity, the question becomes "which is best?" rather than "is mine good enough?".

The tree is never finished. New interviews add opportunities, tests prune solutions, and
the target opportunity moves as the outcome moves. Project 3.3.4 has you build your first.

## Assumption testing

Every solution rests on assumptions: that customers want it, that they will find it, that
it can be built, that it will move the outcome. Continuous discovery asks you to name the
riskiest one and test it in days, not months, before anyone writes production code.

The tests are deliberately small. A fake door that measures clicks on a feature that does
not exist. A concierge version where you do the job by hand for five customers. A one-day
prototype in front of three people. Storyboards walked through with a customer. The
question is never "do they like it?" but "did the behaviour we assumed actually happen?"

Most tests fail. That is the point. A failed assumption test costs a few days and saves a
quarter of engineering. Course 7 applies the same idea to AI features, where the
assumptions are even riskier.

## Making it stick

Continuous discovery dies for boring reasons: the recruiting pipeline runs dry, delivery
pressure eats the interview slot, the tree lives in a file nobody opens. The teams that
keep it going treat the weekly interview as fixed as the sprint review, keep the tree on a
wall or a pinned board, and share one interview insight in every team update. Lesson 2.4.2
on protecting attention applies to the whole team, not just you.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Teresa Torres's
   [Product Discovery Basics](https://www.producttalk.org/continuous-discovery/), twenty
   minutes. The overview of the whole practice from its originator, including the weekly
   touchpoint and why it is the minimum.
2. Read her original post on the
   [opportunity solution tree](https://www.producttalk.org/2016/08/opportunity-solution-tree/),
   fifteen minutes. Read it before the project so you have seen a real one drawn.
3. Read [Empower Product Teams with Product Outcomes, Not Business Outcomes](https://www.producttalk.org/2021/02/product-outcomes/),
   ten minutes. Then write down, in one line each, the business outcome your product idea
   serves and the product outcome your team could own. If they are the same line, try again.
4. Read [Core Concept: The Product Trio](https://www.producttalk.org/2021/03/product-trio/),
   ten minutes, and decide who the three people would be for your product, real or
   hypothetical. If you are learning solo, name the two people you would most want to hear
   your next interview.
5. Set up your weekly touchpoint. One recurring thirty-minute slot in your calendar, one
   standing message you can send to recruit, one template for notes. Run the first one
   before the project.

</div>
