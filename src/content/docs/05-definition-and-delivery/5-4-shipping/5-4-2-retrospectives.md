## Introduction

A retrospective is the team's regular, structured look back at how it has been working:
what went well, what did not, and what to change. Done well, it is where a team gets
better. Done badly, it is forty minutes of polite complaint followed by nothing, and after
three of those people stop coming.

The PM rarely owns the retrospective formally. In Scrum teams the scrum master or
engineering manager runs it. But you are the person most able to make it useful, because
you see the whole loop from discovery to launch, you have the most to learn from it, and
you are usually the subject of some of the feedback. This lesson is about making the hour
count, whoever holds the marker.

## What it is for

A retrospective has one output: a short list of changes the team commits to making before
the next one, each with an owner. Everything else, the sticky notes, the dot voting, the
discussion, exists to produce that list. If a retrospective ends without it, nothing has
happened.

What it is not: a status meeting, a performance review, a place to relitigate a decision
that was made, or a venting session. Venting will happen and some of it is healthy, but it
is the raw material, not the product.

Retrospectives also come in two sizes. The sprint retrospective, every one or two weeks,
looks at how the team worked. The project or launch retrospective, after something big
ships, looks at what the product did and what the process cost. Lesson 5.4.3 covers the
second kind in more depth under postmortems. Both use the same mechanics.

## Three formats

Every format is a way of prompting people to say things they would not say to an open
question. Pick one, rotate occasionally, and do not let the format become the point.

**Start, Stop, Continue.** Three columns. What should we start doing, stop doing, keep
doing. The default format and the most action-oriented, because every note is already a
proposal. Its weakness is that it skips feelings, so teams under strain produce tidy lists
that miss the real problem.

**Mad, Sad, Glad.** Three columns by emotion. What made you angry, what disappointed you,
what pleased you. Better for surfacing the thing nobody wants to say, because framing it as
a feeling lowers the stakes of saying it. Its weakness is the opposite: it produces
feelings and needs a second step to turn them into actions.

**The Four L's.** Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed for. The broadest format. "Learned" pulls
out knowledge the team gained that should be written down; "Longed for" surfaces things
people have stopped asking for because they assume the answer is no. Good for longer
cycles and launch retrospectives.

Whichever you use, the mechanics are the same: silent writing first, so loud voices do not
set the agenda; grouping; voting on what to discuss; discussion time-boxed; actions agreed.

## The shape of a good one

Sixty minutes, roughly:

1. **Set the stage, five minutes.** Read the prime directive (below). State the scope: this
   sprint, or this launch. Check in with one word each if the team likes that.
2. **Gather data, ten minutes.** Silent writing on stickies or a shared board, one thought
   per note, into the format's columns. Silence matters. It is the only part of the meeting
   where the quiet engineer and the loud PM contribute equally.
3. **Generate insight, fifteen minutes.** Group similar notes. Dot-vote on the groups. Take
   the top two or three. For each, ask why until you reach something the team can change,
   not something the weather did.
4. **Decide, fifteen minutes.** For each chosen group, one action, one owner, one date.
   Small enough to be done before the next retrospective. "Communicate better" is not an
   action. "PM posts the sprint goal in the channel on Monday morning" is.
5. **Close, five minutes.** Read the actions back. Check last retrospective's actions were
   done. If they were not, that is the first topic next time.

Write the actions where the team will see them, usually at the top of the next sprint's
board. A retrospective whose actions live in a document nobody opens has produced nothing.

## The prime directive

Norm Kerth's prime directive, which many teams read aloud at the start, says in effect:
whatever we find, we believe everyone did the best they could with what they knew and had
at the time. It sounds soft. It is the opposite. It is the condition under which people will
tell the truth about what went wrong, because the truth is no longer an accusation.

If your team cannot say hard things in a retrospective, no format will help, and the work
is on safety first. Course 2 covered how feedback lands and why. As the PM, you set a lot
of the tone: if you can say "I scoped that badly and it cost us a week" in the room, others
can say their version.

## Where they fail

- **No actions.** The most common. Fixed by refusing to end without them.
- **Same actions every time.** The team keeps agreeing to the same change and not making
  it. The change is too big, or nobody owns it, or something upstream blocks it. Escalate
  the block instead of re-agreeing the action.
- **Only the PM talks.** Silent writing and dot voting fix most of this. If not, let someone
  else facilitate and you sit in the corner with your own stickies.
- **Manager in the room.** Depends on the manager. If people edit themselves, run one
  without them and compare the notes.
- **Retrospective fatigue.** Every two weeks for two years gets stale. Rotate formats, skip
  one when the sprint was uneventful, run a longer one at the end of a quarter.
