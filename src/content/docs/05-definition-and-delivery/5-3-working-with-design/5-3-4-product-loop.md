## Introduction

Most products that last have a loop: something brings the user in, they do something, they
get something for it, and that something makes them more likely to come back or to bring
someone else. Products without a loop have to buy every visit. Drawing the loop is one of
the most useful things a PM can do with a whiteboard, because it shows where the product's
engine actually is, and where the goals sheet from Course 4 may have been pointing at the
wrong part.

This project asks you to draw your product's core loop, be honest about where it is weak,
and go back and revise your goals in light of it. It also gives you a design artifact you
will use when briefing designers in the rest of this course: the loop is the thing every
screen should serve.

## Before you start

Two kinds of loop are worth knowing. An **engagement loop** describes one user's repeated
use: a trigger, an action, a reward and an investment that makes the next trigger more
likely. A **growth loop** describes how use by one user creates more users: content they
make is found by others, invitations they send bring people in, value they get leads them
to recommend it. Most products have one of each. Pick the one that matters more for your
product at its current stage, or draw both if they are short.

You will need your goals sheet and roadmap from project 4.2.5, and your audience map from
project 3.1.4. Read the two short pieces in the assignment first; they take twenty minutes
and will save you an hour of drawing the wrong thing.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Nir Eyal's summary of the Hook model,
   [How to manufacture desire](https://www.nirandfar.com/how-to-manufacture-desire/), ten
   minutes, for the engagement loop: trigger, action, variable reward, investment. Read it
   critically; the same mechanics that build habits build compulsions, and Course 2's
   ethics lesson applies.
2. Read Reforge's [growth loops](https://www.reforge.com/blog/growth-loops) piece, ten
   minutes, for why loops beat funnels as a way to think about growth, and the main kinds
   of loop.
3. Draw your product's core loop. On paper or in a whiteboard tool, as a cycle of four to
   six steps with arrows. For each step write what the user does, what the product does,
   and what the user gets. Mark the step you think is weakest today, and the step where a
   user is most likely to drop out of the loop. Export it as an image.
4. Write half a page answering: which step in the loop does each of your current goals
   from 4.2.5 actually move? Is there a step that none of them touches? Is there a goal that
   does not touch the loop at all?
5. Revise your goals sheet. Change, add or remove goals so that every goal moves a step in
   the loop, and the weakest step has a goal pointed at it. Keep the old version alongside
   and note what changed and why.

#### Extra credit

- Draw the loop for a competitor from your analysis in lesson 3.3.2. Where is theirs
  stronger than yours? Where is it weaker?
- Sketch what one screen would need to show to strengthen the weakest step. One screen,
  as a wireframe, no visual design. Use it as the brief for lesson 5.1.3.
