## Introduction

Ask a product team what their strategy is and you will usually get one of three things: a
sentence about changing the world, a list of the features shipping this quarter, or silence.
The sentence is a vision. The list is a plan. Neither is a strategy, and the silence is the
honest answer most of the time.

This lesson separates the three words, explains what each is for, and introduces the
narrative: a short written argument that connects what you believe about the world to what
you are going to build. The narrative is the most important strategic artifact a product
manager produces. It is also the one most PMs have never written, because nobody asked them
to and the roadmap seemed to be enough. It is not enough. By the end of this section you
will have written one.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why a roadmap is not a strategy, and what goes wrong when it is treated as one.
- The narrative document: what it contains and why writing it is the work.
- The working-backwards press release as one narrative form.
- How a narrative changes what the team does on Monday.

## Three words, three questions

**Vision** answers "what does the world look like if we succeed?" It is a picture of a
future state, five to ten years out, in terms of the people you serve rather than the
product you ship. It should be ambitious enough to be worth years of work and concrete
enough that you could tell whether you were getting closer. A vision rarely changes.

**Strategy** answers "how are we going to get there, given what is true about us and the
market?" It is a set of choices: which customers first, which problems first, what we will
be better at than anyone else, and therefore what we will not do. A strategy lasts a year or
two and changes when the facts change.

**Plan** answers "what are we doing next, in what order?" Roadmaps, quarterly goals,
sprints. A plan changes constantly and should.

The mistake most teams make is to have a vision and a plan and nothing in between. Without
a strategy, the plan is a list of things people asked for, and the vision is a poster. The
PM's job is the middle layer.

## Why a roadmap is not a strategy

A roadmap tells you what, not why. Two teams can have identical roadmaps and completely
different strategies, or the same strategy and different roadmaps. When the roadmap is the
only strategic document, three things happen:

1. **Every request looks equally valid**, because there is no stated reason to prefer one
   over another. Prioritisation becomes politics.
2. **The team cannot adapt.** When a feature ships and does not work, nobody knows which
   belief was wrong, because the belief was never written down.
3. **Leadership and the team drift apart.** Executives think in terms of bets and
   outcomes; the roadmap speaks in features and dates. They stop understanding each other.

Marty Cagan has written for years about product strategy as the missing layer in most
companies, and his overview is the first assignment. Read it for the distinction between a
strategy and a list of projects.

## The narrative

A narrative is a written argument, two to six pages, that says:

- **What we believe about the people we serve.** Who they are, what they are trying to do,
  what is in their way, with evidence from Course 3.
- **What we believe about the market.** What has changed that makes this possible now,
  who else is trying, where they are weak.
- **The bet.** Given those beliefs, the specific thing we will do, for whom, first. The
  wedge, which the next lesson covers.
- **Why us.** The advantage we have or will build that makes this hard to copy.
- **What we will not do**, at least for now, and why.
- **What success looks like** in a year, in numbers where possible.
- **What would make us wrong.** The assumptions the bet rests on and how we would know
  if one failed.

Prose, not slides. Slides let you skip the connective tissue between claims. Prose does
not. Amazon famously banned slide decks in favour of six-page memos for exactly this reason,
and the first assignment in lesson 2.3.1 covered the writing side. This lesson is about the
content.

The narrative is the document you reread before every prioritisation argument, hand to a
new engineer in their first week, and update when a belief changes. If it is accurate, most
roadmap debates resolve themselves by reading it. If it is out of date, that is the first
thing to fix.

## The press release as narrative

Amazon's working-backwards process starts a product by writing the press release and FAQ
that would announce it, before anything is built. The press release names the customer,
the problem, the solution in one sentence, and a quote from a customer about how their
life changed. The FAQ holds the hard questions: how much will it cost, what will it not do,
why would anyone switch.

It is a narrative in disguise. Writing it forces the same questions in a format everyone
can read in five minutes, and it has one advantage over the long form: if the press release
is boring, the product probably is too. Many teams write both, the press release as the
summary and the narrative as the argument underneath it.

## What changes on Monday

A good narrative is visible in the team's behaviour within weeks:

- Feature requests get answered with "that is not where we are betting right now, and
  here is why" instead of "we'll add it to the backlog".
- The roadmap gets shorter, because things that do not serve the bet drop off.
- Engineers propose ideas that fit, because they finally know what fits.
- Metrics reviews ask "is the belief holding?" instead of "did the number go up?"

If none of that happens, the narrative is either wrong or unread. Both are fixable, and
both are the PM's problem.
