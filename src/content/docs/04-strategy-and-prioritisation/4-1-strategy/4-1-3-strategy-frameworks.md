## Introduction

There are hundreds of strategy frameworks and three that are worth a product manager's
time. This lesson covers them: Richard Rumelt's kernel of a good strategy, Roger Martin
and A.G. Lafley's five choices, and Michael Porter's definition of strategy as the
activities you choose not to do. Each is a different lens on the same question your
narrative has to answer.

The lesson ends with why frameworks fail, which they do, constantly, in the hands of smart
people. Knowing the failure modes matters more than knowing the frameworks.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Rumelt: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action, and the four marks of bad strategy.
- Lafley and Martin: the five choices and why "where to play" and "how to win" come
  together.
- Porter: strategy as trade-offs and fit, and the difference from operational
  effectiveness.
- Which lens to use when.
- Why frameworks fail: fluff, consensus, and the plan that is not a strategy.

## Rumelt: the kernel

Richard Rumelt's *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* gives strategy a structure he calls the
kernel, three parts that every real strategy has:

1. **A diagnosis.** A clear statement of what is actually going on: the one or two
   critical obstacles or opportunities, named plainly. Not everything that is true, just
   what matters.
2. **A guiding policy.** The overall approach for dealing with the diagnosis. Not a goal,
   not a vision, an approach. "We will win the small-business segment by being the easiest
   to set up" is a guiding policy. "Grow revenue 30 percent" is not.
3. **Coherent actions.** A set of actions that follow from the policy and reinforce each
   other. Coherent means they fit together; most strategies fail here, with a list of
   initiatives that each make sense alone and compete for the same people.

Rumelt also names what bad strategy looks like, and his list is a checklist for your own
narrative: **fluff** (fancy words restating the obvious), **failure to face the problem**
(no diagnosis), **mistaking goals for strategy** (targets with no approach), and **bad
strategic objectives** (a long list of things to do, none of them a choice).

If your narrative from lesson 4.1.1 has a diagnosis, a policy and three to five actions
that reinforce each other, it passes Rumelt. If it has a vision and fourteen initiatives,
it does not.

## Lafley and Martin: five choices

A.G. Lafley ran Procter and Gamble; Roger Martin advised him. Their book *Playing to Win*
reduces strategy to five questions, answered in order and revised together:

1. **What is our winning aspiration?** Not "be the best", but what winning means for
   whom.
2. **Where will we play?** Which customers, geographies, channels, product categories.
   The wedge from the last lesson is a where-to-play choice.
3. **How will we win?** The advantage that makes customers choose you in that arena.
   Lower cost or differentiation, and the specific form of it.
4. **What capabilities must we have?** The things the organisation has to be genuinely
   good at for the how-to-win to be real.
5. **What management systems are needed?** The measures, processes and structures that
   keep the capabilities alive.

The framework's single most useful idea is that questions two and three are one decision.
Where you play only makes sense given how you intend to win there, and vice versa. Teams
that pick a market first and then ask how to win in it usually find they have picked a
market where they cannot.

For product work, the five questions are a good interview with yourself before writing a
narrative. They are also a good structure for a strategy review with leadership, because
executives recognise them.

## Porter: strategy is what you will not do

Michael Porter's 1996 essay "What Is Strategy?" is the oldest of the three and the most
quoted. Its central claim: operational effectiveness, doing the same things better than
rivals, is necessary but not strategy, because rivals copy it. Strategy is choosing a
different set of activities that deliver a unique mix of value, and the choices only hold
if they involve **trade-offs**, things you deliberately do worse or not at all, and
**fit**, activities that reinforce each other so the whole is hard to copy even when each
part is not.

For a PM, Porter's test is the simplest: what does your strategy say no to? If the answer
is nothing, you have an aspiration, not a strategy. And does each thing you are doing make
the other things more valuable? If the features on your roadmap would be just as useful in
a competitor's product, there is no fit.

## Which lens when

- **Writing a narrative from scratch:** Rumelt. Diagnosis, policy, actions is the
  skeleton of the document.
- **Checking a strategy for holes:** Rumelt's four marks of bad strategy. Read your own
  narrative looking for fluff and goals disguised as approaches.
- **Deciding where to compete:** Lafley and Martin, questions two and three together.
- **Presenting to leadership:** the five choices, because they are a complete story in
  five sentences.
- **Defending a decision to drop something:** Porter. Trade-offs are the point.

None of these replaces the work in Course 3. A framework organises evidence; it does not
produce any.

## Why frameworks fail

Smart teams run these frameworks and still produce bad strategy. The usual reasons:

**The diagnosis is skipped.** The team starts at "how will we win" because it is the fun
part, and never agrees on what the actual problem is. Every later disagreement is really a
disagreement about the diagnosis, surfaced too late.

**Consensus replaces choice.** A strategy that everyone can live with is one that says no
to nothing. Rumelt calls this the "dog's dinner" of objectives. It happens when the
strategy is written in a workshop and nobody owns it.

**The framework becomes the output.** A filled-in template is not a strategy. If the
document could be handed to a competitor without helping them, it contains no choices.

**A plan is mistaken for a strategy.** Roger Martin's short video on exactly this is in
the assignment. A plan is a list of things you will do and control. A strategy is a theory
about why customers will choose you, which you do not control. Teams prefer plans because
plans feel safe.

**The strategy is right and the actions do not follow.** The narrative says one thing and
the roadmap says another. The next section, on goals and roadmaps, is about closing that
gap.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Watch Roger Martin's [A Plan Is Not a Strategy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iuYlGRnC7J8),
   nine minutes. The sharpest short statement of the most common failure, and a video you
   will want to send to people later.
2. Read Michael Porter's [What Is Strategy?](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy),
   forty minutes. It is long and worth it. Read for trade-offs and fit, and skip the
   extended examples if you are short on time.
3. Listen to Richard Rumelt on
   [Lenny's Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/good-strategy-bad-strategy-richard),
   about ninety minutes, or watch his
   [LSE lecture](https://www.lse.ac.uk/lse-player/good-strategy-bad-strategy-the-difference-and-why-it-matters)
   on the same material, ninety minutes. Either gives you the kernel and the four marks of
   bad strategy in his own words. Then run the four marks against the press release you
   wrote in lesson 4.1.1 and note every place it fails.
4. Pick one company whose product you know well and write its strategy in the five-choice
   format, one or two sentences per question, from the outside. Then write what you think
   its real trade-offs are. Compare the two. Where they differ is where frameworks go wrong
   in practice.
