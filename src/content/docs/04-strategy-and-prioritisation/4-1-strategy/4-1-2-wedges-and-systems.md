## Introduction

Two habits separate PMs who set strategy from PMs who write roadmaps. The first is seeing
the product as a system: a set of loops where one thing causes the next, rather than a
list of features. The second is the discipline to enter a market through a narrow wedge,
one small group with one acute problem, instead of launching to everyone at once.

They are connected. The system diagram shows you where a small push produces a large
effect. The wedge is where you push first. This lesson teaches both, and the section
project asks you to draw the diagram for your own product and name the wedge.

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

## Products are systems

A product is not a list of features. It is a set of causes and effects. A user does
something, which produces something, which brings another user or brings the first user
back. Draw those causes and effects as boxes and arrows and you have a system diagram.

The simplest version for most products is one loop:

> More users create more content → more content attracts more visitors → some visitors
> become users → more users create more content.

That is a **reinforcing loop**: each turn makes the next turn bigger. Marketplaces, social
products, user-generated content and anything with network effects have one at their core.
Growth teams call them growth loops, and the Reforge essay in the assignment is the
clearest account of why loops beat funnels as a way to think about growth.

Most products also have **balancing loops** that push back: more users means more support
load, more content means more noise and lower quality, more sellers means lower prices.
A system diagram with only reinforcing loops is a pitch deck, not a model. Draw the
balancing loops too.

## Finding the leverage point

Once the diagram exists, the strategic question becomes visible: where in this system does
a small change produce a large result? Donella Meadows, whose essay on leverage points is
in the assignment, ranked the places to intervene in any system. The least powerful is
adjusting a number, such as a price or a limit. The most powerful is changing what the
system is for. In product terms:

- Changing a number: a discount, a notification frequency, a character limit.
- Changing a flow: making it easier for one step of the loop to complete.
- Adding a new loop: a referral mechanism, a creator payout.
- Changing the goal: deciding the product is for a different job entirely.

Most roadmaps are full of the first kind. Most strategies that work involve the second or
third. Your narrative from lesson 4.1.1 should say which loop you are strengthening and
how.

## Everyone is no one

The commonest strategic mistake in a new product is to launch for the whole market at
once. It feels ambitious. It is actually the opposite, because a product built for
everyone has no reason for any particular person to switch to it today.

The alternative is a wedge: one group of people with one acute problem, small enough that
you can reach them directly and understand them completely, and important enough to them
that a rough product is still worth adopting. You win that group entirely, learn from
them, and expand from a position of strength.

Paul Graham's essay in the assignment is the canonical account of this for startups, and
it applies to any new product inside a company too. The first users were recruited by
hand; the product was shaped around them; growth came later. Fred Wilson's post on
bootstrapping a network with a high-value niche use case shows the same pattern in
consumer products, where the first use case is often narrow to the point of embarrassment
and that is exactly why it works.

## Picking the wedge

A good wedge passes four tests:

1. **The pain is acute.** People are already working around the problem with something
   worse: spreadsheets, group chats, a person on the phone. If nobody is improvising a
   solution, the pain is not acute.
2. **The group is reachable.** You can name where they gather and talk to twenty of them
   this month. Your audience map from project 3.1.4 should have this.
3. **The group is small enough to dominate.** You can become the obvious answer for
   them, not one option among many.
4. **It expands.** Winning this group leads somewhere: adjacent groups, adjacent
   problems, or a network that makes the product better for the next group.

The fourth test is where ambition belongs. The wedge is narrow; the path from it is not.

## Common wedge shapes

- **Tool first, network later.** A single-player tool that is useful alone, which becomes
  a network once enough people use it. Chris Dixon's "come for the tool, stay for the
  network" names the pattern and is in the assignment.
- **One workflow inside a bigger job.** The expense report, not the accounting system.
  The invoice, not the business bank.
- **One underserved segment of a crowded market.** The same product category, for people
  the incumbents treat as an afterthought: a region, a language, a company size, a
  device.
- **One extreme user.** The power user whose needs are so demanding that serving them
  well makes the product better for everyone.
- **A toy.** Something that looks too small to matter to incumbents until it is not.
  Dixon's "the next big thing will start out looking like a toy" is the second piece of
  his in the resources.

For the product you are carrying through this course, one of these shapes almost certainly
fits. The project asks you to say which.

   minutes, if you did not in Course 1, and Fred Wilson's
   [Bootstrap Your Network With A High Value Niche Use Case](https://avc.com/2015/07/bootstrap-your-network-with-a-high-value-niche-use-case/),

   five minutes. Then draw, on paper or in Excalidraw, the main loop of a product you use
   every day: at least one reinforcing loop and one balancing loop, with the arrows
   labelled. Twenty minutes. This is practice for the project.
