## Introduction

Goals, roadmaps and prioritisation are the content of planning. This lesson is about the
process: the meetings, documents and decisions that turn them into something the team
actually does. Done badly, planning eats the last three weeks of every quarter and produces
a slide deck nobody reads. Done well, it takes a few days, involves the right people at the
right moments, and ends with a team that knows what it is doing and why.

There is no single right process. This lesson gives you three shapes that work, a way to
make decisions inside them, and the signs that your planning has become a ritual in the
bad sense.

## What planning is for

Planning exists to answer three questions for a bounded period: what are we trying to
change, what will we work on to change it, and what are we explicitly not doing. The output
should fit on a page or two per team. If it needs a deck, the plan is probably hiding
disagreements rather than resolving them.

A good planning process has three properties. It is bounded in time, so the team spends
most of the period doing rather than planning. It involves the people who will do the work
in shaping the work. And it ends with decisions, not a list of things to discuss further.

## Quarterly planning

The default in most companies. Every three months, leadership sets or restates goals,
teams propose what they will work on, there is some negotiation, and a plan is agreed.

The common shape:

1. **Context from leadership**, a week or two before. What changed, what the company's
   goals are for the quarter, any constraints.
2. **Team proposals.** Each team drafts its goals, roadmap and asks (headcount,
   dependencies) using the material from this section.
3. **Review and negotiation.** Leadership reads the proposals, pushes back, resolves
   conflicts between teams, and signs off.
4. **Commitment.** The plan is published and the quarter starts.

Where it fails: step one arrives late or not at all, so teams plan in a vacuum. Step three
becomes a series of hour-long review meetings with every team, which is where the three
weeks go. Dependencies between teams are discovered in step four. And the whole thing
happens so close to the quarter's start that the first two weeks are spent finishing the
plan.

The fixes are mostly timing: start earlier, do reviews in writing before meeting, and
handle cross-team dependencies in one session rather than in each team's review.

## W planning

W planning is a shape for the back-and-forth that keeps it to two passes. The name comes
from the path the information takes when you draw it: down from leadership, up from teams,
down again, up again, like a W.

1. **Leadership sends context down.** Goals, constraints, the handful of things that must
   happen. Short.
2. **Teams send plans up.** Each team proposes what it will do against that context,
   including what it will not do and what it needs from others.
3. **Leadership integrates and sends down again.** Resolves conflicts, reallocates where
   the proposals do not add up, sends back a draft of the whole.
4. **Teams confirm or flag.** A final pass to catch what the integration broke, then
   commit.

The discipline is that each pass is written and the whole thing takes about two weeks.
There is no third pass; whatever is still unresolved gets decided by leadership and the
teams move on. W planning gives teams real input without the open-ended negotiation that
makes quarterly planning slow.

## Cycles and bets

Some companies have abandoned the quarter altogether in favour of shorter, fixed cycles.
Basecamp's Shape Up is the best-documented version: six-week cycles, each preceded by a
betting table where leadership picks which shaped pieces of work to fund, followed by a
two-week cool-down. The team gets a fixed appetite (the time it is worth spending) rather
than an estimate, and the work is shaped enough to be buildable but not specified in
detail.

The attraction is that planning happens in small, regular doses, the commitment is short,
and unfinished work is not automatically carried over, which forces honest scoping. The
cost is that it works best for teams that control their own roadmap; it is harder when
sales commitments and external deadlines drive the work.

You do not need to adopt the whole system. The ideas of appetite over estimate and of
not carrying work over by default are useful in any planning shape.

## DACI: who decides

Most planning time is lost not to planning but to unclear decision rights. A proposal goes
round in circles because nobody knows who can say yes.

DACI, from Atlassian's team playbook, names four roles for any decision:

- **Driver.** The one person running the decision process: gathering input, setting the
  deadline, writing it up. Usually the PM.
- **Approver.** The one person who makes the call. One, not a committee.
- **Contributors.** People whose input is needed before the decision. They are consulted,
  not asked for consent.
- **Informed.** People who need to know the outcome and nothing more.

Writing the DACI down at the start of a planning cycle, for the handful of decisions that
matter, removes most of the meetings. The usual surprise is discovering that two people
both thought they were the approver, which is better discovered in week one than week
eleven.

## Signs your planning needs fixing

- Planning takes more than two weeks out of a twelve-week quarter.
- The plan is a deck rather than a document.
- Dependencies between teams surface after the plan is agreed.
- Teams do not know what they are explicitly not doing.
- Nobody reads the plan again after week two.
- The same disagreements come up every cycle because they were never decided.

Each of these has a fix in the material above. The last one is almost always a missing
DACI.
