## Introduction

Every company with more than a handful of product managers has a ladder: a list of levels
with a title and a paragraph of expectations attached to each. The ladders look similar
from the outside and are wildly different inside. A senior PM at a thirty-person startup and
a senior PM at a company of fifty thousand share a title and almost nothing else.

This lesson gives you the typical ladder, what actually changes as you climb it, the fork
between staying an individual contributor and becoming a manager, and how to read a
specific company's ladder against the generic one. It will not give you salary numbers. They
move every year and differ by country more than by level, and the lesson would be wrong
within months. For current figures, use the source in the assignment.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The typical ladder from associate PM to chief product officer.
- The three things that actually change with level: scope, ambiguity and influence.
- The individual-contributor track and the manager track, and why the fork is reversible.
- How to decode a specific company's ladder.
- What gets people promoted and what does not.

## The typical ladder

Titles vary, but most ladders are a version of this:

| Level | Typical title | What you own |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Associate PM, Junior PM, PM I | A feature or a slice of a product, with close guidance |
| Core | Product Manager, PM II | A product area and its outcomes, with a team |
| Senior | Senior PM | A larger or riskier area, plus influence beyond the team |
| Staff | Group PM, Principal PM, Lead PM | Several areas, either through other PMs or through hard problems |
| Leadership | Director of Product | A portfolio and the PMs who run it |
| Executive | VP Product, Head of Product | The product organisation and its strategy |
| Top | Chief Product Officer | Product as a function of the company, alongside the other chiefs |

Two things to notice. First, the middle rows are where most careers live. "Senior PM" is a
destination, not a waypoint, for a great many excellent people. Second, the ladder forks
around the staff level, which the next section covers.

## What actually changes

Reading ladder documents, you would think promotion is about doing the same things better.
It is not. Three things change in kind, not degree.

**Scope.** An associate owns a feature. A PM owns an area. A senior PM owns an area plus the
things around it that nobody asked them to own. A director owns a portfolio they mostly do
not touch directly. At each step the unit of work gets bigger and your hands get further
from it.

**Ambiguity.** At entry level, someone hands you a problem. At the core level, someone hands
you a goal and you find the problems. At senior and above, you are expected to notice that
the goal itself is wrong and say so. By the leadership levels, most of the job is deciding
what the questions are.

**Influence.** An associate influences their team. A senior PM influences adjacent teams and
the people one level up. A director influences the company's direction. The tools from
Course 2, writing, saying no, surviving conflict, are the same at every level. What changes
is who is in the room and how much is riding on whether they believe you.

If you want a one-line test for whether you are operating at the next level: are you
regularly solving problems that nobody assigned you, at a scope wider than your team, in a
way other people adopt? That is the behaviour promotions follow.

## The fork: individual contributor or manager

Around the senior-to-staff boundary, the ladder splits.

**The manager track** goes Group PM to Director to VP. You stop owning products directly and
start owning the people who own them. Your output is their output. Your week becomes
one-to-ones, hiring, planning, and representing the function upwards. Many PMs want this
because it is the visible path and because it comes with authority they have lacked for
years. Some find they miss the product and are worse at the people work than they expected.

**The individual-contributor track** goes Principal PM to Distinguished or Fellow at the
companies that have it. You keep your hands on hard problems: a platform migration, a new
line of business, a problem spanning five teams that no single manager can own. You have
influence without reports. Fewer companies have a real version of this track, and at those
that do, it is often harder to get onto than the manager track.

The fork is more reversible than it looks. People move from managing back to building and
back again, and the best leaders have usually done both. Charity Majors calls this the
pendulum, and her essay on it is in the assignment. Do not choose a track at twenty-six and
treat it as a life sentence.

## Reading a specific ladder

Every company's ladder is a fossil of its history, like the job ads in lesson 1.1.2. Ask:

- **How many levels are there?** A startup may have two. A large company may have eight.
  "Senior" at a two-level company is a different job from "Senior" at an eight-level one.
- **Where does strategy appear?** If the word first appears at Director, PMs below that are
  expected to execute someone else's strategy. If it appears at PM, the company expects
  judgement early.
- **Is there a real IC track above Senior?** Ask who is on it. If nobody is, it is
  aspirational.
- **What does the ladder say about people?** Ladders that mention mentoring and influence
  from mid-level up tell you the company values it. Ladders that never mention it tell you
  something too.
- **How does it map to the market?** Public salary and level databases let you line a
  company's level up against others. That is also the only sensible place to get numbers.

## What gets people promoted

Not years served. Not effort. The things that reliably move people up a ladder:

1. **Visible outcomes at the next level's scope.** You have to be doing the next job before
   you get the title. Unfair, and universal.
2. **A manager who argues for you.** Promotions are decided in rooms you are not in. Your
   manager's account of your work is the evidence. Make it easy to tell.
3. **Written record.** Decision journals, retrospectives, memos: the artifacts from Courses
   2 to 7 are also your promotion case.
4. **Peers who vouch.** Engineers and designers are asked. Course 1 covered why they will
   or will not.

What does not: being the loudest, having the most ideas, working the longest hours, or
shipping the most features. Those correlate with burnout more than with promotion.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Marty Cagan's
   [The Product Manager Career Path](https://www.svpg.com/product-management-career-path/),
   ten minutes. A clear statement of the levels and the fork, from someone who has coached
   PMs at every one of them. Note where he says the ladder is about scope of judgement, not
   scope of features.
2. Read Charity Majors' [The Engineer/Manager Pendulum](https://charity.wtf/2017/05/11/the-engineer-manager-pendulum/),
   ten minutes. Written for engineers, and the best argument that the fork is a swing, not a
   one-way door. Everything in it applies to PMs.
3. Browse the UK government's published
   [product manager role description](https://ddat-capability-framework.service.gov.uk/role/product-manager),
   ten minutes. A rare example of a complete, public ladder with skill levels written out
   for every rung. Compare it with the table above and note what it adds.
4. Open [Levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/t/product-manager) and look up product manager
   levels at three companies you might apply to. Note how many levels each has and where
   "Senior" sits. This is where salary numbers live, so look there rather than in this
   lesson. Ten minutes.
5. Write five sentences on where you are on the ladder today, in terms of scope, ambiguity
   and influence rather than title, and which direction you want the next step to be in.
   Keep it for the portfolio project in section 8.2.
