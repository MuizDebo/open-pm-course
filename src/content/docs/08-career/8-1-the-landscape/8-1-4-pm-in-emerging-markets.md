## Introduction

Most product management writing is produced in San Francisco, London and a few other
places, for products used by people with fast phones, cheap data, bank accounts and a
reasonable expectation that a company will do what it says. If you are reading this in
Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, Kampala, Dhaka or Manila, that is not your user and it may not be
your market.

This lesson is for you. It covers the two main career routes, remote and local, honestly.
It covers what makes emerging-market products different and why those differences are a
training ground rather than a handicap. And it covers where the jobs actually are. It does
not quote salaries, which vary by city and move too fast to print.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The remote route: what it offers and what it costs.
- The local route: ecosystems, where the jobs are, and how they differ.
- Five constraints that shape emerging-market products.
- Why those constraints produce excellent product managers.
- How to present emerging-market experience to any employer.

## The remote route

Working for a company based elsewhere while living where you are. The pay is often higher
than local rates, the product and process exposure can be excellent, and the work is real.

The realities:

- **Time zones decide your life.** A European employer means a normal day from West Africa.
  A US employer means your afternoon and evening. An Asian one means your morning. Decide
  what you can sustain before you apply.
- **You are hired on trust you have not built.** Remote first-time PM roles are rare because
  the role runs on trust and trust is slower to build at a distance. Experienced PMs find
  remote roles far more easily than first-timers. If you are starting out, the local route
  is usually the way in, and remote comes later.
- **Payment and contracts need care.** Contractor arrangements, payment platforms, currency
  and tax all need sorting out, and they go wrong more often than the job itself does. Ask
  how the company pays people in your country before you accept.
- **You will be invisible by default.** Promotion follows visibility, and visibility at a
  distance means writing. Course 2's writing lesson is your career strategy here.
- **The job market is global, so is the competition.** You are competing with everyone in
  your time zone band, not everyone in your city.

Remote work is a real route and a good one. It is a poor first step and a strong third one.

## The local route

Every large emerging-market city now has a product ecosystem: fintechs, logistics, health,
education, agritech, e-commerce, and the banks and telecoms that have woken up to product.
The jobs exist, the titles are often loose, and the competition is thinner than for remote
roles.

Where to look:

- **Fintech.** The deepest pool of real product work on the continent, from payments to
  lending to savings. Nigeria and Kenya especially.
- **Banks and telecoms.** Slower, more political, and where the largest user bases live.
  Many are hiring their first real product teams, which means lesson 8.1.2's "first product
  manager" dynamics apply.
- **Logistics, mobility and commerce.** Hard problems with physical constraints, which is
  rare and valuable PM experience.
- **Health, education and agriculture.** Smaller, often donor-funded, high social impact,
  and a route to international organisations later.
- **Startup hubs and talent programmes.** Accelerators and training outfits produce networks
  as much as skills. Their alumni lists are job boards.

Follow the local tech press. The sources in the assignment report funding, launches and
hiring, and reading them weekly tells you which companies are growing before the job ads
appear.

## Five constraints that shape the product

Emerging-market products are different because the conditions are different. The
differences are not deficits; they are design parameters, and a PM who understands them has
skills the San Francisco PM does not.

**Connectivity.** Data is expensive relative to income and coverage is uneven. Every
megabyte your app downloads is a cost the user feels. Offline-first, small payloads,
progressive loading and SMS or USSD fallbacks are not edge cases; they are the product.

**Devices.** Low-end Android phones with limited storage and memory dominate. An app that
works on last year's flagship and crashes on a three-year-old budget phone has failed most
of its users. Google's guidance on building for these devices, linked in the assignment, is
the technical half of this.

**Cash and informality.** Large parts of the economy run on cash, agents and informal
credit. Products that assume a bank account, a card or a formal address exclude most
people. The successful ones meet users where they are: agent networks, mobile money,
pay-as-you-go. The story of mobile money in Kenya, in the additional resources, is the
canonical case.

**Trust.** Users have been burnt by failed companies, unreliable services and fraud. They
expect to be let down. Trust has to be earned in the first session, with transparent
pricing, visible human support, and a product that does exactly what it says. Course 7's
lessons on trust in AI products are the same problem in a different suit.

**Identity and regulation.** Identity systems, data protection laws and sector regulators
vary by country and change often. Lesson 7.5.2 covered the data protection side. The PM
who understands the regulatory environment is worth more than one who does not, in every
market, and especially here.

## Why this makes you better

A PM who has shipped a product that works on a slow phone over a patchy connection, for a
user who pays in cash and trusts nobody, has solved harder problems than most PMs in rich
markets ever face. They have done real discovery, because assumptions imported from
elsewhere fail fast. They have prioritised ruthlessly, because resources are thin. They
have learned to build trust, because nothing else works.

Every one of those is a transferable skill. When you present your experience to any
employer, anywhere, do not apologise for the market. Describe the constraint and what you
did about it. "I grew activation by a third on a product whose users are on two-gigabyte
monthly data plans" is a stronger sentence than most PMs can say.

## Presenting your experience

Three habits that help emerging-market PMs in any job market:

1. **Quantify the constraint along with the result.** Device, connectivity, trust: name
   them. They make your results more impressive, not less.
2. **Translate the domain.** An agent network is a distribution channel. Mobile money is a
   payment rail. USSD is a low-bandwidth interface. Use the words a foreign hiring manager
   knows, then teach them yours.
3. **Publish.** Write about what you learned. The global PM conversation is short of voices
   from your market, and the people who fill that gap get noticed. Lesson 7.7.2's advice on
   building in public applies doubly.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Watch Juliana Rotich's TED talk
   [Meet BRCK, Internet access built for Africa](https://www.ted.com/talks/juliana_rotich_meet_brck_internet_access_built_for_africa),
   ten minutes. A product built from the connectivity constraint outward, by a team that
   lived it. Note how the constraint became the product's reason to exist.
2. Read Google's guide to
   [building for billions](https://developer.android.com/docs/quality-guidelines/build-for-billions),
   twenty minutes. The engineering checklist for low-end devices and poor connectivity. You
   will not implement it, but you need to be able to ask for it and recognise when it has
   been skipped.
3. Spend twenty minutes on [TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/) and
   [Disrupt Africa](https://disrupt-africa.com/), or the equivalent press for your region.
   List five companies that raised money or launched something in the last quarter. Those
   are the companies about to hire.
4. Look up your country's internet penetration on the
   [World Bank's indicator](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/IT.NET.USER.ZS) and skim
   the Bank's [financial inclusion](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/financialinclusion)
   overview, ten minutes. Then write one paragraph on how those two numbers would change the
   audience map you made in project 3.1.4.
5. Write the "constraint plus result" version of one thing you have built or worked on, in
   two sentences, using the pattern in this lesson. Keep it for the portfolio.

## Additional resources

- The Wikipedia article on [M-Pesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-Pesa), the clearest
  case of a product designed from an emerging-market constraint that then became a model
  the rest of the world studied.
- [EFInA](https://efina.org.ng/) publishes financial inclusion research for Nigeria that is
  far more useful for sizing and understanding users than global reports.
- [Stears](https://www.stears.co/) for data-driven analysis of African economies and
  sectors, useful when a strategy document needs a number that is not from a Silicon Valley
  deck.
- Engineering and product blogs of local companies, such as
  [Flutterwave's](https://flutterwave.com/ng/blog) and [Andela's](https://www.andela.com/blog),
  show how teams here actually solve these constraints.
