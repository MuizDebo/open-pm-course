## Introduction

Lesson 1.1.3 introduced company stage as one of the three axes that shape a PM's week.
This lesson goes deeper, because when you are choosing where to apply, the type of company
determines more about your daily life than the title, the product or the pay. The same
person can thrive at one type and quietly fail at another, with no change in ability.

Six types are covered, each with what the job really is there, who does well, and the
questions that reveal which type you are talking to, since companies rarely describe
themselves accurately.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Six company types and the PM job at each.
- Who tends to thrive and struggle at each.
- The questions to ask in an interview that expose the real type.
- Why the type matters more than the brand.

## Early-stage startup

Under about thirty people, often with no PM title at all. If there is one, the founder is
usually the real product manager and you are their extension.

**The job.** Everything. Talking to the first customers, writing the onboarding email, doing
QA at midnight, deciding what the next two weeks look like. Discovery and delivery are the
same conversation. There is no process because there has been no time to build one.

**Who thrives.** People who want to learn fast and broadly, who can tolerate chaos, and who
do not need their work to be recognised by anyone but the team. People who have some
runway, because startups fail more often than they succeed, and the HBR piece in the
assignment explains why.

**Who struggles.** Anyone who needs structure, mentorship, or a clear scope. Anyone who
needs the job to be what it was described as, because it will not be.

## Scale-up

Thirty to a few hundred people, growing fast, past product-market fit or close to it.

**The job.** Teams exist, each with a PM, and process is being invented as you go. You own
an area with real engineers and a designer. Your week is the four kinds of work from lesson
1.1.1 in rotation. Decisions are fast and mistakes are visible. This is where most PMs do
their best learning, because there is enough structure to do the job and not so much that
the structure is the job.

**Who thrives.** People who like building things and building the way things get built at
the same time. People comfortable with their area changing shape every quarter.

**Who struggles.** People who want things decided. Scale-ups are permanently half-decided.

## Big tech

Thousands of people, a known brand, deep pockets, mature process.

**The job.** You own a slice of a slice. The decisions are narrower and the stakes are
wider; a small change can affect millions of users. Much of the work is alignment: twelve
people have to agree before anything ships. Writing is the main instrument, data is
everywhere, and the craft is deep. Career ladders are explicit and promotion is a process.

**Who thrives.** People who like mastery over breadth, who can be patient, who write well,
and who get satisfaction from scale rather than from speed.

**Who struggles.** People who need to see the whole product, who are frustrated by meetings
about meetings, or who measure their worth in things shipped per month.

## Agency and consultancy

You build products for clients rather than for your own company.

**The job.** Several products at once, each with a client who is the real decision-maker.
You learn discovery and definition fast across many domains. You rarely see a product
through to the measurement stage, because the engagement ends at launch. Scope and budget
are fixed by contract, which changes how you say no.

**Who thrives.** People who love variety, who are good with clients, and who want a broad
portfolio quickly. A strong route into PM from design or project management.

**Who struggles.** People who want to own an outcome over years. The agency PM hands the baby
over at the door.

## Enterprise and non-tech companies

Banks, telecoms, retailers, manufacturers, governments: large organisations where software
is important but is not the company.

**The job.** Product management is often new here, and part of your work is explaining what
it is. Stakeholders outnumber users in your calendar. Compliance, legacy systems and
procurement shape what is possible. The upside is impact: a small improvement to a bank app
touches millions of people, and these organisations are often where the real problems of a
country live.

**Who thrives.** Patient people with strong stakeholder skills, who can translate between
business and engineering and who are motivated by the problem more than by the tech.

**Who struggles.** People who want modern tooling and fast iteration, or who are drained by
politics.

## The questions that reveal the type

Companies describe themselves aspirationally. The HBR and SVPG material in Course 1 taught
you to decode job ads; these questions decode interviews.

- **"Walk me through how the last significant feature got decided."** The answer tells you
  who really decides and whether discovery exists.
- **"How often does the PM talk to customers?"** Weekly means product team. Quarterly means
  something else.
- **"What does the roadmap look like, and who owns it?"** Features with dates owned by sales
  or the CEO is a feature team. Problems with outcomes owned by the PM is a product team.
  Marty Cagan's distinction from lesson 1.1.2 applies here.
- **"How many PMs are there, and how long has the longest-tenured one been here?"** Turnover
  tells you more than any culture statement.
- **"What happened the last time a PM pushed back on leadership?"** Silence is an answer.
- **"How is a PM's performance evaluated?"** Outcomes, output, or vibes.

Ask at least three of these in every process. The answers are worth more than the offer
letter.

## The type matters more than the brand

A famous company that runs feature teams will teach you less than an unknown scale-up that
runs real product teams. A big-tech job that looks like a prize can be two years of writing
documents nobody reads. A bank job that looks dull can be the most consequential product
work in your city.

Choose the type that matches the week you want to live, as lesson 1.1.3 said, and then
choose the company within that type. People who do it the other way round spend their
first year surprised.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Marty Cagan's [Product vs. Feature Teams](https://www.svpg.com/product-vs-feature-teams/)
   again, fifteen minutes, this time with the interview questions above in hand. For each
   question, write down what a product-team answer and a feature-team answer would sound
   like.
2. Read Gergely Orosz on the
   [trimodal nature of tech companies](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/trimodal),
   fifteen minutes. It is about engineering pay, but the three tiers it describes are a
   sharper model of company type than any org-chart description, and they apply to PMs.
3. Read Tom Eisenmann's [Why Start-ups Fail](https://hbr.org/2021/05/why-start-ups-fail) in
   HBR, twenty minutes, before you take an early-stage job. Not to put you off, but so you
   know what you are betting on and can ask founders the right questions.
4. Pick two companies of different types that you would consider working for. For each,
   write the three interview questions you would ask and what answer would make you walk
   away. Keep them; section 8.3 uses them.

</div>

## Additional resources

- Paul Graham's [Startup = Growth](http://paulgraham.com/growth.html), for what makes a
  startup different from a small business and why that shapes everything about working at
  one.
- [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/) publishes long interviews with
  operators at startups and scale-ups, and is the best free source on what those jobs are
  really like week to week.
- Marty Cagan's [The First Product Manager](https://www.svpg.com/the-first-product-manager/),
  on what the role is when a company hires its first one, which is a type of its own.
