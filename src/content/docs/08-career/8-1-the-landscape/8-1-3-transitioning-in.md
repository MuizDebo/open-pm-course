## Introduction

Almost nobody starts as a product manager. The role has no degree, few entry-level
positions, and a job description that asks for experience doing the job. People get in
sideways, from a neighbouring role, and the route they take shapes what kind of PM they
become.

This lesson covers the common starting points, what each gives you and what each leaves
out, and the three routes that actually work: the internal transfer, the external hire, and
the associate programme. If you are already a PM, read it anyway. Knowing where your
colleagues came from explains a great deal about how they work.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Seven starting points and what each brings and lacks.
- The internal transfer, the most reliable route in.
- The external hire and how to make it work without PM experience.
- Associate PM programmes and who they are for.
- The one thing every route has in common.

## Where PMs come from

**Engineering.** Brings technical credibility, an understanding of what is hard, and the
respect of the team you will work with most. Lacks, often, the habit of talking to users and
the tolerance for ambiguity that comes from problems without a correct answer. The risk is
becoming the PM who designs the solution before understanding the problem. The pragmatic
engineer's essay in the assignment describes the engineers who make the jump well.

**Design.** Brings user empathy, research skills, and the ability to make ideas visible.
Lacks, often, comfort with business models, metrics and saying no to beautiful things. The
risk is a product that is lovely and does not make money.

**Customer support and success.** Brings the deepest knowledge of what users actually
struggle with, and a tolerance for difficult conversations. Lacks, often, exposure to
strategy and to engineering. The risk is prioritising the loudest complaint over the
largest problem. One of the most underrated routes in.

**Consulting and business analysis.** Brings structured thinking, stakeholder management,
and writing. Lacks, often, the experience of living with a decision after it ships.
Consultants leave; PMs stay and find out. The risk is frameworks over judgement.

**Data and analytics.** Brings rigour, comfort with metrics, and scepticism about claims.
Lacks, often, qualitative instincts and the willingness to decide before the data is
conclusive. The risk is waiting for certainty that never arrives.

**Founding.** Brings everything at once, including the scars. Founders who become PMs have
done discovery, strategy, delivery and measurement under pressure. Lacks, often, patience
with organisations and with not being the one who decides. The risk is frustration.

**Outside tech.** Teachers, lawyers, doctors, operations managers, marketers. Brings domain
expertise that technologists lack, plus people skills built in harder environments. Lacks
technical vocabulary and network. The risk is being screened out before anyone talks to
you. Course 5 exists partly to close the first gap; the next section addresses the second.

None of these is a better starting point than the others. Each produces a recognisable kind
of PM, and the best teams have a mix.

## Route one: the internal transfer

The most reliable way into product management is to already work at a company that has
PMs, do adjacent work well, and move across. It works because the hardest part of hiring a
first-time PM is trust, and your colleagues already trust you.

How it goes, usually:

1. **Do PM-shaped work in your current role.** Talk to customers, write a one-pager about a
   problem you see, propose a small experiment, volunteer for the planning meeting. Lesson
   1.1.4's one-hour test, done for real, at work.
2. **Tell your manager and a PM you want to move.** Early. People cannot help you with a
   plan they do not know about.
3. **Find a PM who will let you shadow or take a slice.** A small feature, a research
   project, a launch checklist. Real work, visibly done.
4. **Wait for an opening or make one.** Headcount appears. Someone leaves. A new area needs
   an owner and you are the person who already knows it.

The internal route takes six to eighteen months. It is slow and it is the one with the
highest success rate.

## Route two: the external hire

Applying for PM jobs from outside, without the title. This is harder and it is how many
people do it, especially from consulting, design and founding.

What makes it work:

- **A portfolio, not a CV.** You cannot show PM experience. You can show PM artifacts. The
  projects in this course, from the audience map to the eval suite, are exactly that. Section
  8.2 turns them into a portfolio.
- **A story about why.** Hiring managers will ask why you want to switch. "I've already been
  doing the job without the title" with evidence beats "I've always been interested in
  product".
- **Targeting companies that hire first-time PMs.** Scale-ups and companies in your current
  industry. Big tech rarely hires external first-time PMs outside associate programmes.
- **Using the domain you have.** A nurse applying to a health product, a banker applying to
  a fintech, a teacher applying to an education product. Domain knowledge is the one thing
  you have that the other candidates do not.

Expect many rejections before the first yes. That is the route, not a verdict on you.

## Route three: associate PM programmes

A small number of companies run structured programmes that take people straight out of
university or early career into product management, with rotations and mentoring. The best
known began at Google in 2002, and several large companies now run their own.

They are excellent and they are rare. They are also not for everyone: most take people with
little work experience, which means a career-changer with eight years in another field is
usually not the target. If you are early in your career and have a technical or analytical
degree, look for them. If you are not, do not treat their absence from your life as a gap.

## What every route has in common

Whichever door you come through, the same three things get you in and keep you there:

1. **Evidence you can do the work.** Artifacts, not adjectives.
2. **Someone who vouches for you.** A manager, a PM, a founder, a hiring manager who took a
   chance. Networks are how trust travels. Lesson 8.2.2 is about building one without being
   awful.
3. **A reason the company believes.** Why you, why product, why here. Section 8.3 covers how
   to tell it.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Gergely Orosz's [The Product-Minded Software Engineer](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/the-product-minded-engineer/),
   fifteen minutes. Written about engineers who think like PMs, it is the clearest
   description of what any background should show before switching: curiosity about why,
   not just how.
2. Read Lenny Rachitsky's
   [How to get into product management](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-get-into-product-management),
   twenty minutes. A survey of how real PMs got their first role, with the routes above
   broken down by frequency. Note which route people from your background used most.
3. Read Google's description of its
   [Associate Product Manager programme](https://buildyourfuture.withgoogle.com/programs/apm),
   five minutes, to see what the structured route looks like and whether you are in its
   target group.
4. Identify your own starting point from the list above. Write two hundred words on what it
   gives you, what it lacks, and which of the three routes is realistic for you in the next
   twelve months. Then write the single most useful thing you could do this month toward
   that route. Keep it; the portfolio project builds on it.

  hired many.

  career.
