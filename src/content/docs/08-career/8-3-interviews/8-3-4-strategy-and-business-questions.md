## Introduction

"Should Spotify enter the audiobook market?" "How would you price a new tier for small
businesses?" "A competitor just launched a free version of your core feature. What do you
do?" These are strategy questions, and they appear more often as you go up the ladder and at
companies where PMs own business outcomes rather than features.

They feel like consulting case interviews, and they borrow some of that structure. But the
interviewer is a product leader, not a consultant, and what they want is a product manager's
judgement about users, markets and the company's real advantages. Course 4 gave you the
material. This lesson shows the shape of a good answer and works through the four most common
question types.

## The structure

Every strategy answer has the same five movements. Spend them in roughly this proportion.

**Goal.** Clarify what the company is trying to achieve and over what horizon. Growth,
profit, defence, a mission? A good answer to "should we enter this market" is different for a
company trying to grow revenue next year and one trying to build a moat over a decade.

**Situation.** Three or four facts that matter: the company's advantages (distribution, data,
brand, cost), the market (size, growth, who wins today), the user (what job they are hiring a
product for, lesson 3.1.2), the constraints (capital, regulation, time). Lesson 4.1.3's
frameworks are useful here as checklists, but say the facts, not the framework names.

**Options.** Two or three genuinely different paths. Include "do nothing" if it is credible.

**Choice.** Pick one and give the two or three reasons that drove it. Say what you would need
to believe for it to be the right call, which is lesson 4.1.1's narrative in miniature.

**Risks and what you would watch.** Name the biggest risk, how you would reduce it, and the
metric or signal that would tell you to stop. Interviewers rate this movement highly and most
candidates skip it.

Twenty-five minutes. If you run short, cut the situation, not the choice.

## Market entry

"Should a mobile-money app in Nigeria enter Ghana?"

Goal: growth, and a stated ambition to be pan-African. Situation: Ghana has a smaller
population, higher mobile-money penetration dominated by telecom operators, different
regulation, and a currency the company does not hold. The company's advantages are an agent
network playbook and a brand among Nigerian traders, some of whom trade across the border.
Options: full consumer launch; a cross-border remittance product for the Nigeria-Ghana
corridor first; a partnership with an existing Ghanaian operator; wait and enter a larger
market instead.

Choice: the corridor product. It uses the existing advantage, needs a licence on only one
side initially, and teaches the company about Ghanaian regulation and users before a full
launch. What you would need to believe: that corridor volume is large enough to matter, which
you would size as in lesson 3.3.1. Risk: the telecom incumbents respond by cutting remittance
fees; signal to watch is corridor volume and take rate in the first two quarters.

Notice that the answer uses the course's own vocabulary: wedge, early adopters, what you would
need to believe, the metric that tells you to stop. That is not an accident. Strategy
interviews test whether you have internalised Course 4.

## Pricing

"How would you price a new tier for small businesses?"

Interviewers do not expect a number. They expect you to know what pricing depends on. Start
with value: what does the tier let a small business do, and what is that worth to them in
time or money? Then anchors: what do they pay today for the alternative, including the
alternative of doing it by hand? Then structure: per seat, per usage, flat monthly, and why
one fits the way these customers think about cost. Then the business: margin, the risk of
cannibalising a higher tier, the sales motion a price point implies. Finish with how you
would test it: a willingness-to-pay survey, a price test in one market, or a pilot with a
handful of customers.

Lesson 1.2.3 covered the vocabulary. The key signal is that you reason from the customer's
value and the company's alternatives, not from cost plus a margin.

## Build, buy or partner

"We need a payments capability. Build it, buy a company, or partner?"

Three questions decide most of these. Is this capability core to how we win, or a commodity
we need to have? Core leans build; commodity leans partner. How fast do we need it, and what
does delay cost? Partner is fastest, build is slowest, buy is in between and expensive. What
do we lose with each: control and margin with a partner, focus and capital with a build,
integration pain and culture with a buy? Say which you would choose for this company at this
stage, and name the trigger that would change the answer. Lesson 7.2.3 made the same argument
for AI capabilities; the logic is identical.

## Competitive response

"A competitor just made free the feature we charge for. What do you do?"

The trap is to react. The strong answer first asks whether it matters: who uses that feature,
how much of our value and revenue does it carry, and are those users at risk of leaving?
Then it considers the competitor's position: can they sustain free, and what are they trying
to achieve? Then options: match, ignore, differentiate by going deeper on the feature or
moving value elsewhere, or reprice the bundle. Choice with reasons, risk, signal to watch.
Lesson 4.1.2's wedges and systems and lesson 3.3.2's competitive analysis are both in play.
The candidate who says "I would not immediately match, and here is what I would look at
first" is usually ahead.

## What is being graded

- Did you clarify the goal before answering?
- Did you reason from the company's actual advantages and the user's actual job?
- Were your options genuinely different?
- Did you choose and say what you would need to believe?
- Did you name a risk and a signal to watch?
- Did you sound like a product manager, not a consultant?
