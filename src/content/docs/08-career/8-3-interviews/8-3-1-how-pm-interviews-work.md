## Introduction

Product management interviews have a reputation for being vague. "Design a fridge for the
blind." "Our daily active users dropped eight percent, what do you do?" "Should we enter
Brazil?" Candidates who have done the job for years walk out feeling they were tested on
something other than the job.

They were not. Every one of those questions is a compressed version of real PM work, and the
loop is built to see how you think when the problem is unfamiliar and the clock is running.
Once you can see what each round is testing, the vagueness goes away and preparation becomes
ordinary practice. This lesson maps the loop, shows how it varies by company, and gives you a
four-week plan that uses the projects you have already built in this course as your material.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

## The loop

Most PM processes have the same skeleton, even when the names change.

**Recruiter screen.** Twenty to thirty minutes. Tests whether you are a plausible fit for the
level and the team, and whether you can explain your background in two minutes. Not a
formality: a muddled answer to "tell me about yourself" ends processes.

**Hiring manager.** Forty-five to sixty minutes. Tests whether you have done the work. Expect
to walk through one product you shipped in depth: why it was built, how you decided, what
happened, what you would change. This is where your project portfolio from this course earns
its keep.

**Product sense, sometimes called product design.** The "design X for Y" round. Tests
whether you can find a user, find their problem, generate options, pick one and say how you
would know it worked. Lesson 8.3.2.

**Execution, sometimes called analytical.** Metric drops, "how would you measure", trade-offs,
estimation. Tests whether you can reason with data and prioritise under constraints. Lesson
8.3.3.

**Strategy.** Market entry, pricing, build versus buy, competitive moves. More common at
senior levels and at companies where PMs own business outcomes. Lesson 8.3.4.

**Behavioural.** Conflict, failure, influence, ambiguity. Tests whether you can work with
people and whether you learn. Lesson 8.3.5.

**Technical.** How a system works, how you would explain a trade-off to engineers, sometimes
light system design. Rarely coding. Lesson 8.3.6.

**Take-home or presentation.** Some companies send a case and ask you to present it to a
panel. It tests the whole job at once: thinking, writing, presenting, handling questions.

Not every company runs every round, and many combine two into one conversation. But if you
can do each of these cold, the combinations hold no surprises.

## What each round is actually testing

Interviewers are not grading whether you reach the "right" answer. There rarely is one. They
are grading a short list of signals, and the same signals appear across rounds:

- **Structure.** Do you break a messy question into parts before diving in?
- **User focus.** Do you name a specific user and their problem before proposing anything?
- **Judgement.** When you have to choose, do you choose, and can you say why?
- **Evidence.** Do you reach for data, and do you know what data you would want?
- **Communication.** Can the interviewer follow you without effort?
- **Self-awareness.** When you are wrong or stuck, do you notice and adjust?

A candidate who shows all six and reaches a mediocre conclusion beats one who lands on a
brilliant idea by luck and cannot explain how they got there.

## How companies differ

**Large technology companies** run the most standardised loops, often five or six rounds in a
day, with written rubrics and multiple interviewers per round. Preparation pays off most here
because the formats are public and stable. Frameworks are expected but must be worn lightly.

**Startups** compress everything. Two or three conversations, often including the founder, and
a strong bias toward "show me what you have shipped." The product sense round is usually
about their product. Read it, use it, have opinions before you walk in.

**Non-tech companies hiring their first PMs**, banks, telecoms, retailers, logistics firms,
often do not know how to interview for the role. Expect a more conventional interview, more
questions about stakeholder management and delivery, and some confusion about what a PM is.
Lesson 1.1.2's layer decoder tells you which job they are actually hiring for. Part of your
task in the room is to show them what good looks like.

**Remote-first and international hiring** adds a screening layer on written communication.
Your application email, your portfolio README and your take-home all get read as writing
samples. Course 2's writing lessons apply directly.

## Your projects are the examples

Interviewers ask for examples because examples are hard to fake. By this point in the course
you have a repository of them, and each maps to a round:

- The role audit (1.1.5) and the letter to your past self (2.4.5) feed "tell me about
  yourself" and "why product management".
- The five interviews (3.2.5) and the opportunity tree (3.3.4) are your discovery stories.
- The strategy pack (4.2.5) answers "tell me about a time you prioritised".
- The PRD (5.1.4) and launch pack (5.4.4) answer "walk me through something you shipped".
- The experiment analysis (6.8) is a ready-made execution story with real numbers.
- The AI PRD (7.3.6), eval suite (7.4.6) and prototype (7.6.4) are what AI PM roles ask for
  and almost nobody has.

If you are an experienced PM, use work examples where you can and course examples to fill
gaps. If you are new to the role, the course examples are not a consolation prize. They are
evidence that you can do the work, which is exactly what the interviewer is trying to find
out.

## A four-week plan

Four weeks of focused preparation, at the same eight to ten hours a week you have been giving
this course, is enough for most loops.

1. **Week one: inventory.** Write out six to eight stories from your projects and work in a
   consistent form: situation, what you did, what happened, what you learned. Tag each with
   the rounds it serves. Reread lessons 1.1.1 and 1.1.4 and rewrite your hundred-word
   definition of the job.
2. **Week two: product sense and execution.** Work through lessons 8.3.2 and 8.3.3. Do two
   practice questions a day out loud, timed, from the open question banks linked below.
   Record yourself once.
3. **Week three: strategy, behavioural, technical.** Lessons 8.3.4 to 8.3.6. Do one mock
   interview with a peer from the course Discussions.
4. **Week four: mocks and rest.** Two or three full mocks, then stop practising two days
   before the loop. Tired candidates ramble.

The mock interviews project (8.3.8) is this plan's final step.

## Mistakes that lose strong candidates

- **Answering the question you wish you were asked.** Clarify first. Thirty seconds of
  questions saves ten minutes of the wrong answer.
- **Reciting a framework.** Interviewers can hear it. Use the structure, hide the scaffolding.
- **Never choosing.** "It depends" is where you start, not where you finish.
- **Ignoring the interviewer.** They will give hints. Take them.
- **Treating the hiring manager round as a chat.** It is the most important round and the
  least prepared for.
