## Introduction

"Design an alarm clock for the blind." "Improve the experience of waiting for a bus."
"How would you build a product for people who have just moved to a new city?" These are
product sense questions, and they are the signature of the PM interview. Nobody will ever
ask you to design an alarm clock at work. What they are testing is whether you can go from a
vague brief to a specific, defensible proposal in thirty minutes, which is something you will
do at work every week.

This lesson gives you a structure, two worked examples in prose, and the reason the structure
must stay invisible. It leans on everything from Course 3 on discovery and Course 4 on
prioritisation. If those felt abstract, this is where they pay.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What the product sense round is actually grading.
- A six-step structure: clarify, choose a user, find the problem, generate options,
  prioritise, define success.
- Two worked examples.
- Why frameworks help and why reciting one loses the interview.
- How to practise so the structure becomes instinct.

## What is being graded

The interviewer has a rubric with roughly these lines on it: did the candidate clarify the
goal, did they pick a specific user rather than "everyone", did they identify a real problem
before proposing solutions, were the solutions creative and varied, did they prioritise with a
stated reason, did they say how they would measure success, and could I follow them. Reaching
a clever idea is nice. Showing all seven is what gets the hire.

Notice that this is lesson 1.1.1's three questions in miniature: what should we build and
why, is it feasible, did it work.

## The six steps

**Clarify.** Spend two minutes, no more, asking what the goal is and what the constraints are.
Is this for an existing company or a new one? Mobile, web, physical? Is the goal growth,
revenue, retention, a mission? What is out of scope? Then state your understanding back in
one sentence and move on. Candidates who clarify for ten minutes are stalling and the
interviewer knows it.

**Choose a user.** List three or four plausible user groups, say one sentence about each,
and pick one with a reason. "I will focus on recently arrived students, because they have
the most acute version of the problem and the least existing support." Picking is the point.
Lesson 3.1.3 on segments and early adopters is this step.

**Find the problem.** For your chosen user, name two or three real problems. Make them
specific and grounded in what you know or can reasonably infer. Then pick the one that is
most painful, most frequent, or least well served. Say which criterion you used. Lesson
3.1.1: problems before solutions.

**Generate options.** Give three solutions that are genuinely different from each other, not
three variants of one idea. Include one that is small and one that is ambitious. Interviewers
grade range here. If you are stuck, change the axis: a different channel, a different moment
in the user's day, a different business model.

**Prioritise.** Pick one, out loud, with a reason that uses two or three criteria: impact on
the problem, effort, risk, fit with the company. A two-by-two sketched on the whiteboard is
fine. Lesson 4.2.3 gave you the vocabulary. Do not say "I would do all three."

**Define success.** Name one primary metric and one or two guardrails, and say what you would
expect to see in the first month if it worked. Lesson 6.2. Then stop and ask if they want
you to go deeper anywhere.

The whole thing takes twenty-five to thirty minutes. Spend most of it on the middle four
steps.

## Worked example one: waiting for a bus

Asked to improve the experience of waiting for a bus, you clarify: is this for a city transit
authority or a third-party app, and is the goal rider satisfaction or ridership? Say the
answer is a transit authority wanting more riders.

Users: daily commuters, occasional riders who do not know the system, people with mobility
constraints, parents with children. You pick occasional riders, because the uncertainty of
waiting is what keeps them in taxis, and converting them is where ridership growth is.

Problems: not knowing when the bus is coming, not knowing whether this is the right stop, not
feeling safe at night. You pick the first, because it is the most frequent and the authority
already has location data it does not expose well.

Options: live arrival times on a sign at every stop, which is expensive and slow to roll out;
a text message service where you send the stop code and get the next three arrivals, which
works on any phone; a partnership putting arrival data into the mapping apps people already
use. You prioritise the text service: lowest effort, works without a smartphone or data, and
can ship in a quarter, with the mapping partnership as the follow-on.

Success: the share of occasional riders who take a second trip within thirty days, with a
guardrail on message cost per rider. You would expect text volume to show adoption within
weeks and the repeat-trip metric to move within a quarter.

Nothing in that answer is brilliant. All of it is structured, specific and chosen.

## Worked example two: a product for new arrivals to a city

Clarify: a startup, consumer, mobile, goal is to find a wedge into a large eventual market.
Users: students, professionals relocating for work, migrants arriving with families, retirees.
You pick professionals relocating for work: they have money, a deadline, and an employer who
may pay.

Problems: finding housing they can trust, building a social circle, handling the
administrative maze of a new country. You pick housing, because it is the first, most
expensive decision and the one where fraud is most common.

Options: a verified-listings marketplace, a concierge service that does the search for a fee,
a community of recent arrivals who share listings and warnings. You prioritise the concierge
service: it is unscalable, which lesson 1.1.3's startup paragraph told you is right at this
stage, it generates revenue from day one, and it teaches you exactly what the marketplace
will need later. Success: completed placements per month and the share of customers who refer
a colleague.

## Why frameworks help and why reciting one loses

The six steps are a framework. Use them. The failure mode is announcing them: "Now I will
clarify. Now I will segment the users. Now I will brainstorm." Interviewers hear that daily
and it signals memorisation rather than thought. The structure should show in the shape of
your answer, not in its headings. Move through the steps with natural transitions: "Given
that, the people I would focus on are..." or "Of those three, the one I would build first is..."

A second failure is letting the framework replace judgement. If the interviewer pushes back
on your user choice, do not defend the framework. Engage with the pushback, and change your
mind if they are right. That flexibility is itself a signal they are grading.

## How to practise

Out loud, timed, with a listener if you can get one. Reading answers in your head does not
build the skill. Take a question from the bank linked below, set a timer for thirty minutes,
and talk through it to a peer, a recording or a wall. Then review: did you choose at every
step? Did you give reasons? Where did you ramble? Two a day for a week is more than most
candidates ever do.
