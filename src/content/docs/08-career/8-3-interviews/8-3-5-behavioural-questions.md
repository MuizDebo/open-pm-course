## Introduction

"Tell me about a time when" is the most common question in any PM interview loop and the
one candidates prepare for least. People rehearse product sense and strategy because those
feel like the hard part. Then they get asked about a disagreement with an engineer and give
a vague, flattering summary that tells the interviewer nothing.

Behavioural questions are not small talk. They are the interviewer's best tool for finding
out how you actually behave, as opposed to how you say you would. A hiring manager who has
heard forty candidates can tell a real story from a reconstructed one in about fifteen
seconds. This lesson is about having real stories ready, telling them so the point is clear,
and doing it without sounding like you are reading a template.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

## What the question is really asking

Behind every "tell me about a time" is a trait the interviewer is probing. "Tell me about a
disagreement with engineering" is asking whether you can hold a position without
steamrolling people. "Tell me about a product that failed" is asking whether you take
ownership or deflect. "Tell me about influencing a decision you did not control" is asking
the central question of Course 2: can you get outcomes without authority?

Before you answer, name the trait to yourself. It tells you which details matter. A story
about a launch delay told to show ownership emphasises what you decided and what you
changed afterwards. The same story told to show communication emphasises how you kept
stakeholders informed. Same events, different story.

## STAR without sounding like STAR

The STAR structure, situation, task, action, result, is useful because it stops you from
skipping the part that matters, which is what you personally did. It is harmful when it
turns into a recital where each section is announced like a heading.

Use it as a checklist, not a script:

- **Situation.** Two sentences. Enough context for the listener to see the stakes, no
  more. Interviewers switch off during long setups.
- **Task.** What was your responsibility, specifically? "I was the PM on the payments
  team" is not a task. "I owned the decision on whether to ship with the known bug" is.
- **Action.** The bulk of the answer. What you did, in the first person. Not "we decided",
  "I recommended". Not "the team pushed back", "the tech lead told me it would take six
  weeks and I had to decide whether to believe him".
- **Result.** What happened, with a number if you have one, and what you learned or changed.
  A result without a lesson sounds like luck.

Told well, it sounds like a story a colleague would tell over coffee, with a beginning, a
turn and an ending, and it takes about two minutes. If you are past three, you have included
too much situation.

## The eight stories

Most behavioural questions are variations on a small set of themes. Prepare one true story
for each of these and you will have an answer for nearly anything:

1. **A disagreement with engineering** that you resolved without pulling rank you did not
   have. Lesson 1.3.1 and 2.3.4 are the background.
2. **A product or feature that failed**, where you were responsible. Lesson 2.1.2 on
   accountability versus blame is the tone to aim for.
3. **A time you changed your mind** because of evidence, and what it cost you to say so.
   Lesson 2.1.4.
4. **Influencing a decision you did not control.** Lesson 1.3.3. The more senior the person
   you influenced, the better the story.
5. **Saying no** to a stakeholder with a reasonable request. Lesson 2.1.3.
6. **A hard prioritisation** where good things were cut. Course 4.
7. **Dealing with ambiguity**, where nobody could tell you what the right answer was and you
   had to make one.
8. **Handling a conflict between users and the business**, or between two sets of users.

If you have done the projects in this course, several of these already exist in your
decision journal from project 2.1.5 and your letter from project 2.4.5. That is where to
look first. A decision journal entry is a behavioural answer with the dates still attached.

If you are early in your career and have not done PM work, use the nearest equivalent:
group projects, volunteer work, a side project, a job in another field. Interviewers care
about the behaviour, not the title. Say plainly that the context was different and then
tell the story properly.

## Telling a failure well

The failure story is the one most people fluff. The temptation is to pick a fake failure,
"I worked too hard", or to tell a real one and then blame circumstances. Both are visible
from across the room.

A good failure story has four parts. What you decided and why it seemed right at the time.
What actually happened. What you got wrong, named specifically and owned. What you do
differently now, with an example of having done it. The last part is what turns a
confession into evidence of growth. Course 2's blameless postmortem framing applies
exactly: the point is the learning, not the self-flagellation.

Pick a real failure of moderate size. Not a typo, not a company-ending disaster. Something
where a reasonable PM could have made your mistake and a better one would not have.

## Where answers go wrong

- **Too much "we".** The interviewer needs to know what you did. If the honest answer is
  that you were a small part of it, pick a different story.
- **No result.** The story ends at "and then we shipped it". Shipped it and then what?
- **A result with no lesson.** "It went great" tells nobody anything.
- **Reconstructed detail.** You cannot remember the numbers so you invent them. Interviewers
  probe, and invented details fall apart under one follow-up question. Say "roughly" and
  give the real rough number.
- **Rehearsed to death.** You have told the story so many times that it has lost the pauses
  where you actually think. Practise the stories out loud a few times and then stop.
- **Badmouthing.** The engineer was difficult, the CEO was wrong, the designer did not
  listen. Even when true, it makes the interviewer wonder how you will describe them next
  year. Describe the disagreement, not the person.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read the Wikipedia summary of the
   [situation, task, action, result](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situation,_task,_action,_result)
   structure, five minutes, so you know the standard form and can hear when an answer is
   too obviously following it.
2. Read Julia Evans on [brag documents](https://jvns.ca/blog/brag-documents/), fifteen
   minutes. It is about tracking your own work so you can describe it later, which is
   exactly the inventory behavioural interviews draw on. Start one today if you do not have
   one.
3. Watch Matt Huang's
   [Answering behavioral interview questions is shockingly uncomplicated](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WdyiUe7_3cA),
   a short video. His point, that the answer is a plain story with a clear point, is the
   antidote to over-rehearsed STAR.
4. Read Amazon's [leadership principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles),
   ten minutes. Many companies' behavioural loops are built around a list like this, whether
   or not they say so. Map your eight stories against it and see which principles you have
   no story for.
5. Write your eight stories, one paragraph each, in your work repository. Say which trait
   each one demonstrates. Mine your decision journal from project 2.1.5 first. You will use
   these in project 8.3.8.

</div>
