## Introduction

You have done eight interviews and the recruiter calls with a number. Most people say yes
within a minute, partly from relief and partly from fear that asking for more will make the
offer vanish. It almost never does. Companies expect negotiation, budget for it, and the
recruiter's job is to close you, not to withdraw.

This lesson is about the twenty minutes of your career that are most expensive to get
wrong. It covers how to read an offer, what the parts actually mean, how to negotiate like a
product manager, which is to say with evidence and without drama, and when to walk away. It
does not give salary figures, because they go stale and vary wildly by market; the sources
in the assignment do that.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The parts of an offer and how to compare two of them.
- What equity means at different company stages, and what it is usually worth.
- The negotiation itself: timing, tone, evidence, and the one rule that matters most.
- Competing offers and how to use them honestly.
- When to say no.

## Reading the offer

An offer is at least four numbers pretending to be one. Separate them.

**Base salary.** The only number that is certain. Everything else has conditions.

**Bonus.** Usually a target percentage of base, paid on company and personal performance.
Ask what the actual payout has been for the last two years. "Target 15 percent" that pays
out at 60 percent is 9 percent.

**Equity.** Shares or options that vest over time, typically four years with a one-year
cliff, meaning nothing until month twelve. Its value depends entirely on the company's stage
and future, covered below.

**Everything else.** Health cover, pension or retirement contributions, leave, learning
budget, remote-work terms, relocation, a signing bonus. These are negotiable too, and some
are easier to move than base.

Put both numbers side by side in a spreadsheet when comparing offers, with equity valued
conservatively, and look at year-one cash separately from four-year total. The offer with
the bigger headline is often not the bigger offer.

## What equity actually means

Equity is where candidates most often fool themselves, because the company has every
incentive to present it generously.

At a **public company**, equity is restricted stock units with a known market price. Value
it at the current price, and remember it can fall. This is the only case where the headline
number is roughly real.

At a **late-stage private company**, you will usually be given a number of shares and a
"preferred price" from the last funding round. Your common shares are worth less than that,
the company may never go public, and you may not be able to sell for years. Value it at a
steep discount, or at zero for the purpose of deciding whether you can pay rent.

At an **early-stage startup**, you will usually get options: the right to buy shares at a
fixed price. They are worth something only if the company succeeds and you can afford to
exercise them. Ask for the number of shares, the total shares outstanding so you know your
percentage, the strike price, and the latest valuation. If they will not tell you the
percentage, treat that as information.

None of this means equity is bad. It means you should treat it as a lottery ticket with
good odds, not as salary, and never accept a lower base because the equity "makes up for
it" unless you can afford to lose the equity entirely.

## Negotiating like a PM

Everything in Course 2 and Course 4 applies here. You are making a case, with evidence, to
someone who has constraints you cannot see, and the relationship continues after the
decision.

**Do not give the first number.** When asked for salary expectations early in the process,
say you would like to understand the role fully first and ask what range they have budgeted.
If pressed, give a range anchored on research, with your real target at the bottom of it.

**Negotiate after the offer, not before.** Once they have decided they want you, your
position is strongest. Before that, it is weakest.

**Ask for time.** "Thank you, I am very pleased. Can I take a few days to review it
properly?" is always acceptable. Nobody good has ever lost an offer for asking for 48 hours.

**Bring evidence, not feelings.** Market data for the role and location, your competing
offers if any, the specific value you bring that the interview surfaced. "I was hoping for
more" is a feeling. "Comparable roles at this level in this market are paying X to Y, and
my offer is below that range" is evidence.

**Ask for one thing clearly.** Pick the component that matters most to you and ask for a
specific number. Vague requests get vague answers. If base is fixed, which it sometimes
genuinely is at larger companies with bands, move to signing bonus, equity, level or start
date.

**Stay warm.** The recruiter is not your adversary. They are the person who will advocate
for you internally if you give them something to advocate with. Be the candidate they want
to go back to the hiring manager for.

**Get it in writing.** Verbal agreements evaporate. Nothing is agreed until the revised
offer letter arrives.

The one rule above all of them: never bluff. Do not invent a competing offer, do not claim
a number you cannot support. The market is small, people talk, and a bluff that gets called
ends the conversation.

## Competing offers

A real competing offer is the strongest evidence you can have, and you may use it. Tell the
company you prefer them, tell them the gap, and ask whether they can close it. Do not share
the other company's letter unless asked, and do not play the two against each other beyond
one round. Companies will match once; they resent being used as a bidding floor.

If you do not have a competing offer, do not pretend to. Use market data instead. It is
weaker evidence, and it is honest.

## When to walk away

Some offers should be declined, however good the number. The interviews showed you a manager
you did not trust. The company could not explain what the PM role actually does, which
lesson 1.1.4 warned you about. The equity is the only attractive part and you cannot afford
for it to be worth nothing. They pressured you to decide in 24 hours. Any of these is a
reason to say no, and the ability to say it is itself a negotiating position.

A good decline is short, warm and leaves the door open. People move between companies. The
recruiter you decline politely this year may be the one who calls with the right role in
two.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Patrick McKenzie's
   [Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2012/01/23/salary-negotiation/),
   thirty minutes. The most-read essay on the subject for a reason. Read it for the
   reframing: negotiation is a normal business conversation, not a confrontation.
2. Read Haseeb Qureshi's
   [Ten Rules for Negotiating a Job Offer](https://haseebq.com/my-ten-rules-for-negotiating-a-job-offer/),
   twenty minutes, and the follow-up
   [How Not to Bomb Your Offer Negotiation](https://haseebq.com/how-not-to-bomb-your-offer-negotiation/),
   twenty minutes. Written about engineering offers; every rule transfers.
3. Read the opening sections of the
   [Open Guide to Equity Compensation](https://github.com/jlevy/og-equity-compensation),
   thirty minutes, at least through the parts on vesting, options and taxes. You do not
   need to finish it. You need to know the questions to ask when an offer includes equity.
4. Look up the role and level you are targeting on [Levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/) or
   the best equivalent for your market, fifteen minutes. Note the range. This is the
   evidence you will use, and the reason this lesson gives no numbers of its own.
5. Write your negotiation script for a hypothetical offer that comes in 15 percent below
   your target: the opening thank-you, the request for time, the evidence, the one specific
   ask, and the close. Keep it under a page. Practise it aloud once with a friend playing a
   polite but firm recruiter.
