## Introduction

There are more product manager jobs than the job boards show, and the ones on the boards are
the most contested. Knowing where roles actually come from, how to read a posting for what it
is really asking, and how to run a search without losing your mind are skills in themselves.
This lesson treats the job search as what it is: a funnel you manage, with a conversion rate
you can improve.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The four channels roles come through, and their odds.
- Boards worth watching, general, PM-specific, remote and regional.
- Reading a posting with the decoder from lesson 1.1.2.
- When to apply even if you miss requirements.
- Running the search as a pipeline with a weekly review.

## The four channels

**Referrals.** Someone inside recommends you. The highest conversion by far, because the
hiring manager starts from trust. Everything in lesson 8.2.2 feeds this channel.

**Direct to the company.** You find a company you want to work for and apply on its careers
page, ideally after an informational interview with someone there. Less crowded than boards,
and the application is read by a human sooner.

**Recruiters.** Internal recruiters find you through LinkedIn searches, which is why lesson
8.2.1 fixed your headline. External recruiters matter more at senior levels.

**Boards.** Public postings. The most volume and the most competition. Useful for finding
out who is hiring and for the roles nobody you know has a connection to.

Spend your time in roughly that order of priority, and do not spend it all on the last one.

## Boards worth watching

General boards get the most postings but the least signal. PM-specific and curated boards
have fewer roles and better ones. A reasonable watchlist:

- **PM-specific.** [Mind the Product jobs](https://www.mindtheproduct.com/jobs/) and
  [Lenny's job board](https://www.lennysjobs.com/) both curate product roles, the second
  skewing to well-funded technology companies.
- **Startups.** [Wellfound](https://wellfound.com/) (formerly AngelList Talent) and
  [Otta](https://otta.com/) focus on startups and scale-ups and show salary ranges more
  often than most.
- **Remote.** [We Work Remotely](https://weworkremotely.com/),
  [Remotive](https://remotive.com/) and [Remote OK](https://remoteok.com/) list remote-first
  roles. Check whether "remote" means your time zone or theirs.
- **Regional.** In Nigeria, [Jobberman](https://www.jobberman.com/) and
  [MyJobMag](https://www.myjobmag.com/) carry most locally advertised product roles.
  Equivalent boards exist in most countries; ask in a local community which one people
  actually use.
- **Company pages.** Make a list of twenty companies you would want to work at and check
  their careers pages fortnightly. Many roles never reach a board.

Set up alerts rather than browsing. Browsing boards is the job-search equivalent of
refreshing a dashboard: it feels productive and is not.

## Reading a posting

Lesson 1.1.2 gave you a decoder for which layer of product management a job description
comes from. Use it on every posting, and add these questions:

- **Is there a user in the first paragraph?** If the role is described entirely in terms of
  stakeholders, delivery and process, it is an execution role, whatever the title.
- **Does it mention outcomes or features?** "Drive adoption of X" versus "deliver the X
  roadmap".
- **What does the team look like?** A PM with one designer and six engineers is a different
  job from a PM with none and a vendor.
- **Is the list of requirements a wish list or a filter?** Ten bullets with "nice to have"
  mixed in means a wish list. Three hard requirements means a filter.
- **What is missing?** No mention of data, no mention of customers, no mention of what
  success looks like. Absences tell you what the company does not do.

Then decide whether you want the job, not just whether you could get it.

## Apply anyway

Most postings describe the ideal candidate, not the minimum one. People who meet sixty
percent of the requirements get hired constantly, and the research on who applies shows
that many strong candidates rule themselves out at the posting stage. If you meet the hard
requirements and most of the rest, apply. If you miss a hard requirement, apply if you can
make a credible case in two sentences why it does not matter for this role.

The exception is where the requirement is a legal or practical gate: a work permit, a
language, a location without remote. Do not waste either side's time on those.

## Running the pipeline

A job search is a funnel: roles found, applications sent, first conversations, interview
loops, offers. Treat it like one.

Keep a sheet, or use a tracker such as [Huntr](https://huntr.co/), with one row per role:
company, source channel, date applied, stage, next action, and notes. Review it weekly, as
you would a product dashboard, and ask the funnel questions:

- **Are enough roles coming in at the top?** If not, widen the channels.
- **Where is the drop-off?** Many applications and no first calls means the resume or the
  targeting is wrong. Many first calls and no loops means the story in the screen is not
  landing. Loops and no offers is an interview-skills problem, which section 8.3 covers.
- **What is the cycle time?** Roles take four to eight weeks from application to offer in
  most companies. Plan your runway accordingly.

Twenty well-targeted applications with a referral or an informational interview behind each
beat two hundred cold ones. Your conversion rate will tell you if that is true for you.

   actually works, including how much of it runs on referrals and how badly boards perform.

   [Why women don't apply for jobs unless they're 100% qualified](https://hbr.org/2014/08/why-women-dont-apply-for-jobs-unless-theyre-100-qualified),
   five minutes. The finding applies to everyone who reads a requirements list as a filter
   rather than a wish list.

   above, with a search tight enough that you would consider most of what it returns.

   this month, including the ones you did not apply for and why. Save it in your work
   repository under `8-2-3-pipeline/`.

   message from lesson 8.2.2 to. You do not have to send them yet.
