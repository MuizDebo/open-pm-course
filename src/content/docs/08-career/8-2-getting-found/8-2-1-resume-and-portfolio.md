## Introduction

A hiring manager spends somewhere between six and thirty seconds on a resume before deciding
whether to keep reading. In that time they are answering one question: has this person made
decisions about what to build and been accountable for how it turned out? Everything on the
page either helps answer that or is noise.

Most PM resumes are noise. They list responsibilities ("managed the roadmap", "worked with
engineering") instead of outcomes, they run to two pages, and they are indistinguishable
from a hundred others. This lesson is about writing one that is not, and about the portfolio
that does the heavier lifting when your resume cannot yet say "product manager" on it.

## The one question

Hiring managers are not hiring a list of skills. They are hiring someone to make calls under
uncertainty and own the result. So every line on your resume should be legible as a
decision you made, the scope it covered, and what happened. The shape that works:

**Verb, what you decided or built, for whom, with what result, and the number.**

"Led the redesign of onboarding for a mobile-money app, cutting drop-off at identity
verification from 41 to 27 percent over two quarters" tells the reader you owned a problem,
chose where to intervene, and measured it. "Responsible for onboarding experience" tells
them nothing.

If you do not have the number, say what you can honestly say: the scale ("used by 300
shops"), the duration, the team size, or the qualitative result with a source ("support
tickets on this flow fell to near zero, per the support lead"). A vague claim with no
number is worse than a modest claim with one.

## Scope and judgement

Two things beyond outcomes separate a strong PM resume from a competent one.

**Scope.** How big was the thing you owned? A feature, a product area, a product, a line?
How many engineers, how many users, how much revenue? Say it in each role's first line.
Interviewers calibrate everything else against it.

**Judgement.** Somewhere, ideally once per role, show a decision that was not obvious: a
feature you cut, a launch you delayed, a metric you argued against, a bet that did not pay
off and what you did next. Hiring managers trust people who have been wrong and know it.
The decision journal from lesson 2.1.5 is where these come from.

## One page

One page, unless you have fifteen years of relevant work. The constraint forces the editing
that makes the page readable. What to cut, in order:

1. Anything before your last three roles, unless it is directly relevant. Summarise older
   work in one line.
2. Responsibilities. Replace each with an outcome or delete it.
3. Skills lists. "Jira, Figma, SQL" tells nobody anything. If a tool matters, it appears
   inside an outcome.
4. Objective statements. The reader knows your objective is this job.
5. Hobbies, unless one is a product you built.

What stays: name and contact, a one-line positioning statement (the kind of PM you are and
the kind of problem you are good at, as in lesson 4.1.1), roles with outcomes, education in
one line, and a link to your portfolio.

## The portfolio

If you are already a PM, the portfolio is optional and useful. If you are not, it is the
whole game. Nobody will hire you as a PM on the strength of a resume that says you were a
support lead, an engineer or an analyst. They will hire you if they can see you have already
done the work.

A portfolio is a page with three to six pieces of product work, each with a short write-up:
the problem, what you did, the artifact itself, and what you learned or would do differently.
It proves the things a resume can only claim: that you can frame a problem, talk to users,
make a prioritisation call, write a spec, and reason about data.

This course has been building your portfolio since lesson 0.2. Everything in your work
repository is a candidate. Lead with the pieces that show judgement rather than effort:

- **The strategy pack** (project 4.2.5). Shows you can set goals, prioritise and say no.
- **The five interviews and synthesis** (project 3.2.5). Shows you talk to users and can
  turn what they said into decisions.
- **The PRD** (project 5.1.4). Shows you can write something a team can build from.
- **The experiment analysis** (project 6.8). Shows you can read data honestly and write a
  ship or no-ship memo.
- **The AI opportunity audit or eval suite** (projects 7.2.4 and 7.4.6), if you are aiming
  at AI PM roles. Very few candidates have either.
- **The working prototype** (project 7.6.4), because a thing people can click beats any
  document.

Not everything. Three strong pieces beat eight mixed ones. The project at the end of this
section builds the page.

## LinkedIn, in fifteen minutes

Recruiters search LinkedIn. You do not need to perform there; you need to be findable and
consistent with your resume.

- Headline: the positioning line from your resume, not your current job title. "Product
  manager, payments and onboarding for emerging-market fintech" is searchable. "Senior
  Associate" is not.
- About: three or four sentences, same voice as your resume, with a link to your
  portfolio.
- Experience: the same outcome lines as your resume. Copy them over.
- A real photo, a real location, an "open to work" setting if you are.

Then stop. Posting is covered in the next lesson, and only if you want to.

## Assignment

1. Read Ken Norton's [How to Hire a Product Manager](https://www.bringthedonuts.com/essays/productmanager.html),
   fifteen minutes. Written from the hiring side. Read it for what he is looking for, then
   reread your resume and ask whether any line would make him stop and keep reading.
2. Read the resume and portfolio sections of the
   [Product Management Interview Checklist](https://github.com/product-school/Product-Management-Interview-Checklist),
   ten minutes. A practical list of what to include and what hiring managers skip over.
3. Watch Susan Colantuono's
   [The career advice you probably didn't get](https://www.ted.com/talks/susan_colantuono_the_career_advice_you_probably_didn_t_get),
   fourteen minutes. The missing third of what gets people promoted, business and strategic
   acumen, is exactly what a PM resume has to demonstrate and what most do not.
4. Rewrite your resume to one page using the verb, decision, scope, result, number shape.
   Every role gets at most four lines and at least one number. Save it as a PDF in your work
   repository under `8-2-1-resume/`. You will refine it in the project.
5. Fix your LinkedIn headline and About section to match. Fifteen minutes, no more.
