## Introduction

Most PM jobs are filled through people, not postings. A referral, a former colleague, a
person you helped once, someone who read something you wrote. That is not a conspiracy; it is
how hiring managers reduce risk. It means that if you only apply through job boards, you are
competing in the most crowded channel for the fewest roles.

The word "networking" puts most people off because it sounds like collecting contacts and
asking favours of strangers. That version does not work anyway. The version that works is
slower and more pleasant: be genuinely useful to people in the field, in public, over time,
and ask for small, specific things when you need them. This lesson is the practical form of
that.

## Weak ties

In 1973 the sociologist Mark Granovetter showed that most people found their jobs not through
close friends but through acquaintances: former colleagues, friends of friends, people they
saw occasionally. Close friends know the same things you do. Acquaintances know different
things, including about jobs you have never heard of. The finding has held up for fifty
years.

The practical consequence: the goal of networking is not a few deep relationships with
important people. It is a wide circle of people who know what you do and think well of you.
That is built by being around, being useful and being easy to recommend.

## Communities

You need two or three places where PMs talk, and you need to be a participant, not a lurker.

- **This course's Discussions.** The people reviewing your projects and whose projects you
  review are your first professional network as a PM. Treat it that way.
- **Mind the Product.** The largest independent PM community, with local meetups in many
  cities, a Slack and a conference. The meetups are the valuable part.
- **Product School's community** and events. More beginner-focused, large, free to join.
- **Local meetups.** Search for product meetups in your city. In Lagos, Nairobi, Accra or
  Johannesburg the product communities are smaller and warmer than in London or San
  Francisco, which is an advantage: you will be remembered.
- **Newsletter communities.** Several major PM newsletters run paid communities. Worth it
  only once you are actively job-hunting at a senior level.

How to show up: answer questions you can answer, share one thing you learned each week, and
go to in-person events with the aim of having two real conversations rather than collecting
twenty names. Being known as the person who helps is the whole strategy.

## Informational interviews

An informational interview is a twenty-minute conversation with someone who does the job you
want, at a company you are curious about, where you ask about their work rather than for a
job. Done well, they are how you learn what roles actually involve and how you become a name
the person remembers when a role opens.

Done well means:

- **Ask for twenty minutes, not coffee.** A video call with a fixed end is a smaller ask
  than an hour in a cafe.
- **Do the homework.** Read what they have written, know what their product does, and come
  with three specific questions you could not have answered by searching.
- **Ask about them, not about openings.** "What does a good week look like for you?" and
  "What do you wish you had known before taking this role?" open people up. "Are you
  hiring?" closes them down.
- **End on time.** Even if it is going well. Especially if it is going well.
- **Follow up within a day** with a two-line thank you and the one thing you took from it.
  Then, a few weeks later, send them something relevant with no ask attached.

## The cold message

Most cold messages fail because they are about the sender. The ones that work are short,
specific and about the recipient. A template that gets replies:

> Hi [name]. I read your piece on [specific thing] and it changed how I think about
> [specific thing]; I used it in [what you did]. I am moving into product management from
> [background] and would value twenty minutes to hear how you approach [one specific
> aspect of their work]. Happy to work around your calendar, and no problem if now is not a
> good time.

Four sentences. One specific compliment with evidence you read the thing. One sentence about
you. One specific, bounded ask. One easy exit. Send it to five people a week, not fifty, and
expect one or two replies. That is a good rate.

Never send a connection request with no message, never ask someone you have never spoken to
for a referral, and never follow up more than once.

## Building in public

The slowest and strongest way to build a network is to let people find you. Write up what
you learn as you learn it. Post your course projects with a short reflection. Share the
experiment analysis from project 6.8, the AI opportunity audit from 7.2.4, the things that
went wrong in your prototype. Lesson 7.7.2 made this case for AI PM roles specifically; it
holds for all of them.

You do not need an audience. You need a public record that a hiring manager or a weak tie
can stumble on and think "this person thinks clearly". Three pieces a year is enough to
start. The portfolio you build in the next project is the home for them.

## Mentors

A mentor is not someone you ask to be your mentor. That request puts a weight on the
relationship that most people decline. A mentor is someone whose advice you asked for once,
acted on, and reported back to. Do that three times and you have a mentor, whatever either
of you calls it.

Ask for specific things: a read of your resume, a reaction to how you handled a situation,
one introduction. Report back on what you did with the advice. Offer something in return
when you can, even if it is only a useful link. The report-back is the part almost everyone
skips and the part that turns a favour into a relationship.
