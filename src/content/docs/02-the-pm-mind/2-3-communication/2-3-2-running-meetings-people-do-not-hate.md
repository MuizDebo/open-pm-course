## Introduction

Product managers call more meetings than anyone else on a team, and engineers resent
meetings more than anyone else on a team. Those two facts are related. Every meeting you
call costs the attendees' attention, breaks someone's working block, and signals how much
you value their time. A PM who runs bad meetings is quietly spending down the trust the rest
of the job depends on.

The fix is not fewer meetings on principle. Some decisions genuinely need five people in a
room for twenty minutes. The fix is knowing which decisions those are, and running those
twenty minutes so that everyone leaves knowing what was decided and what happens next.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why meetings cost more than their length, especially for engineers.
- The three questions that tell you whether to meet at all.
- The shape of a meeting that works: purpose, pre-read, decision, owner.
- The handful of meeting types a PM actually runs and what each is for.
- What to do in the ten minutes after.

## The real cost of a meeting

A thirty-minute meeting with six people is not thirty minutes. It is three hours of salary,
plus the time each person spent getting back into what they were doing before, plus the
hour before it that nobody started anything serious because the meeting was coming. For an
engineer in the middle of a hard problem, a meeting at 11am can take out the whole morning.

Managers run on a calendar of hour-long slots and barely notice this. Makers run on half-day
blocks and feel every interruption. A PM sits between the two and has to think like a maker
when scheduling, even while living like a manager. The reading in the assignment is the
classic statement of the difference, and it should change where you put your invites.

## Three questions before you send the invite

1. **Is there a decision to make, or a problem to solve together?** If the purpose is to
   share information, write it down instead. If it is to get a status, ask in writing. A
   meeting exists to do something that cannot be done asynchronously: resolve a disagreement,
   generate options with people who think differently, make a call that needs several
   people's consent at once.
2. **Who actually has to be there?** The people who will decide and the people with
   information the decision needs. Nobody else. Attendees who are there "to stay in the loop"
   can read the notes. Fewer people, faster meetings, and the people who are there speak.
3. **Could a document do the first half?** Most of what happens in the first fifteen minutes
   of a meeting is catching people up. A one-page pre-read, sent a day before, replaces that.
   If people will not read a pre-read, that is worth knowing too.

If the answers are "no decision", "everyone" and "nobody will read it", cancel the meeting
and write the memo.

## The shape of a meeting that works

**A purpose in the invite.** One sentence: what this meeting will decide or produce. If you
cannot write it, you do not have a meeting yet.

**A pre-read.** The one-pager from lesson 2.3.1. Context, options, your recommendation. Some
teams start the meeting with five silent minutes to read it. That feels odd the first time
and saves fifteen minutes every time after.

**A short agenda with times.** Three items at most. The decision item first, while attention
is fresh. Updates, if any, last.

**Someone running it.** Usually you. That means starting on time, naming the decision, cutting
tangents with "good point, let's take that offline, I'll follow up", and watching the clock
so the decision does not get squeezed into the last two minutes.

**A decision, said aloud.** "So we are doing X, not Y, because Z. Does anyone disagree?"
Silence is agreement, and now everyone heard the same thing. A meeting that ends with people
holding different beliefs about what was decided has failed, however pleasant it was.

**An owner and a date for every action.** "Someone should look into that" means nobody will.

## The meetings a PM actually runs

**The decision meeting.** Everything above. Thirty minutes, rarely more.

**The working session.** Two to four people, a whiteboard, a problem to work on together.
Agenda is loose; purpose is tight. Often the most productive hour of the week. Protect it
from becoming a status meeting.

**The planning meeting.** Sprint planning, quarterly planning. Longer, structured, with
material prepared. Course 5 covers the mechanics.

**The one-to-one.** Not a status meeting. The other person's time, for whatever they need:
feedback, concerns, context, career. Lesson 2.3.3 takes this further.

**The review or demo.** The team shows work; stakeholders react. The PM's job is to frame
what is being shown and what kind of feedback is useful, so the reaction is "does this solve
the problem" and not "I would have made the button blue".

**The standup.** Fifteen minutes, standing or not, for the team to surface blockers. If it
is running thirty minutes and everyone is reporting to you, it has become a status meeting
and the team will start skipping it. Fix it before they do.

## The ten minutes after

The meeting is not over when people leave. Within the hour, send the notes: decision,
reasoning in one line, actions with owners and dates, open questions. Short. To everyone who
was invited, including those who did not come. This is what turns a conversation into a
record, and it is the thing attendees will thank you for most.

Then put the actions somewhere you will see them before the next meeting, and check them
the day before. A PM who follows up is a PM whose meetings people come to prepared, because
they know the actions will be asked about.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Paul Graham's
   [Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule](http://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html), ten
   minutes. Read it for the cost of an 11am meeting to someone writing code, and then look
   at where your own invites land in other people's days.
2. Read Steven Rogelberg's
   [Why Your Meetings Stink and What to Do About It](https://hbr.org/2019/01/why-your-meetings-stink-and-what-to-do-about-it)
   in Harvard Business Review, fifteen minutes. The research summary behind most of this
   lesson, by the person who has studied meetings longest.
3. Read Elizabeth Grace Saunders'
   [Do You Really Need to Hold That Meeting?](https://hbr.org/2015/03/do-you-really-need-to-hold-that-meeting),
   five minutes, for a decision tree you can apply before every invite.
4. Take the next meeting you are invited to or would call. Before it, write the one-sentence
   purpose, the three-item agenda and a half-page pre-read. After it, write the notes in the
   format above within an hour. Compare the notes with what you remember being said. Note
   the gaps.

</div>
