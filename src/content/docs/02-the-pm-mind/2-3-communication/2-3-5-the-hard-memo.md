## Introduction

Everything in this section comes together in one document: a memo that says no. Not a
vague no, and not a no hidden in a roadmap. A one-page, signed recommendation that a feature
someone senior asked for should not be built, with the evidence, the strongest case for
building it anyway, and a plan for the conversation with the person who asked.

This is the document PMs most often fail to write. They say yes and let it die quietly, or
they say no in a meeting and leave no record, or they write three pages of hedging that
nobody can act on. A clear hard memo is rare enough that writing one well will be noticed,
and it is a skill you will use for the rest of your career.

## Before you start

You need a feature to cut. Use a real one if you can sanitise it, following lesson 0.3's
rules on confidential material. If not, pick a product you use and a feature it has
announced, or one a vocal group of users keeps asking for, that you believe is the wrong call.
It must be something a specific person would want and be disappointed to lose. A memo
recommending against something nobody cares about is not hard.

You also need the two pieces from earlier in this section: the rewritten memo from lesson
2.3.1 and the conflict script from lesson 2.3.4. Expect this project to take four to six
hours.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. **Write the memo.** One page. Not one page of small type: roughly five hundred words.
   Structure it so a reader who stops after the first paragraph knows what you recommend and
   why. It must contain:
   - The recommendation, in the first two sentences, with the one strongest reason.
   - The context: what the feature is, who asked for it, what problem they believe it
     solves.
   - The evidence: at least three concrete points, with numbers wherever you can get or
     estimate them. Usage data, support volume, user interview findings, cost of building,
     cost of maintaining, what it displaces on the roadmap.
   - The counter-argument, stated as well as its best advocate would state it. If a reader
     cannot tell from this paragraph that you understand why they want it, the memo fails.
   - What you propose instead: a smaller version, a different solution to the same problem,
     or a date to revisit with a condition attached.
   - What you need from the reader: a decision, by when.
2. **Write the conversation plan.** Half a page, separate from the memo. The person who asked
   for the feature will read the memo and then talk to you. Write: what you will say first,
   the two objections you expect and your response to each, and the point at which you would
   disagree and commit if overruled.
3. **Run the writing routine.** Ugly draft, find the answer, cut, read aloud, one reader. Give
   the memo to someone and ask only what they took from it. If their answer does not match
   your recommendation, revise and try again. Note in your submission how many rounds it
   took.
4. **Get it critiqued.** Post the memo in the project thread and ask reviewers one specific
   question: is the counter-argument stated fairly? Revise once based on what comes back.
5. **Submit.** Put the memo, the conversation plan and a short note on the revision rounds in
   a folder called `2-3-5-the-hard-memo` in your work repository. Post the link in the
   project's Discussions thread using the template from lesson 0.3, and review two other
   memos. In your reviews, grade the counter-argument first.

#### Extra credit

- Write the reply you would send if the person who asked for the feature responded with
  "build it anyway". Two paragraphs, in the voice of someone committing fully.
- Record yourself presenting the memo's recommendation in under ninety seconds, as if at the
  start of a meeting. Listen back. Note where you hedged.
