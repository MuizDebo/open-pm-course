## Introduction

Product management is a job made of disagreements. Not because PMs are difficult, but
because the role sits where every function's priorities meet, and the priorities do not
agree. Engineering wants to pay down debt. Sales wants the feature the big customer asked
for. Design wants another round. Leadership wants it by the quarter end. Each of them is
right from where they stand, and you are the person who has to say no to most of them.

Many new PMs handle this by avoiding it: agreeing to everything, hoping it works out, and
discovering at the end of the quarter that it did not. Others handle it by fighting and
winning, once or twice, before the team stops telling them things. This lesson is the third
way: having the disagreement properly.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why conflict is a feature of the role, not a sign of failure.
- The three conflicts every PM meets, and what each is really about.
- How to disagree well: interests over positions, questions over assertions.
- Disagree and commit, and when it applies.
- What to do when you lose.

## Conflict is the job working

A team with no disagreements is a team where someone has stopped talking. The engineer who
says "that will take three months, not three weeks" is giving you information. The sales lead
who says "we will lose the deal without this" is giving you information. The conflict is the
information arriving. Your job is not to make it stop. Your job is to get the information
out, weigh it, decide, and keep everyone in the room for the next one.

That reframe matters because it changes how you walk into the conversation. If conflict
means something has gone wrong, you walk in defensive. If conflict means the system is
surfacing what it is supposed to surface, you walk in curious.

## The three conflicts

**Engineering says no.** Usually one of three things underneath: the estimate is much larger
than you think, the approach you implied is wrong, or the team is carrying debt or burnout
you have not seen. Each has a different answer. Ask which it is before arguing the point.
"Help me understand what makes this three months" gets you further than "it can't be that
hard". Often the real answer is a smaller version that takes three weeks, and the engineer
knew that before you walked in.

**Sales already promised it.** A customer was told a feature exists or is coming, and now it
has to. Underneath: a deal with a date, a salesperson under quota pressure, and a gap in how
the roadmap is communicated. The feature question is secondary. The first conversation is
about the deal, honestly: what was promised, to whom, by when, what happens if it slips. The
second is about how promises get made, and that one is with the sales lead, calmly, after
the deal is handled. Never fight this one in front of the customer.

**Leadership changed its mind.** The strategy you planned the quarter around has moved.
Underneath: new information you may not have, a board or market pressure, or sometimes just
a bad week. Ask what changed. If there is a real reason, you need it to re-plan. If there is
not, naming the cost of the change, in plain numbers, is the most useful thing you can do.
"That moves the launch by six weeks and drops the retention work entirely. Is that the
trade-off you want?" Sometimes the answer is yes and you commit. Sometimes the question is
enough to reverse it.

## How to disagree well

A few habits separate PMs who are trusted in a disagreement from PMs who are merely argued
with.

**Interests, not positions.** A position is "we need this feature". An interest is "I need to
close this deal" or "I need the system not to fall over in December". Positions collide.
Interests can usually both be served, if you find them. The negotiation literature in the
assignment is built on this one idea.

**Questions before assertions.** "What would have to be true for this to work?" "What are you
most worried about?" "If we did the small version, what would we lose?" A question gets you
their reasoning. An assertion gets you their defence.

**Argue the argument.** Address the strongest version of what the other person said, not the
weakest. Paul Graham's hierarchy of disagreement, in the assignment, is a quick way to
notice when you have slipped from the point to the person.

**Say the trade-off out loud.** Most conflicts are two people each holding one half of a
trade-off. Putting both halves on the table, in writing if it is serious, moves the
conversation from "who wins" to "which do we want".

**Keep it in the open.** Side conversations and escalations behind someone's back win the
battle and end the relationship. If you need to escalate, say so to the person first.

## Disagree and commit

Sometimes the decision goes against you and the decision is final. The phrase for what comes
next is "disagree and commit": you say your piece, you are overruled, and you then work for
the decision as if it were your own. Not grudgingly, not with "I told you so" held in
reserve. Fully.

This is harder than it sounds and it is the mark of a senior PM. It is also what you need
from your team when the decision goes your way. A team that watches you commit to decisions
you argued against will do the same for you.

Two cautions. Disagree and commit applies to decisions that are reversible or whose cost of
being wrong is bearable. If you believe a decision is both irreversible and seriously wrong,
that is an escalation, and you make it openly and once. And commitment does not mean
silence about results. If the thing you disagreed with fails, you say so, with data,
without blame, so the next decision is better.

## When you lose

You will lose arguments you should have won. The feature gets built. The debt gets ignored.
The date gets set. What matters afterwards:

- **Write down what you argued and why**, dated, privately. Not to say "I told you so" later,
  but so that when the outcome is known you can learn whether your judgement was right. This
  is what the decision journal from lesson 2.1.5 is for.
- **Help the thing you argued against succeed.** Its failure is not a vindication; it is a
  cost to the product you are responsible for.
- **Notice patterns.** Losing the same argument three times means you are either making it
  badly or making it to the wrong person. Both are fixable.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Paul Graham's [How to Disagree](http://paulgraham.com/disagree.html), ten minutes.
   The seven-level hierarchy from name-calling to refuting the central point. Use it to grade
   your own last three disagreements, honestly.
2. Watch William Ury's TED talk
   [The walk from "no" to "yes"](https://www.ted.com/talks/william_ury_the_walk_from_no_to_yes),
   twenty minutes. Ury co-wrote the standard text on interest-based negotiation; this is the
   short version, and the idea of going "to the balcony" when a conversation heats up is one
   you will use weekly.
3. Read the Harvard Business Review piece
   [How to Handle Difficult Conversations at Work](https://hbr.org/2015/01/how-to-handle-difficult-conversations-at-work),
   ten minutes, for a practical sequence you can run before and during a hard conversation.
4. Pick one of the three conflicts above that you have lived or can imagine vividly. Write
   the conversation as a script: what they say, what you say, using interests, questions and
   the trade-off said aloud. Then write the version where you lose, and what you do next. Two
   pages at most. Keep it; the section project draws on it.

</div>

## Additional resources

- [Getting to Yes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getting_to_Yes), Fisher and Ury. The source
  of "interests, not positions" and still the clearest book on negotiating with people you
  have to keep working with.
- [Crucial Conversations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucial_Conversations), Patterson and
  others. A step-by-step method for high-stakes, high-emotion conversations. More structured
  than most PMs need, but the chapter on making it safe to talk is worth the book.
- The Wikipedia entry on [Disagree and commit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disagree_and_commit)
  traces the phrase through Intel and Amazon and is a useful five-minute history for when
  someone uses it at you.
