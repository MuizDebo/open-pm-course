## Introduction

A product manager gives feedback all day, mostly without calling it that. Reacting to a
design. Commenting on a pull request's description. Telling a stakeholder their request will
not make the quarter. And a PM receives feedback all day too, on specs, on priorities, on
the roadmap, from people who are often blunter than they would be with a peer in their own
discipline, because the PM has no formal authority to be careful of.

Both directions are skills. Both can be learned. Most people are mediocre at the first and
bad at the second, and the second matters more for your career than almost anything else in
this course. This lesson covers the craft of giving useful feedback, the particular case of
design critique, and then the hard part.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What makes feedback useful: specific, about the work, tied to the goal.
- A simple structure for giving it.
- Design critique: how to react to a design without wrecking it or the designer.
- One-to-ones as the place feedback actually lands.
- Receiving feedback: the reaction to control and the questions to ask.

## Feedback that is useful

Useful feedback has three properties. It is **specific**: not "this feels off" but "the
second step asks for a card number before the user knows the price". It is **about the work
and its effect**, not the person: "this flow will lose users at step two" rather than "you
did not think about the user". And it is **tied to the goal**: feedback on a design that
ignores what the design was trying to do is just taste.

A structure that keeps all three in place: observation, effect, question. "I noticed the
price only appears on the last screen. I think that will make people abandon before they get
there. What was the reasoning for putting it last?" The question matters. It assumes there
was a reason, which there usually was, and it opens a conversation instead of a defence.

The failure modes are the obvious ones. Vague praise ("looks great") teaches nothing. Vague
criticism ("not sure about this") creates anxiety and no action. Prescriptions ("make the
button blue") skip the problem and jump to your solution, which is rarely the best one and
always undermines the person whose job the solution is.

## Design critique

Critique is where PMs do the most damage, because it is where the gap between "I have an
opinion" and "I have the expertise" is widest. Some rules that designers will thank you for:

- **Restate the goal first.** "This is meant to get a first-time user to their first payment
  in under a minute, right?" Now everyone is critiquing against the same thing.
- **Ask what stage it is at.** Rough exploration wants big-picture reactions. A near-final
  design wants detail. Giving pixel feedback on a sketch wastes everyone's time.
- **Problems, not solutions.** You are the voice of the user and the business. Say what will
  go wrong for them. Let the designer solve it. If you have a solution idea, offer it last
  and lightly.
- **Separate must from could.** "This will fail with our older users" is a must. "I wonder
  if the illustration is too playful" is a could. Say which is which.
- **Say what works.** Specifically. Designers need to know what to keep as much as what to
  change, and a critique that is all problems makes the next round worse.

Structured critique formats exist and some teams use them. The format matters less than the
habit of critiquing the work against its goal, in the open, regularly.

## One-to-ones

The one-to-one is where feedback actually lands, in both directions, because it is private
and recurring. A PM has one-to-ones with their manager, often with their engineering and
design counterparts, and sometimes with stakeholders. Some things that make them work:

- **It is their meeting.** Your counterpart sets the agenda. If they have nothing, you have
  questions ready, and the questions are about them, not about the project.
- **Ask for feedback before you give it.** "What is one thing I could do differently that
  would make your week easier?" Then listen. Then do it.
- **Give feedback close to the event.** A concern from three weeks ago is history. A concern
  from Tuesday is useful.
- **Write it down.** Both what you heard and what you committed to. The next one-to-one
  starts with those.

Over months, one-to-ones build the trust that makes everything else in this course possible.
A spec from someone who has asked you how you are doing every week reads differently from
one that arrived cold.

## Receiving feedback

This is the hard part, and it is the one that decides whether you become a good PM.

You will get feedback on your work constantly, some of it wrong, some of it rude, most of it
mixed in with the other person's frustrations. The reflex is to defend, to explain, to find
the flaw in their reasoning so you can set it aside. That reflex is natural and it will cost
you more than any mistake in the work.

What to do instead, in the moment:

1. **Say thank you, and mean it.** Not because the feedback is right but because it was
   given. People stop giving feedback to those who argue with it.
2. **Ask one clarifying question.** "Can you give me an example?" Most feedback is a
   conclusion; the example is where the information is.
3. **Do not decide in the room.** "Let me think about that" is a complete sentence. You can
   agree, disagree or partly agree tomorrow, after the first reaction has passed.
4. **Separate the three questions.** Is this true? Is it important? What am I going to do
   about it? They have different answers, and the second is yours to judge.
5. **Come back.** Tell the person what you are changing and what you are not, and why. That
   closes the loop and shows that giving you feedback is worth their time.

The feeling of being criticised does not go away with experience. The gap between the feeling
and the response does. That gap is the skill.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Watch LeeAnn Renninger's TED talk
   [The secret to giving great feedback](https://www.ted.com/talks/leeann_renninger_the_secret_to_giving_great_feedback),
   ten minutes. A four-part formula close to the observation, effect, question structure
   above, with the research behind why it works.
2. Read Lara Hogan's [Feedback equation](https://larahogan.me/blog/feedback-equation/), ten
   minutes, for a tighter version of the same idea and worked examples that sound like
   things a person would actually say.
3. Read Nielsen Norman Group's [Design Critiques](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-critiques/),
   fifteen minutes. The clearest short guide to running and taking part in a critique, from
   the user-research side rather than the design side, which is where a PM sits.
4. Give one piece of feedback this week using observation, effect, question, on a design, a
   document or a plan. Then ask one person for feedback on something you made, and practise
   the five steps above. Write a paragraph on each: what you said, what happened, what you
   would do differently.

</div>

## Additional resources

- Lara Hogan's [Questions for our first 1:1](https://larahogan.me/blog/first-one-on-one-questions/).
  A list to open a new working relationship with, usable with managers, engineers and
  designers alike.
- [Radical Candor](https://www.radicalcandor.com/our-approach/), Kim Scott's framework. The
  two-axis model of caring personally and challenging directly is a useful check on which
  quadrant your feedback tends to land in.
- [Thanks for the Feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanks_for_the_Feedback) by Douglas
  Stone and Sheila Heen. The best full-length treatment of the receiving side, including why
  feedback from someone you do not respect is still worth sorting through.
