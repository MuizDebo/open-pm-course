## Introduction

Ask a product manager what they made last week and they will point at the team's work: the
feature, the launch, the experiment. Ask what they themselves produced and the honest answer
is documents. A one-pager that got a project funded. A spec the engineers built from. An
update that stopped leadership asking the same question for the third time. A decision memo
that will be read in a year by someone trying to work out why.

Writing is not a side skill for a PM. It is the job's main output, and it is also the job's
main quality check. A decision you cannot write down clearly is a decision you have not
finished making. This lesson is about writing as a working tool: what the documents are,
what makes them good, and how to get better fast.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why writing is the PM's primary output and primary thinking tool.
- The four documents you will write most: one-pager, spec, update, decision memo.
- What makes a document good: a reader, a purpose, an answer, a length.
- The narrative memo and why some companies ban slides.
- A short routine for writing badly and then well.

## Writing is thinking you can inspect

Talking is forgiving. You can gesture, back up, read the room, say "you know what I mean".
Writing is not. The moment you try to put a decision in a paragraph, every gap shows: the
assumption you never tested, the two options you conflated, the metric you cannot actually
name. That is why the act of writing feels harder than the thinking seemed. It is not
harder. It is the first time the thinking has been checked.

This is also why your writing is the thing people judge you on. Engineers decide whether to
trust your spec by whether it is clear. Leadership decides whether you understand the
problem by whether your memo does. A reader can forgive a wrong idea; they rarely forgive a
muddled one, because muddle means they have to do the thinking you were paid to do.

## The four documents

**The one-pager.** A problem, who has it, evidence, a proposed direction, what it would take,
what you want from the reader. One page is a discipline, not a limit. If it needs two, the
thinking is not done.

**The spec.** What is being built, for whom, what success looks like, what is out of scope,
open questions. Course 5 covers it in depth. Its reader is an engineer who wants to build the
right thing without a meeting.

**The update.** Where things are, what changed, what is blocked, what you need. Weekly or
per milestone. Its reader is busy and skims. Put the headline in the first line and the asks
in a list, so a reader who stops after ten seconds still knows the state.

**The decision memo.** The options, the trade-offs, the recommendation, the reasoning. Its
reader may be you, a year from now, or the person who inherits the product. Write it so that
someone who disagrees can see exactly where they disagree.

Everything else, from roadmap narratives to launch announcements, is a variant of one of
these four.

## What makes a document good

Four questions, in order. If you cannot answer one, stop writing until you can.

1. **Who is reading this?** One reader, named, with a job. A spec for engineers reads
   differently from a one-pager for a finance director. "Everyone" is not a reader.
2. **What do I want them to do?** Approve, build, decide, stop asking. Say it in the first
   paragraph, not the last.
3. **What is the answer?** The recommendation goes at the top. Reasoning follows. Readers
   who want the reasoning will read on; readers who trust you will not need to. A document
   that makes the reader hunt for the point is a document that will be skimmed and
   misunderstood.
4. **How short can it be?** Cut until removing the next sentence would lose meaning. Short
   sentences. Plain words. One idea per paragraph. Headings a skimmer can navigate by.

Clarity is not a style. It is respect for the reader's time, and the reader can tell.

## The narrative memo

Some companies, Amazon most famously, run meetings on written memos instead of slides.
Everyone reads the document in silence for the first part of the meeting, then discusses it.
The reasoning is the whole of this lesson: slides let a presenter hide gaps behind delivery,
while a narrative memo has to carry its own logic. Writing six pages of connected prose about
a proposal forces the author to actually have the proposal.

You do not need your company to adopt the practice to benefit from it. Write the memo
anyway, even if you then present slides. The memo is for you, and for the three people who
will read it closely and find the hole before the meeting does.

## A routine that works

Writing well on the first pass is rare. A routine most good writers use, in some form:

1. **Write the ugly version fast.** Everything you know, in whatever order, no editing. Half
   an hour. It will be bad. That is the point.
2. **Find the answer.** Read it back and look for the sentence that is actually the point.
   Move it to the top.
3. **Cut.** Remove everything that does not support that sentence. Expect to lose a third.
4. **Read it aloud.** Anywhere you stumble, the reader will too. Rewrite that sentence.
5. **Give it to one person** who is like the intended reader. Ask only: what did you take
   from this? If their answer is not your answer, the document is not done.

Twenty minutes of steps two to five turns most ugly versions into something a tired
engineer can act on. That is the bar.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Paul Graham's [Write Simply](http://paulgraham.com/simply.html), five minutes, then
   his [Putting Ideas into Words](http://paulgraham.com/words.html), ten minutes. The first
   is the case for plain language. The second is the argument this lesson rests on: that
   writing is how you find out what you actually think.
2. Watch Larry McEnerney's lecture
   [The Craft of Writing Effectively](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vtIzMaLkCaM), one
   hour twenty minutes, or at least the first thirty. It is aimed at academics and it is the
   best thing on the internet about writing for a reader who has the power to ignore you,
   which describes every reader a PM has.
3. Read Brad Porter's
   [The Beauty of Amazon's 6-Pager](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/beauty-amazons-6-pager-brad-porter/),
   ten minutes, for an insider's account of the narrative-memo practice and why it survives
   contact with real meetings.
4. Take the memo you wrote in lesson 1.1.4's one-hour test and run it through the five-step
   routine above. Keep both versions side by side. Write three sentences on what changed and
   why. You will reuse this in the section project.

</div>

## Additional resources

- Julian Shapiro's [writing guide](https://www.julian.com/guide/write/intro). A practical,
  example-heavy handbook on drafting, cutting and making a reader keep going. Longer than
  one sitting; worth returning to.
- George Orwell's
  [Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/).
  Written in 1946 about political prose and still the sharpest list of rules for not writing
  like a committee. Twenty minutes.
- [Working Backwards](https://www.workingbackwards.com/), the book by two former Amazon
  executives, has a full chapter on the six-page narrative and the press-release-first
  method you will meet again in Course 5.
