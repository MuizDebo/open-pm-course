## Introduction

At some point, probably in your first six months and again every time you change level or
company, you will sit in a meeting full of people who know more than you about engineering,
design, data, sales and the customer, and you will think: I have no business being the one
who decides. Everyone in that room could do a piece of your job better than you. What you
are for, you will struggle to say.

That feeling has a name, it is common, and in product management it is close to
structural. This lesson explains why the role produces it, how to tell it apart from the
useful signal of actually being out of your depth, and what working PMs do about it. It will
not go away. It can stop running you.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What imposter syndrome is and what it is not.
- Four reasons the PM role manufactures it.
- Telling the feeling apart from a real gap in skill.
- Five habits that reduce it, and one that makes it worse.
- When to take it seriously as a sign to change something.

## What it is

The term comes from a 1978 study of high-achieving women who could not internalise their
own success, attributing it to luck, timing or having fooled people. It has since been found
everywhere, across genders, levels and fields. The pattern: you have evidence of competence,
you discount the evidence, you expect exposure, and you work harder to prevent it, which
produces more evidence, which you discount.

Two things it is not. It is not humility, which is accurate about what you know and do not.
And it is not a diagnosis; it is a pattern of thought that most capable people have at least
sometimes. The research on it is in the assignment. Read it so that you are reacting to what
is known rather than to the phrase.

## Why the role manufactures it

Most jobs give you a craft you can get visibly better at. Product management gives you a
room full of craftspeople and asks you to make the call. Four features of the job turn a
normal amount of doubt into a constant hum.

**You are the generalist among specialists.** Every person on the team is deeper than you in
their area. That is the design, not a failure, but it means every technical, design or data
conversation contains a moment where you know less than the person you are talking to.
Lesson 1.1.1 covered why the generalist is still necessary. Knowing that does not stop the
feeling.

**Your output is invisible.** An engineer ships code. A designer ships screens. You ship a
decision, a document, an alignment. When the product succeeds, the credit visibly belongs to
the people who built it. When it fails, lesson 2.1.1 already told you whose name is on it.

**Feedback is delayed and noisy.** You find out whether a decision was good months later,
and even then the signal is mixed with a hundred other factors. You can go a year without
clean evidence that you are good at this.

**The job description is vague.** Lesson 1.1.2 showed that the role means different things
in different companies. If you cannot say exactly what the job is, you cannot know you are
doing it well, and the gap gets filled with doubt.

## Feeling versus signal

Imposter syndrome is a problem partly because it hides a real signal. Sometimes you are
out of your depth, and that is useful information. The difference is checkable.

Ask three questions:

1. **Can I name the specific thing I cannot do?** "I can't read a SQL query" is a gap. "I'm
   not really a PM" is a feeling.
2. **Would I accept this evidence about someone else?** If a colleague shipped what you
   shipped and got the feedback you got, would you think they were a fraud?
3. **What would a person who was actually failing be hearing?** Missed deadlines, lost
   trust, direct feedback, being routed around. If none of that is happening, the feeling is
   not reporting on reality.

If question one gives you a specific gap, close it. That is just learning. If the other two
come back "no" and "nothing", you are looking at the feeling, and the rest of this lesson
applies.

## What helps

From a lot of PMs who have been through it, roughly in order of usefulness:

**Keep a record.** A running document of decisions you made, what happened, and what you
learned. The decision journal from project 2.1.5 is this. The point is not to collect wins.
It is to have evidence you cannot discount in the moment, in your own handwriting.

**Say it out loud to one person.** Most PMs who admit the feeling to a peer discover the
peer has it too. That does not fix it, but it recategorises it from "my secret" to "a known
feature of the job", which is most of the relief.

**Separate judgement from knowledge.** You are not in the room because you know the most.
You are there because someone has to weigh what everyone knows and choose. Get good at that
and the gap in knowledge stops being the point. Lesson 2.2.4 on judgement and taste is the
long version.

**Ask the question you are afraid reveals you.** "What does that term mean?" said plainly
by a PM does two things: it gets you the answer, and it gives everyone else in the room
permission to be honest about what they do not know. PMs who never ask are the ones the
team quietly stops trusting.

**Pick a thing to be properly good at.** Writing, user interviews, metrics, a domain. One
area where you are the person others come to. It will not cover the whole job, but it gives
the feeling somewhere it cannot reach.

The habit that makes it worse: overworking to prevent exposure. It feels like the solution
and it is the mechanism. The extra hours produce more results you cannot take credit for and
less time to notice that. Lesson 2.4.2 is about where that road ends.

## When to take it seriously

Sometimes the feeling is right about something, just not about you. If every meeting leaves
you feeling fraudulent, check whether the role is one the company actually supports. A PM
with no authority, no access to customers and no say in the roadmap will feel like an
imposter because, in that job, nobody could do what the title implies. That is a signal to
change jobs, not to work harder. Lesson 1.1.4 listed it as the second most common reason
people leave.

## Assignment

1. Read the Wikipedia article on [Impostor syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impostor_syndrome),
   fifteen minutes. It is a fair summary of the research, including the criticism of the
   concept. You want the real shape of it, not the version from a motivational post.
2. Watch the TED-Ed short
   [What is imposter syndrome and how can you combat it?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZQUxL4Jm1Lo),
   four minutes. A clear animation of the discounting loop, with the two moves that break
   it: naming it and hearing others name it.
3. Read Ruchika Tulshyan and Jodi-Ann Burey's
   [Stop Telling Women They Have Imposter Syndrome](https://hbr.org/2021/02/stop-telling-women-they-have-imposter-syndrome)
   in Harvard Business Review, ten minutes. The strongest argument that the feeling is often
   an accurate reading of an environment, not a defect in the person. Keep that in mind when
   you reach the "when to take it seriously" section above.
4. Run the three questions from "Feeling versus signal" on yourself, in writing, about your
   current job or the last one. Add the result to your decision journal. If question one
   produced a specific gap, write the one thing you will do about it this month.

## Additional resources

- Carol Dweck's TED talk
  [The power of believing that you can improve](https://www.ted.com/talks/carol_dweck_the_power_of_believing_that_you_can_improve),
  ten minutes. Growth mindset is overused as a phrase, but the underlying idea, that ability
  is built rather than revealed, is the direct antidote to "I was never really a PM".
