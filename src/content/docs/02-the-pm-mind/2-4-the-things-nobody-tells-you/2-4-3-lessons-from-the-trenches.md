## Introduction

:::caution[Draft]
This lesson is waiting on the author's own stories. The frame below shows what it will hold.
Each marked section is a placeholder for a real episode from the author's product work,
told in the first person. Nothing in those sections will be invented or ghost-written.
:::

Every other lesson in this course is written in the voice of the course: measured, sourced,
in the second person. This one is different on purpose. It is written in the first person,
by the person who built this course, about things that went wrong in their own product
work. Names are changed. The mistakes are not.

Why it belongs here. Most of what you have read about product management, in this course
and everywhere else, is written after the fact by people who now know the answer. The
frameworks are clean because the failures that produced them have been edited out. But the
job is lived forwards, with partial information and people who disagree with you, and the
only honest way to show that is to tell you about the times it went badly and what it cost.

Read this lesson as evidence, not as instruction. The point is not that you will face the
same situations. It is that when you face your own, you will recognise the shape of the
mistake a little earlier.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- A handful of real product episodes, told plainly, with the decision, the reasoning at the
  time, and what actually happened.
- The patterns underneath them, which earlier lessons in this course described in the
  abstract.
- How to find and learn from other people's product failures, which are far more available
  than most PMs realise.

## The stories

:::tip[Author's story goes here]
**A launch that failed.** Something that shipped on time, with a team that worked hard, and
did not do what it was supposed to for the people it was built for. What was the decision
that made it inevitable, when was it made, and what was known at the time? What did the
retrospective say, and what did it leave out?
:::

:::tip[Author's story goes here]
**A time I was overruled and was right.** The call went the other way, the result bore out
the original position, and the question is what to do with that. How was the disagreement
handled at the time? What would a better version of me have done differently in the room,
rather than afterwards?
:::

:::tip[Author's story goes here]
**A time I was overruled and was wrong.** The harder one to tell. The reasoning that felt
solid, the thing the other person saw that I did not, and how long it took to admit it.
Lesson 2.1.4 is about this in general; this is the specific.
:::

:::tip[Author's story goes here]
**The stakeholder I handled badly.** A relationship that went wrong because of something I
did or failed to do: a promise, an assumption, a conversation avoided. What it cost the
product, and what I now do differently with people like that.
:::

:::tip[Author's story goes here]
**The decision I would reverse.** If one call could be taken back, which one, why, and what
the honest reason for making it was at the time. Not the reason given in the document. The
real one.
:::

## What to take from these

The episodes above are specific to one person and a handful of companies. The patterns are
not. When you read them, you should be able to connect each one to something earlier in
this course:

- **The failed launch** is usually a discovery problem wearing a delivery costume. Course 3
  exists because of launches like that.
- **Being overruled** in both directions is the daily texture of accountability without
  authority. Lesson 2.1.1 described it; the stories show what it feels like.
- **The stakeholder handled badly** is almost always a communication failure that was
  visible weeks before it became a relationship failure. Section 2.3 is the toolkit.
- **The decision you would reverse** is the one where the stated reason and the real reason
  diverged. Lesson 2.2.2 on bias is about why that happens to everyone.

Your own list will look different. The habit to build is noticing, while it is happening,
which pattern you are in. The decision journal from project 2.1.5 is where that noticing
goes.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read the chapter on
   [postmortem culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/) from Google's Site
   Reliability Engineering book, twenty minutes. It is written for engineers about outages,
   and it is the best public description of how to write up a failure so that it teaches
   rather than blames. Product failures deserve the same treatment and almost never get it.
2. Read Tom Eisenmann's [Why Start-ups Fail](https://hbr.org/2021/05/why-start-ups-fail) in
   Harvard Business Review, twenty minutes. A Harvard Business School study of hundreds of
   failed companies. Note how many of the failure patterns are product decisions: false
   starts, false positives from early customers, the wrong first market.
3. Find one product failure story told in the first person by the person responsible. A
   founder's post-mortem, a PM on a podcast, a conference talk, a long thread. Write it up in
   your own words, no more than a page: what was decided, what was known at the time, what
   happened, and which pattern from this course it matches. Put it in your work repository
   under this lesson's number. These write-ups form a shared library in Discussions; post the
   link if you are willing.
4. Browse [Killed by Google](https://killedbygoogle.com/), ten minutes. Pick one product
   you remember using and try to work out, from public information, why it died. The
   exercise is in separating "the product was bad" from "the decision to build it was
   reasonable with what was known".

</div>
