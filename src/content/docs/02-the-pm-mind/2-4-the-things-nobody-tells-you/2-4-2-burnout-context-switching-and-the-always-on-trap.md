## Introduction

Lesson 1.1.4 warned you: the job arrives as interruptions, and shipping is the midpoint,
not the end. Put those together over a year and you get a person who is always a little
behind, always reachable, and never quite finished. That is not a character flaw. It is what
the role does by default, and it is the third most common reason PMs leave.

This lesson is about defending the two things the job spends fastest: your attention and
your recovery. It explains how context switching actually costs you, how burnout arrives
(slowly, then all at once), and a short set of habits that working PMs use to stay in the
job for a decade rather than three years.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What context switching costs, and why PMs pay it more than anyone.
- The maker's schedule and the manager's schedule, and why you need both.
- How burnout is defined and how it shows up in a PM.
- Six habits that protect attention and recovery.
- What to do when the habits are not enough.

## The cost of switching

Every time you move from one task to another, you pay twice: once to put down what you were
holding, and again to pick up the context of the new thing. Research on interruptions puts
the recovery time for a complex task at twenty minutes or more, and finds that people who
are interrupted often compensate by working faster and feeling more stressed, with more
mistakes. The assignment has the source.

A PM's day is built from switches. A standup, a message from sales, a design review, a
customer call, a spec, a question from an engineer, a planning document, another message. By
mid-afternoon you have started a dozen things and finished none, and the one piece of
thinking the team needed from you, the decision, has not happened.

The cost is not just the lost minutes. It is that the hardest work of the job, judgement
under uncertainty, is exactly the kind of work that interruption destroys. You can answer
messages in a fragmented day. You cannot decide well in one.

## Two schedules

Paul Graham's distinction between the maker's schedule and the manager's schedule is the
most useful frame for this. Makers need long, unbroken blocks; a single meeting in the
middle of an afternoon costs them the afternoon. Managers run on hour-long units and can
switch freely. The problem for a PM is that the job needs both: manager hours for the
coordination, maker hours for the thinking and writing.

Most PMs let the manager schedule win, because meetings arrive with calendar invitations and
thinking does not. The fix is to treat thinking like a meeting. Block it, name it, protect
it, and decline things that collide with it. Lesson 0.2's planner did this for your study
time. Do it for your work.

## How burnout arrives

The World Health Organization describes burnout as chronic workplace stress that has not
been managed, with three features: exhaustion, growing mental distance or cynicism about
the job, and reduced effectiveness. All three are easy to miss in a PM because each looks
like something else.

Exhaustion looks like "it's a busy quarter". Cynicism looks like "I've learned how this
company really works". Reduced effectiveness looks like "I'm being more strategic now",
when what has happened is that you stopped doing the parts of the job that need energy:
the customer calls, the hard conversations, the careful writing.

The pattern in PMs specifically:

1. The always-on loop starts. Messages at night, "just checking" on weekends, the laptop
   open during dinner. It feels like diligence.
2. Thinking time disappears first, because it has no owner. Decisions get made in meetings,
   reactively, with less evidence.
3. The quality of the work drops, which produces more problems, which produce more messages.
4. The person notices they do not care about the product any more, and is frightened by
   that, and works harder to compensate.
5. Something gives: health, a relationship, a resignation written at 2am.

It takes months. The early signs are in step one, and step one is the one everyone praises.

## Six habits

None of these is original. All of them are used by PMs who have stayed in the job.

**Two maker blocks a week, minimum.** Ninety minutes or more, in the calendar, with
notifications off. Writing, analysis, decisions. Treat them as the most important meetings
you have, because they are.

**A hard stop.** A time after which you do not read work messages, and a reason you can say
out loud. The reason matters less than the line. Teams adapt to a PM with a boundary in
about two weeks. They never adapt to one without.

**Batch the inbox.** Messages and email at fixed times, three or four a day, rather than
continuously. Tell the team the times. Genuine emergencies have phones.

**One list, reviewed once a day.** Not a system, a list. The job generates more tasks than
you can do; the list is where you decide which ones you will not do, and that decision is
the real work. Lesson 2.1.3 on saying no applies to your own list as much as the roadmap.

**Close the loop on finished things.** Because shipping is the midpoint, you rarely get the
feeling of being done. Make it. Write a two-line note when something launches, tell the team,
and move the item off the list. Small, artificial closure beats none.

**Take the leave.** PMs are the worst in the company at this, because there is always a
launch. There will always be a launch. The team ships without you for two weeks, and if it
cannot, that is a different problem worth knowing about.

## When habits are not enough

If you recognised yourself in steps three to five above, habits will not fix it, and
reading this lesson is not the same as doing something. Three moves, in order:

1. **Say it to your manager, plainly.** "I am burning out and I need to change how this job
   works." Most managers would rather hear it than lose you. The ones who would not are
   telling you something.
2. **Change the job, not just the hours.** Fewer products, a narrower remit, a team that is
   staffed. The always-on loop is often a symptom of a role that is two roles.
3. **Get help that is not a productivity book.** A doctor, a therapist, a coach. Burnout has
   a physical component and the habits above do not treat it.

Course 8 covers how to evaluate a role before you take it, including the questions that
reveal whether the always-on loop is the culture or the exception.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Paul Graham's [Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule](http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html),
   ten minutes. The clearest statement of why a single meeting can cost an afternoon and
   why the two kinds of schedule need to be kept apart.
2. Read the American Psychological Association's summary
   [Multitasking: Switching costs](https://www.apa.org/topics/research/multitasking),
   ten minutes. The research on what task switching costs, in one page, so you can quote it
   when you need to defend a maker block.
3. Read the World Health Organization's note on
   [Burn-out as an occupational phenomenon](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases),
   five minutes. The three-part definition is what you check yourself against, and it is
   deliberately about the workplace, not the person.
4. Read Cal Newport's
   [The importance of planning every minute of your work day](https://calnewport.com/deep-habits-the-importance-of-planning-every-minute-of-your-work-day/),
   ten minutes. Time-block planning is the single habit most working PMs who protect their
   attention have in common. You do not need to adopt it fully; you need to see what it looks
   like.
5. Look at your calendar for the coming week. Add two maker blocks of ninety minutes and a
   hard stop. Then write down, in your decision journal, which of the five burnout steps
   above you are currently at. Be honest; nobody else reads it.
