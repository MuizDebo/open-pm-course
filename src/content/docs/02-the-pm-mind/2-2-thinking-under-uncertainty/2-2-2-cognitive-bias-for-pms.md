## Introduction

There are more than a hundred named cognitive biases. You do not need them all. In product
work, four of them account for most of the bad decisions you will sit through: sunk cost,
confirmation, survivorship, and deference to the highest-paid person's opinion. Each has a
recognisable shape in a meeting, and each has a counter-move that works without making
anyone feel accused.

The aim of this lesson is not to make you a neutral observer of human folly. You have the
same biases. The aim is to help you notice them in real time, in yourself first, and to
have something practical to do about it.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why knowing about biases does not remove them, and what does help.
- Sunk cost: the feature that is too expensive to kill.
- Confirmation: the research that found what we hoped.
- Survivorship: the case study that only shows the winners.
- HiPPO: the opinion that ends the discussion.
- A short pre-decision checklist you can run in any meeting.

## Knowing is not enough

The uncomfortable finding from decades of research is that being told about a bias does
little to reduce it. You will read this lesson, agree with it, and anchor on the first
estimate in your next planning meeting anyway. What does help is structure: a question
asked every time, a step that cannot be skipped, a person whose job is to argue the other
side. The counters below are all structural for that reason. Do not rely on noticing. Rely
on the habit.

## Sunk cost

**What it looks like.** "We've already put three months into this, we can't stop now." The
feature is late, the evidence has turned, and the argument for continuing is the amount
already spent. Sometimes it is dressed up: "the team's morale would suffer", "we've told
customers", "it's nearly done". Those can be real costs, but they are future costs and
should be weighed as such. The past spend is gone either way.

**The counter.** Ask the zero-base question: if we were starting today, knowing what we
know now, with the time and people we have left, would we choose to build this? If the
answer is no, the rest is grief, and grief is allowed, but it is not a plan. Make the
question a standing item in any review of work that is behind.

## Confirmation

**What it looks like.** You believe users want a feature. You run five interviews. Four of
them mention something adjacent and you hear agreement. The fifth says they would not use
it and you decide they are not the target user. You report that research confirmed the
need. Nobody lied. The evidence was filtered on the way in.

**The counter.** Decide what would change your mind before you look. Write down, before
the interviews or the data pull, what result would make you drop the idea. Then look for
that result specifically. In a meeting, appoint one person to argue that the evidence
points the other way, and listen to them as if they might be right, because sometimes they
are.

## Survivorship

**What it looks like.** "Company X did it this way and they're worth a billion dollars."
Every framework, playbook and case study you read is drawn from products that survived.
The ones that did the same thing and died are not writing blog posts. You end up copying
the visible habits of winners without knowing whether those habits caused the winning, or
whether a hundred losers had the same habits.

**The counter.** Ask what the failures looked like. For any practice you are about to copy,
try to name a team that did the same and did not succeed. If you cannot, treat the case
study as a story rather than evidence. The assignment has the aircraft example that made
this bias famous, and it is worth keeping in your head as a picture.

## HiPPO

**What it looks like.** The discussion is going well until the most senior person in the
room says what they think. Then it stops. Everyone adjusts their position toward theirs,
including you, and the decision is made with one person's judgement and six people's
silent doubts. The term stands for the highest-paid person's opinion, and it was coined by
an analytics leader who watched data lose to it over and over.

**The counter.** Get opinions before the senior person speaks. Ask for written positions
before the meeting, or go around the room junior-first. If you are the PM running the
meeting, you can do this politely: "Before we hear from Ada, I'd like to get everyone
else's read." If you are the senior person, speak last, and say explicitly that you want
to be disagreed with. Most HiPPOs do not know they are one.

## A checklist you can run in any meeting

Before a decision of consequence, run these four questions out loud. They take two
minutes and they target the four biases above, in order.

1. If we were starting from scratch today, would we do this?
2. What did we decide, in advance, would change our mind, and did we see it?
3. Who tried this and failed, and what was different about them?
4. Has everyone said what they think before the most senior person did?

You will not always like the answers. That is the point.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Watch Daniel Kahneman's talk
   [Thinking, Fast and Slow](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CjVQJdIrDJ0) at Google, one
   hour. The clearest account from the person who did the research of why fast, intuitive
   judgement is both necessary and unreliable. If you only have twenty minutes, watch the
   first third.
2. Read [Before You Make That Big Decision](https://hbr.org/2011/06/the-big-idea-before-you-make-that-big-decision)
   by Kahneman, Lovallo and Sibony, twenty-five minutes. A twelve-question checklist for
   reviewing a recommendation, built for executives and directly usable by a PM reviewing a
   proposal.
3. Read the Wikipedia article on [Survivorship bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias),
   ten minutes, for the Abraham Wald aircraft story and the examples from business.
4. Read Avinash Kaushik's
   [Seven Steps to Creating a Data Driven Decision Making Culture](https://www.kaushik.net/avinash/seven-steps-to-creating-a-data-driven-decision-making-culture/),
   fifteen minutes. The post that named the HiPPO, and a practical account of how to get
   opinions out of the way of evidence.
5. Pick a recent meeting where a decision was made. Write down which of the four biases
   was present, what it looked like, and what counter you could have used. Half a page.
   Add it to your decision journal.

</div>
