## Introduction

Most product decisions are made with less information than you want, under more time
pressure than you like, in a room where other people have opinions. That is the job, and it
is not going to change. What can change is how often you walk into the same traps: treating
a reversible choice as if it were final, treating a final choice as if it were reversible,
framing the question so only one answer looks sane, and anchoring on the first number
anyone said out loud.

This lesson gives you the short list of traps that catch product managers most, and two
questions to ask before any decision of consequence. Lesson 2.2.2 covers the biases
underneath; this one is about the shape of the decision itself.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Reversible and irreversible decisions, and why they deserve different speeds.
- The classic decision traps: anchoring, framing, status quo, sunk cost, overconfidence.
- Why high-stakes decisions in organisations fail for process reasons, not intelligence.
- Two questions to ask before deciding anything that matters.
- A simple habit, the decision journal, that makes you better over time.

## Two kinds of door

The most useful distinction in product decision-making is also the simplest. Some decisions
can be undone cheaply. Most can. Changing the copy on a button, running an experiment,
reordering a backlog, choosing which customer to interview next. If you are wrong, you walk
back through the door and try again. These deserve speed, not deliberation. A team that
agonises over them is spending its best thinking on choices that do not need it.

A few decisions cannot be undone, or only at great cost. Choosing a pricing model that
customers will anchor on. Committing to a platform. Shipping a data-collection practice.
Announcing a date in public. Killing a product line. These are one-way doors, and they
deserve the slow treatment: more evidence, more voices, a written case, a night's sleep.

The trap is treating one kind like the other. Teams that treat everything as a one-way door
move slowly and ship little. Teams that treat everything as a two-way door eventually walk
through a one-way one at a jog. Your first job on any decision is to say which kind it is,
out loud, and get agreement on that before you argue about the answer.

## The classic traps

A famous article on decision traps, in the assignment, lists the psychological traps that
show up in nearly every high-stakes decision. The ones that matter most to product work:

**Anchoring.** The first number, date or option mentioned sets the range for everything
after. Someone says "could we ship by March?" and every plan is now measured against March.
Counter it by generating your own estimate before you hear anyone else's, and by asking for
the range rather than the point.

**Framing.** The same choice described as a loss or as a gain gets different answers. "We
would lose 5 percent of users" and "we would keep 95 percent of users" are the same fact.
Counter it by restating every important choice both ways before deciding.

**Status quo.** Doing nothing feels safe because nobody gets blamed for it. It is still a
decision, with costs. Counter it by writing the do-nothing option into the list of options
and giving it the same scrutiny as the others.

**Sunk cost.** The three months already spent on a feature are not a reason to spend a
fourth. Counter it by asking: if we were starting today with what we know now, would we
start this? Lesson 2.2.2 goes deeper on this one.

**Overconfidence.** Estimates are tighter than the world justifies. Counter it by asking for
the case in which you are wrong, and what you would see first if you were.

## Why organisations decide badly

Individuals fall into the traps above. Organisations have their own failure mode: the
decision is never really made. It is discussed in a planning meeting, deferred to a
steering group, revisited by someone senior who missed the first meeting, and finally
settled by whoever is most persistent. Research on how companies make strategic decisions
finds that the quality of the process matters more than the quality of the analysis, and
most companies have no process at all.

For a product manager this means three things. Decide who decides before you start
arguing. Put the options and the reasoning in writing so the decision survives the meeting.
And set a date by which it will be made, because an undecided decision is a decision to
wait, usually for the worst reason, which is that nobody wanted to own it.

## Two questions before you decide

Before any decision that matters, ask these two, in this order:

1. **Which kind of door is this?** If it is reversible, decide fast, write down what would
   make you reverse it, and move. If it is not, slow down and do the next question
   properly.
2. **What would have to be true for the other option to be right?** This is the single
   most useful question in product work. It turns a fight about preferences into a search
   for evidence, and it tells you what to go and find out before you commit.

Neither question takes long. Both are skipped constantly.

## The decision journal

The way you get better at decisions is not by reading about them. It is by keeping a record
of the ones you make, what you expected, how confident you were, and what happened. Over
months, a pattern appears: the kinds of decision where your judgement is good and the kinds
where it is reliably off. Nobody else can tell you that. The record can.

The project at the end of section 2.1 asks you to keep one for two weeks. Keep it longer.
Working PMs who do this for a year describe it as the single most useful habit they have.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read [The Hidden Traps in Decision Making](https://hbr.org/1998/09/the-hidden-traps-in-decision-making-2)
   by Hammond, Keeney and Raiffa, twenty-five minutes. The original catalogue of the traps
   above, with the research behind each. Read it for the counters, which are practical.
2. Read Farnam Street's
   [Reversible and Irreversible Decisions](https://fs.blog/reversible-irreversible-decisions/),
   ten minutes. The one-way and two-way door idea in full, with the point that most of us
   treat far too many decisions as irreversible.
3. Read Jeff Bezos's
   [2016 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders),
   fifteen minutes, for the sections on high-velocity decision making: deciding with about
   seventy percent of the information, and "disagree and commit". This is where the door
   language comes from in practice.
4. Read [Stop Making Plans; Start Making Decisions](https://hbr.org/2006/01/stop-making-plans-start-making-decisions)
   by Mankins and Steele, twenty minutes. On why organisations fail to decide, and what a
   decision process that works looks like. Skim the corporate-strategy framing and read for
   the process.
5. Take a product decision you are facing or recently faced. Write down which kind of door
   it is, the do-nothing option, both framings, and what would have to be true for the
   option you dislike to be right. One page. Add it to your decision journal.

</div>
