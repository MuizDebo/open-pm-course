## Introduction

Ask a hiring manager what separates a good product manager from a great one and you will
hear the phrase "product sense", usually with a shrug, as if it were a talent you either
have or lack. It is not. Product sense is judgement about what makes a product good for a
particular person in a particular moment, and like any judgement it is built from
exposure, feedback and deliberate attention. Some people start with more. Everyone can
build it.

This lesson pins down what the phrase means, separates it from the things it is confused
with, and gives you a practice you can run every week for the rest of the course and your
career.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why taste lags ambition, and what to do about the gap.
- The weekly practices that build judgement.
- How interviewers test for it, and why the practice above is also interview prep.

## What product sense is

Product sense is the ability to predict, more often than chance, what will make a product
better for its users, and to explain why. It has two parts that get muddled together.

**Empathy** is understanding what the user is actually trying to do, what they feel while
doing it, and what they would never say in an interview. It is built by watching people use
things, including you, with attention.

**Taste** is judgement about solutions: which of three designs is right, which feature
belongs and which is noise, when a product is finished. It is built by looking at a great
many products closely and forming opinions about why some work and some do not.

A PM with empathy and no taste knows what users need and ships clumsy answers to it. A PM
with taste and no empathy ships beautiful things nobody asked for. You need both, and most
people are stronger at one, which is useful to know about yourself.

## What it is not

It is not the same as having ideas. Ideas are cheap and everyone has them. Product sense is
knowing which idea to pick.

It is not the same as being a designer. Designers go deep on the craft of the solution. A
PM needs enough taste to recognise good design, to brief for it, and to tell when something
is wrong, not to produce it.

It is not the same as data. Data tells you what happened. Product sense is what lets you
form the hypothesis worth testing, and read the result without fooling yourself.

And it is not a fixed quantity. The people who seem to have it in abundance have mostly
spent years looking at products on purpose. That is available to you.

## The gap

Early on, your taste is ahead of your ability. You can tell that a product is clumsy before
you can say why, and long before you could make it better. This gap is uncomfortable and it
is where most people stop: they decide they lack the gift and retreat to process and
tickets, where judgement is not required.

The gap is the signal that your taste is working. The only way through it is volume:
looking at more products, making more calls, being wrong and finding out. A well-known
talk on this is in the assignment. It was made for storytellers and it applies to product
work without a word changed.

## Practices that build judgement

None of these take long. All of them compound.

**The weekly teardown.** Pick one product a week, something you use or something new. Spend
thirty minutes on one flow. Write down what it is trying to do, three things it does well
and why, three things it does badly and why, and what you would change first. Keep them.
After six months you will have a notebook of opinions and the beginnings of a point of
view.

**Predict, then look.** Before you see a metric, a research result or a competitor's
release, write down what you expect. Then compare. The gap between your prediction and the
world is the most honest measure of your judgement you will ever get, and it narrows with
use.

**Steal from adjacent fields.** Good restaurants, good tools, good games, good forms. Taste
transfers. The products you admire outside software will teach you what you value in it.

**Ask better people why.** When someone with strong judgement makes a call you would not
have made, ask them to walk you through it. Not to argue. To hear how they think. Most are
happy to, and ten of those conversations are worth a year of reading.

**Make calls and own them.** Judgement is built by exercising it. Say which option you
prefer and why, in meetings, in reviews, in your decision journal. Being wrong in the open
is how it improves. Being silent keeps it where it is.

## Why this is also interview prep

Product-sense interviews ask you to design or improve a product on the spot, and most
candidates reach for a framework because they have nothing else. The ones who do well have
opinions, formed from a lot of looking, and a way of reasoning from the user to the
solution that they can show out loud. That is exactly what the weekly teardown builds.
Course 8 covers the interview format in detail. Everything in this lesson is the
preparation it assumes you have done.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Watch Ira Glass's short talk on [the gap between taste and skill](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X2wLP0izeJE),
   five minutes. The most honest description of what the early years of building judgement
   feel like, and why volume is the only way through.
2. Read Paul Graham's [Taste for Makers](https://paulgraham.com/taste.html), twenty-five
   minutes. The argument that good taste is real, learnable, and roughly the same across
   fields, with a list of what good design tends to have in common. Keep the list; it is a
   good lens for teardowns.
3. Read Jules Walter's
   [How to develop product sense](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-develop-product-sense),
   twenty minutes. A working PM's account of the two ingredients and the practices that
   build them, close to the ones in this lesson.
4. Do your first weekly teardown using the structure above. Thirty minutes, one product,
   one flow. Put it in your work repository in a folder called `teardowns`, and keep adding
   one a week for the rest of the course.

</div>
