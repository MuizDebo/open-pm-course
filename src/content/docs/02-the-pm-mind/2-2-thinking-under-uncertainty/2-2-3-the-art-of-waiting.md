## Introduction

Everything in product culture pushes you to act. Ship faster. Bias for action. Move fast.
Most of the time that is right, because most decisions are two-way doors and the cost of
waiting is higher than the cost of being wrong. But some of the best product decisions you
will ever make are decisions not to act yet, and nobody will congratulate you for them,
because nothing visibly happened.

This lesson is about that skill. Waiting on purpose is different from stalling, and the
difference is whether you know what you are waiting for. By the end you should be able to
tell the two apart in yourself, and to wait in a way that makes the eventual decision
better rather than just later.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why the last responsible moment is usually later than it feels.
- Waiting versus stalling: the one question that separates them.
- Four situations where not acting is the stronger move.
- How to wait well: what to do during the delay.
- The cost of waiting, honestly, and when it outweighs the benefit.

## The last responsible moment

There is a moment for every decision after which delaying starts to cost more than it
gains. Call it the last responsible moment. Before it, waiting buys you information: more
data, another customer conversation, a competitor's move, an engineer's spike. After it,
waiting costs you options: the window closes, the team idles, the customer leaves.

Most people decide well before the last responsible moment, because an open decision is
uncomfortable. A planning meeting wants an answer. A stakeholder wants a date. Your own
mind wants the question closed. So the decision is made early, with less information than
was available, to relieve the discomfort rather than to serve the product.

The skill is to find the real last responsible moment and hold the decision open until
close to it. Not past it. Close to it.

## Waiting versus stalling

The question that separates the two: **what, specifically, am I waiting for?**

If you can answer it, you are waiting. "I'm waiting for the retention data from the cohort
that started in March, which lands on the 14th." "I'm waiting to see whether the competitor
actually ships what they announced." "I'm waiting for the two interviews on Thursday,
because both are with the segment we are least sure about."

If you cannot answer it, or the answer is "for things to become clearer", you are stalling.
Clarity does not arrive on its own. Stalling feels like prudence from the inside and looks
like indecision from the outside, and it is the version of waiting that gets product
managers a reputation for being slow.

Write the answer down, with a date. Waiting for something specific, by a specific time, is
a decision. Waiting in general is the absence of one.

## When not acting is the stronger move

**When the next piece of evidence is close and cheap.** If a result lands next week that
would change your mind either way, deciding this week is throwing that information away.
This is the most common and the easiest case.

**When the problem might solve itself.** Some problems are caused by something that is
about to change anyway: a migration finishing, a seasonal pattern, a customer who is
churning regardless. Acting on them builds something for a world that is about to stop
existing. Ask whether the problem will still be there in a month before you spend a month
on it.

**When the ask is a reaction, not a need.** A customer is angry, a competitor made an
announcement, an executive read an article. The pressure to respond is immediate; the
underlying need may not survive the week. Acknowledge the reaction, log it, and look again
when the heat is off. A surprising share of urgent requests quietly disappear.

**When your own confidence is suspiciously high.** If you feel certain about something you
have not tested, that certainty is a signal to wait, not to act. Lesson 2.2.2 explained
why. The feeling of being obviously right is exactly how confirmation bias feels from the
inside.

## How to wait well

Waiting is not passive. While the decision is open:

- **Name the trigger.** What result, event or date ends the wait? Put it in writing where
  the people waiting on you can see it.
- **Do the cheap work.** Run the spike, draft the spec, line up the interviews. When the
  trigger fires you want to move the same day, not start.
- **Think second-order.** Ask not only what happens if you do X, but what happens after
  that, and after that. Waiting is when you have time for this and acting is when you do
  not.
- **Pre-decide.** Write down now what you will do for each possible outcome of the thing
  you are waiting for. This turns the eventual decision into a lookup and strips the
  emotion out of it.
- **Tell people.** "We are deciding this on the 14th when the cohort data lands" is a
  sentence that stops three follow-up messages and protects the wait from being mistaken
  for neglect.

## The cost of waiting

Waiting is never free, and this lesson is not a licence to delay. The costs are real: an
idle team, a customer who stops believing you, a competitor who moves, a window that
closes, and the slow erosion of trust that comes from being the person who never quite
decides. Every wait should be weighed against them, in the open.

The test is the same as everywhere in this section. If you can say what you are waiting
for, when it arrives, and what it would cost to decide now instead, you are making a
decision. If not, decide now. Most of the time, deciding now with seventy percent of the
information is the right call. This lesson is about recognising the other times.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Farnam Street's [Second-Order Thinking](https://fs.blog/second-order-thinking/),
   ten minutes. The habit of asking "and then what?" is the main thing to do while a
   decision is open, and this is the clearest short explanation of it.
2. Read Farnam Street's [Inversion](https://fs.blog/inversion/), ten minutes. Before
   deciding, ask what would guarantee failure and whether acting now does any of it. A
   good way to find the cases where waiting is right.
3. Read the Wikipedia entry on [Frank Partnoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Partnoy),
   five minutes, for the summary of his book on delay, which collects the research on
   why experts in fast-moving fields wait longer than novices before committing. The book
   itself is in the additional resources.
4. Take a decision that is currently open on your team or in your own work. Write down:
   what specifically you are waiting for, when it arrives, what it would cost to decide
   today, and your pre-decided answer for each likely outcome. If you cannot fill in the
   first line, decide today and note that you did. Add it to your decision journal.

</div>

## Additional resources

- *Wait: The Art and Science of Delay* by Frank Partnoy. A whole book on the research
  behind this lesson, from tennis returns to apologies to product launches.
- The economics idea of [option value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_value): a
  decision not yet made has a value of its own, which is what you give up when you decide
  early. Dry, but it puts a name to the thing this lesson is protecting.
