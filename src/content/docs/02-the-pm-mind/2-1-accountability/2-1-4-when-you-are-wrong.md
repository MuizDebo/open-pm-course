## Introduction

You will make a call, defend it, get the team to build it, and watch it fail. Not once. Many
times. The question is not whether this happens to you but what you do in the twenty-four
hours after you realise it. Those hours decide whether the team trusts your next call more
or less.

Most PMs handle being wrong in one of two bad ways. They dig in, because reversing feels
like admitting the critics were right all along. Or they collapse, reversing everything and
apologising to everyone, which teaches the team that pressure works. This lesson is the
third option: reverse cleanly, explain honestly, keep the parts that were right, and move
on without a scar. It also covers the mirror case, where you think a decision is wrong and
it is not yours to make.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Good decisions and good outcomes are different things, and why that matters.
- One-way and two-way doors, and how it changes how hard you should hold a position.
- How to reverse a decision in public without losing credibility.
- Disagree and commit: what it is, what it is not, and when to use it.
- Resulting and escalation of commitment, the two traps on either side.

## Decisions and outcomes are different

A poker player who goes all in with the best possible hand and loses to a lucky draw made a
good decision with a bad outcome. A player who bets everything on a terrible hand and wins
made a bad decision with a good outcome. Judge them by outcomes and you will reward the
second and punish the first, which is exactly backwards.

Product work is the same. A feature built on strong evidence can fail because the market
moved. A feature built on a hunch can succeed. If you judge yourself and your team only on
outcomes, you will learn the wrong lessons: you will stop making the right bets that
happened to lose, and keep making the wrong ones that happened to win. Annie Duke calls
the error "resulting", and it is the single most useful word in this lesson.

So when something fails, ask two questions separately. Was the outcome bad? Was the
decision bad given what we knew? The answers are independent, and only the second one tells
you what to change.

## One-way and two-way doors

Jeff Bezos's distinction, from his 2015 and 2016 letters to shareholders: some decisions
are one-way doors, hard or impossible to reverse, and deserve slow, careful deliberation.
Most decisions are two-way doors. You walk through, look around, and if you do not like it,
you walk back. Those deserve speed, and treating them like one-way doors is how
organisations grind to a halt.

For the PM this is a tool for calibrating how hard to hold a position. A two-way door
decision that turned out wrong is cheap to reverse, so reverse it without drama. A one-way
door decision that turned out wrong is a real loss and needs a real postmortem. Most of the
arguments that feel existential in a product team are about two-way doors, and naming that
out loud lowers the temperature fast.

## Reversing in public

When you have decided you were wrong, do it like this.

**Say it first, before anyone makes you.** The credibility cost of reversing is small. The
cost of being seen to resist the evidence until forced is large, and it compounds.

**Say what changed.** "When we decided, we believed X. We now know Y. Given Y, the right
call is Z." This is the whole speech. It separates the decision, which was reasonable on
X, from the reversal, which is reasonable on Y. It is not an apology, because a reasonable
decision on the evidence available does not need one.

**Keep what was right.** Rarely is everything about a decision wrong. The problem may still
be real, the user research may still hold, part of the build may be reusable. Say so.
Reversal is not the same as erasure.

**Thank the person who was right.** By name, in the same message. If someone argued
against the decision and the evidence proved them right, the team needs to see that being
right is rewarded, not that being right annoyed the PM.

**Do not over-reverse.** One bad outcome does not mean the strategy is wrong. Reverse the
decision, not the direction, unless the evidence really does reach that far.

## Disagree and commit

The mirror case. A decision has been made, by your manager, by leadership, by the team in a
vote you lost, and you think it is wrong. You have three choices. Keep fighting it, which
after a point is sabotage. Comply while making your disagreement visible in every meeting,
which is worse. Or disagree and commit.

Disagree and commit means: you argued your case fully, with evidence, while the decision
was open. You lost. Now you execute as if it were your own decision, without hedging,
without "I told you so" waiting in the wings, and without quietly starving it of effort.
You keep your disagreement on record, in your decision journal, and if the evidence later
supports you, you raise it then, once, with the evidence.

What it is not: a way to make people stop arguing before the decision is made. A manager
who says "disagree and commit" while the question is still open is using it to end a debate
they are losing. The phrase applies after the decision, not instead of one. It is also not
permanent. If the evidence changes materially, the decision reopens, and raising it then is
not disloyalty.

The reason this matters for the PM in particular is that you ask it of everyone else
constantly. Every no you say in lesson 2.1.3 is a request for someone to disagree and
commit. You cannot ask it credibly if you do not do it yourself.

## Two traps

**Resulting**, already covered: judging a decision by its outcome. The cure is the decision
journal, which records what you knew at the time so you can judge the decision on that.

**Escalation of commitment**, the sunk-cost habit in its organisational form. The more
visibly you defended a decision, the more it costs to reverse, so you keep going, so it
costs more. Teams can burn quarters this way. The cure is the two-way-door question, asked
early and often: if we were starting today, knowing what we know, would we choose this?
If not, the money already spent is not a reason to spend more.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Jeff Bezos's [2016 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders),
   fifteen minutes. It contains the clearest statements of both ideas in this lesson:
   high-velocity decisions through two-way doors, and disagree and commit, including his
   own example of committing to a decision he disagreed with.
2. Read Paul Graham's [How to Disagree](http://paulgraham.com/disagree.html), ten minutes.
   A hierarchy of disagreement from name-calling to refuting the central point. Use it to
   check that when you argue a case before a decision, you are arguing at the top of the
   hierarchy, because that is the only kind of disagreement that earns the right to commit
   afterwards with credibility.
3. Read Gary Klein's [Performing a Project Premortem](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem),
   five minutes. The pre-mortem is the cheapest way to find out you are wrong before it
   costs anything: imagine the decision has failed and ask why. Course 5 makes it a team
   ritual; use it privately from now on.
4. Find a decision you are currently defending, at work or elsewhere. Write down, in one
   paragraph each: what evidence would make you reverse it, whether it is a one-way or
   two-way door, and whether anyone has argued against it with evidence you have not
   properly answered. If the last answer is yes, go and answer it this week.

## Additional resources

- Annie Duke's [books](https://www.annieduke.com/books/), especially *Thinking in Bets*,
  are the long version of the decision-versus-outcome distinction. The first two chapters
  are enough to change how you review your own calls.
- The Wikipedia entry on [Escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment)
  has the classic experiments and the organisational examples. Worth ten minutes if you
  have ever watched a team keep building something everyone knew was dead.
