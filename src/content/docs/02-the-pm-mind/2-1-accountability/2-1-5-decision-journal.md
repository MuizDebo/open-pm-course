## Introduction

The four lessons in this section keep pointing at the same tool: a written record of what
you decided, what you knew, what you expected and how confident you were, made before the
outcome arrives. That record is the only defence against hindsight bias, the only fair way
to judge a decision separately from its result, and the place your disagreements live when
you have committed to something you argued against.

This project starts the habit. You will keep a decision journal for two weeks, then review
it, then submit the template and one anonymised entry with your reflection. Many working
PMs keep one for their whole career. The ones who do are noticeably better at knowing when
they are right and when they are lucky.

## What goes in an entry

Each entry is short. Five to ten minutes at most, written at the moment of deciding, not
after. The fields:

- **Date and decision.** One sentence. "Chose to ship the onboarding change to 10 percent
  of new users rather than everyone."
- **Context.** What situation prompted it, in two or three sentences.
- **What I know.** The evidence, briefly. Data, research, opinions you weighed.
- **What I do not know.** The gaps you are deciding across. This field matters most.
- **Options considered.** Including the one you picked and at least one you rejected, with
  a line on why.
- **Expected outcome.** What you think will happen, concretely enough to check later.
- **Confidence.** A number from 50 to 100 percent that the expected outcome occurs. Not
  a feeling: a number you are willing to be scored on.
- **Who disagreed.** If anyone argued for a different option, name it (anonymise if
  submitting) and their reason, in their terms.
- **Review date.** When you will come back and check.

The entries do not need to be about work. A decision journal about which flat to rent, which
course to take next or whether to raise a problem with a friend works just as well for
building the habit, and is easier to submit publicly.

If you want a second opinion on the template, Farnam Street's
[decision journal](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/) page is the best-known one and the
source for several of the fields above. Borrow what helps.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. **Set up the journal.** A Markdown file in your work repository, a notes app, a Google
   Doc, or paper. Create a template with the nine fields above. Put a daily reminder in your
   calendar for the two weeks; the planner from lesson 0.2 can do this.
2. **Make your first entry from lesson 2.1.2.** Rewrite the blameless postmortem you wrote
   there as a journal entry, as if you had written it before the outcome. Fill in "what I
   knew" and "confidence" honestly, from your memory of the time. This is practice at
   separating the decision from the result.
3. **Keep the journal for fourteen days.** Aim for at least ten entries. Record every
   decision that has a real alternative and a checkable outcome: what to prioritise, what to
   say no to, what to commit to despite disagreeing, and at least two non-work decisions.
   Record each one before you know how it turns out.
4. **Review at the end.** For every entry whose outcome is now known, add two lines: what
   happened, and whether the decision was good given what you knew, separately from whether
   the outcome was good. Mark each entry as one of: good decision, good outcome; good
   decision, bad outcome; bad decision, good outcome; bad decision, bad outcome.
5. **Write the reflection.** Three hundred to five hundred words. What did you notice about
   your own patterns? Were your confidence numbers calibrated, meaning did roughly 70
   percent of your 70 percent calls come true? Which of the four quadrants did most entries
   land in? What will you change about how you decide?
6. **Submit.** A folder called `2-1-5-decision-journal` in your work repository containing
   your blank template, one complete anonymised entry with its review, and the reflection.
   Do not submit the whole journal; most of it should stay private. Post the link in the
   project's Discussions thread using the template from lesson 0.3, and review two other
   submissions. In your reviews, look especially at whether the "what I do not know" field
   is honest or empty.

#### Extra credit

- Keep the journal for another month and submit a second reflection on whether your
  calibration improved.
- Add a "pre-mortem" line to your template, from lesson 2.1.4: before deciding, imagine it
  has failed and write the most likely reason. Check at review time whether you were right
  about the failure mode.
