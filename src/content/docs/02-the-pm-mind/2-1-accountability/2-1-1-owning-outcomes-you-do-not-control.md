## Introduction

Course 1 told you the fact. This lesson is about living with it. A product manager is held
to account for whether the product works, grows and earns, and has no authority over the
people who design it, build it, sell it or fund it. Engineers can decline your priority.
Designers can disagree with your framing. Leadership can change the goal in a meeting you
were not invited to. And when the quarter ends, the question "why didn't this land?" still
comes to you.

Most of the misery in product management comes from one of two wrong reactions to this.
One is to grab for control you do not have: issuing orders, escalating, hovering. The other
is to disown the outcome: "I can't be blamed, it wasn't my decision". Both are
understandable and both end careers. This lesson lays out the third way, which is the only
one that works, and what it costs.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why the role is designed this way, and why it is not a bug.
- The difference between accountability, responsibility and authority.
- What you actually control, and how much that turns out to be.
- The two failure modes: grabbing control and disowning outcomes.
- How to take ownership without lying to yourself about control.

## Why the job is built like this

It would be simpler if the PM ran the team. Companies have tried it. What they get is a
manager whose engineers tell them what they want to hear, whose designers stop pushing back,
and whose decisions are only as good as one person's judgement. The point of having a
product manager who cannot give orders is to force decisions to be made with evidence and
argument rather than rank. If you cannot compel anyone, you have to convince them, and
convincing people requires you to be right more often than not, and visibly so.

So the arrangement is deliberate. The outcome has one owner so that someone is always
asking "is this working?" The means are distributed so that no one person's blind spots
become the product's. The PM lives in the gap between those two facts.

## Three words that get mixed up

**Authority** is the power to decide and to compel. You have very little of it. You can
decide what the team works on next, within limits set above you, and that is roughly the
end of the list.

**Responsibility** is the duty to do a piece of work well. Engineers are responsible for
the code, designers for the design, you for the problem framing, the requirements, the
prioritisation and the measurement.

**Accountability** is being the person who answers for the result. It cannot be delegated
and it does not require authority. A football manager does not kick the ball. A conductor
does not play a note. Both are accountable for the performance.

The PM's position is high accountability, moderate responsibility, low authority. Once you
see those as three separate dials rather than one, the job stops feeling like a trick.

## What you actually control

More than it seems on a bad day. You control:

- **The problem.** Which problem the team is solving and how it is framed. A clear,
  evidence-backed problem is most of the influence you will ever have.
- **The decision and its reasoning.** You make the call on what to build, and you can
  make it legibly: here is what we know, here are the options, here is why this one.
  Written reasoning is persuasive in a way that opinions are not.
- **The quality of the inputs.** User research, data, competitive context. A team that
  has heard the customer does not need to be told what matters.
- **Attention.** Where you look, what you ask about, what you follow up on. Teams notice
  what the PM cares about and drift toward it.
- **Your reaction when it goes wrong.** Which is the subject of the next three lessons.

You do not control velocity, quality of execution, design taste, the market, the
competition, or the executive who changed their mind. Listing it this plainly is useful
because it stops you spending energy on the second list and tells you where the first list
needs to be excellent.

## The two failure modes

**Grabbing.** The PM who, lacking authority, behaves as if they had it. Micromanages
estimates, rewrites the design, escalates every disagreement to the engineering manager,
copies the director on emails. It sometimes works for a month. Then the team routes around
them: decisions get made in channels the PM is not in, estimates get padded, the designer
stops bringing early work. The PM ends up with less influence than they started with and
cannot understand why.

**Disowning.** The PM who, lacking authority, concludes they cannot be accountable. "I
wrote the spec; engineering missed the date." "Leadership changed the priority." "The
designer wanted it that way." Every sentence may be true. The pattern is still fatal,
because the one thing the organisation needs from a PM is someone who will own the result,
and this person has just announced that they will not. Disowning PMs get moved sideways
into project management or out.

Both failure modes come from the same mistake: believing that accountability needs
authority. It does not. It needs ownership.

## Ownership without control

Ownership is a stance, not a power. It means that when the result is bad, your first
question is "what could I have done differently?", asked sincerely and answered honestly,
even when the proximate cause was someone else. It is not self-blame. It is the refusal to
let "not my fault" be the end of the analysis.

In practice:

1. **Say "we" when it works and "I" when it does not.** Credit is distributed;
   accountability is not.
2. **Name the risk before it lands.** If the date is slipping, say so in week two, not week
   eight, with options. Accountability includes the duty to warn.
3. **Ask what you would do differently, every time.** Not whether you could have. What
   you would. If the honest answer is "nothing", say that too, with the reasoning.
4. **Separate the decision from the outcome.** Good decisions have bad outcomes, and the
   reverse. Lesson 2.1.4 and the project at the end of this section are built on this
   distinction.

This is harder than it sounds, because it means absorbing the discomfort of a bad result
without the relief of pointing at someone. The PMs who last are the ones who can hold that
discomfort and stay curious. The next lesson is about the structure that makes it bearable.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Marty Cagan's [Empowered Product Teams](https://www.svpg.com/empowered-product-teams/),
   ten minutes. The clearest statement of why the team is given a problem and an outcome,
   not a list of features, and why that makes the PM accountable for the result rather than
   the output.
2. Read Amazon's [Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles),
   ten minutes, with attention to "Ownership" and "Disagree and Commit". Whatever you think
   of the company, these two principles are the most-copied statement of the stance this
   lesson describes, and you will meet them in interviews.
3. Watch Jocko Willink's [Extreme Ownership](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ljqra3BcqWM),
   fourteen minutes. It is a military talk and the tone will not be to everyone's taste.
   Watch it for the single idea: the leader who owns everything that happens in their
   area, including the parts they did not do, is the one the team follows.
4. Write down a result from your work, any work, that went badly and was mostly not your
   fault. Then write three things you could have done differently that would have made it
   less bad. Not "worked harder". Specific, available-at-the-time actions. Keep it; the
   decision journal project uses this.

## Additional resources

- The Wikipedia entry on [Locus of control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locus_of_control)
  is the psychology underneath this lesson. PMs who last tend to have an internal locus
  about decisions and an external one about outcomes, and the distinction is worth
  knowing by name.
- Ben Horowitz's [Good Product Manager / Bad Product Manager](https://a16z.com/good-product-manager-bad-product-manager/),
  which you read in Course 1, is worth a second pass now. Notice how many of the "good"
  lines are about ownership and how few are about authority.
