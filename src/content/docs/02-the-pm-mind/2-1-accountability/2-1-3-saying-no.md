## Introduction

Course 4 will teach you prioritisation frameworks. This lesson is about the part the
frameworks leave out: every item that drops below the line belonged to somebody, and that
somebody is going to hear "no" from you. A sales lead with a deal on the table. A senior
engineer with a refactor they have wanted for a year. An executive with an idea from a
conference. A loyal customer who has asked three times.

Saying no is most of what a product manager does, and it is the part most PMs do worst,
because it is uncomfortable and because nobody taught them. Done badly, it produces
resentment, end-runs and a roadmap that fills up with things you did not choose. Done
well, it produces the opposite: people who trust that when you say yes, you mean it.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why "no" is the job, not a failure of the job.
- Why it feels so bad, and what that feeling is actually about.
- A structure for a good no: acknowledge, reason, alternative, door.
- The five requests you will say no to most, and what each one needs.
- The nos you should not say.

## Strategy is what you say no to

A product strategy that permits everything is not a strategy. The value of a direction
comes from what it excludes. If the team is building a tool for small accountants, it is
not building a tool for enterprise finance departments, and every enterprise request is a
chance to either hold that line or lose it. Hold it twenty times and you have a product.
Lose it twenty times and you have a feature list.

This reframes the no. It is not you refusing someone. It is the strategy, which the
company agreed to, doing what strategies do. Your job is to represent it honestly and to
notice when the twentieth request is actually evidence that the strategy is wrong, which
sometimes it is.

## Why it feels bad

Three things are happening when you flinch from a no.

You want to be liked, which is human and not a flaw, and a no risks that. You are not sure
you are right, which is often true, and a no exposes you to being wrong in public. And the
person asking usually has a real need, so the no feels like refusing to help.

All three are real. None of them is a reason to say yes. The person who says yes to avoid
the discomfort is not being kind; they are buying their own comfort with the team's time
and the asker's false hope. The engineers will build the thing, the customer will wait for
it, and the honest conversation happens anyway, three months later and worse.

## The shape of a good no

A good no has four parts. It can be delivered in two sentences or a page, but all four are
there.

**Acknowledge the need.** Say back what they are asking for and why it matters to them,
accurately enough that they would agree you understood. Most bad nos fail here: the asker
leaves believing you did not get it, so they ask again, louder.

**Give the reason.** The real one, in terms of the trade-off. "We are focused on X this
quarter and this would cost us Y." Not "it's not on the roadmap", which is a description,
not a reason. If the real reason is that you do not think it is a good idea, say that, with
your evidence.

**Offer what you can.** Often there is something. A smaller version. A workaround. A date
when it will be reconsidered. An introduction to someone who can help. Sometimes there is
nothing, and then do not invent something.

**Leave the door open, honestly.** "If we see three more customers with this, I want to
hear about it." Then mean it. A no that comes with a condition for yes is one people can
accept, and it turns the asker into a source of evidence instead of an opponent.

What is missing from this list: apology. Do not apologise for a decision you believe in.
"Sorry, but" signals that you think the no is wrong, and people will act accordingly.

## Five requests and what each needs

**From sales, with a deal attached.** Acknowledge the deal size. Ask what the customer is
actually trying to do, because the feature they named is often not the only way to do it.
If you say no, give sales something they can say to the customer. Never let the answer be
"the PM said no", because then it is a person, not a strategy.

**From an engineer, for technical work.** Take it seriously; engineers rarely ask for
refactors they do not need. Ask what it unlocks and what breaks if it waits. If it is a
no-for-now, agree on a trigger: a date, a metric, a dependency. Engineers accept a sequenced
no far better than a dismissed one.

**From an executive.** Do not say no in the meeting. Say "let me look at it properly"
and come back within days with a written one-pager: what it would cost, what it would
displace, what you recommend. Executives are used to being agreed with to their face and
ignored afterwards; a direct, reasoned, written response earns more respect than either.

**From a customer, repeatedly.** The hardest, because they are right that they need it.
Be straight: this is not something we plan to build, here is why, here is what we do
instead or who does. Losing a customer honestly is better than keeping one on false hope.

**From yourself.** Your own ideas are the ones you are least able to say no to. Run them
through the same evidence bar as everyone else's. Ask a colleague to argue against them.

## The nos you should not say

Some nos are reflexes, and reflexes are not decisions.

- **No because it was not your idea.** If it is a good idea, the origin does not matter.
- **No because the asker is annoying.** Separate the request from the person.
- **No because yes would be more work for you.** That is a reason to plan, not to refuse.
- **No because you said no last time.** Evidence changes. If the twentieth request is
  telling you something, listen to it.

The test is whether you can write the reason down and show it to the asker. If you cannot,
it is not a no yet. It is a feeling.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Des Traynor's [Product strategy means saying no](https://www.intercom.com/blog/product-strategy-means-saying-no/),
   ten minutes. The best short piece on why most feature requests, however reasonable,
   should be declined, with twelve of the arguments you will hear for "yes" and why each is
   weaker than it sounds.
2. Watch Steve Jobs on ["Focusing is about saying no"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H8eP99neOVs),
   two minutes. A clip from 1997, and the single most-quoted thing a product leader has
   said about this. Note that he describes it as painful, not as easy.
3. Read Marty Cagan's [Managing Stakeholders](https://www.svpg.com/managing-stakeholders/),
   ten minutes, for how the relationship with the people you say no to should work over
   time: early involvement, no surprises, and the difference between informing and
   persuading.
4. Write three nos you should have said in the last year, at work or anywhere, and did not.
   For each, write the four-part version you would say now: acknowledge, reason,
   alternative, door. Then pick one that is still live and actually say it this week.

</div>
