## Introduction

Everything before this point was practice in pieces. You interviewed users in one course,
wrote a roadmap in another, specified a feature in a third, designed an eval suite in a
fourth. Each artifact stood on its own and was reviewed on its own. The capstone puts them
in a line and asks the question a hiring manager, a founder or your future self will ask:
can you take one product from a vague problem to a launch plan, end to end, and defend every
step?

The capstone is six packs and a pitch, built over about three weeks. It is the largest
single piece of work in the course and the one that matters most for your portfolio. It is
also, for most learners, the point where the course stops feeling like lessons and starts
feeling like the job.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What the capstone is and why it is structured as integrated packs.
- Choosing your product: your own idea, or the supplied case.
- The six packs and the pitch, and which courses each one draws on.
- A three-week schedule that working learners can keep.
- What "done" looks like, how peers review it, and where it ends up.

## Why packs, not a report

A capstone could be a long document. It is not, for two reasons.

First, product managers do not produce long documents. They produce a chain of smaller
artifacts, each made for a particular audience at a particular moment: a synthesis for the
team, a narrative for leadership, a PRD for engineers, a launch plan for everyone. The
capstone asks for that chain, so that each piece is the thing you would actually hand
someone.

Second, the chain has to hold together. A roadmap that ignores the interviews, a PRD that
does not serve the goals sheet, a metrics tree that does not measure the strategy: these are
the ordinary failures of real product work, and they are invisible when you only ever look
at one artifact. Putting six packs side by side makes the joints visible. The review in
Discussions will look at the joints as much as the pieces.

## Choosing your product

You have two honest options.

**Your own idea.** If you carried one product from project 3.1.4 Audience map through the
projects in Courses 4 to 7, use it. You already have an audience map, interviews, an
opportunity tree, a narrative, a goals sheet, a roadmap, a PRD, a sprint plan, a launch
pack, a metrics tree and an AI feature with its evals. The capstone is then not a restart.
It is the work of integrating those pieces, tightening each one with what you learned
later, and filling the gaps you skipped. Expect to rewrite more than you expect to keep.
That is the point.

If you prefer a fresh idea, to prove you can do the whole thing cold, that is allowed and
respected. It is also roughly twice the work, because you start the discovery pack from
nothing. Choose it with your eyes open.

**The supplied case.** If you do not have an idea, or your earlier product has lost your
interest, use this one. It is designed so that every pack has something real to bite on.

> **Restock.** A WhatsApp-first ordering tool that lets small neighbourhood shop owners in
> Lagos restock from wholesalers without closing the shop to travel to the market. Most
> shop owners use low-end Android phones on metered data. They pay in cash or by bank
> transfer. Trust between shop owners and wholesalers is low and margins on both sides are
> thin. The wholesalers are not technical. The founding team is three people with six months
> of runway.

Nothing more is given. Who exactly the first users are, what the first feature is, how
trust gets built, how the business makes money, where AI belongs if anywhere, and how you
would know it is working are all yours to work out. If you choose Restock, you can still
interview real shop owners and wholesalers; there are some within walking distance of most
learners, and lesson 3.2.1 Get out of the building applies in full. Every Course 9 project
describes what the pack looks like for Restock so you are never guessing at the shape.

Whichever you choose, write it down in one paragraph before you start. The assignment asks
for that paragraph.

## The six packs and the pitch

Each pack corresponds to a course, and each project page tells you which earlier projects
it draws on and what "tightened" means for them.

| Pack | Project | Draws on | What it contains |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery | 9.2 | Course 3 | Audience map, interviews and synthesis, opportunity solution tree |
| Strategy | 9.3 | Course 4 | Narrative, system map, goals sheet, prioritised backlog, roadmap |
| Delivery | 9.4 | Course 5 | PRD for the first feature, wireframes, sprint plan, launch plan, pre-mortem |
| Measurement | 9.5 | Course 6 | Metrics tree, dashboard sketch, experiment design |
| AI | 9.6 | Course 7 | AI feature decision, AI PRD, eval suite, prototype |
| The pitch | 9.7 | All of it | A ten-minute recorded walkthrough of the whole case |

Course 2 has no pack. It is in all of them: in the decisions you record, the trade-offs you
name honestly, and the memo you write when a pack tells you to cut something. The review
guide asks peers to look for exactly that.

## The three-week schedule

Three weeks at eight to ten hours a week, in the order the packs are listed. The order
matters: each pack feeds the next.

- **Week one.** Discovery pack, then strategy pack. If you are reusing earlier work, this
  week is mostly rereading your own artifacts with Course 4 and Course 6 eyes and fixing
  what no longer holds. If you are starting fresh, the interviews take most of the week and
  the strategy pack will spill into week two.
- **Week two.** Delivery pack, then measurement pack. The PRD is the heaviest single
  artifact in the capstone. Give it two sessions.
- **Week three.** AI pack, then the pitch. Record the pitch on the last day, after you have
  reread everything once.

If you have less time, stretch to four or five weeks rather than thinning the packs. A
thin capstone is worse than a late one. If you have more time, spend it on interviews and
on the pitch, in that order.

## What done looks like

Each project page has its own checklist. Across the whole capstone, done means:

- Every pack is in a single capstone folder in your work repository, with a short `README`
  that links each artifact and says in two sentences what it is.
- The packs agree with each other. The first feature in the PRD is the top of the backlog.
  The metrics tree measures the goals sheet. The AI feature came out of the opportunity
  audit, not out of nowhere.
- Every pack has at least one decision recorded as a decision: what you chose, what you
  rejected, and why.
- The pitch can be watched by someone who has read none of it and leaves them knowing what
  the product is, who it is for, why it will work, and what you would do first.

## How it is reviewed

Post each pack in its own Discussions thread as you finish it, using the submission
template from lesson 0.3. Review two other learners' packs for each one you post. For the
capstone, reviewers are asked to look at three things in order: does the pack do what its
project page asks, does it agree with the packs before it, and is there a decision in it
that the author clearly found hard. A pack with a visible hard decision beats a tidy pack
with none.

When all six packs and the pitch are posted, post one final message in the capstone thread
linking the folder. That message is what you will point people to.

## Where it goes

The capstone is the centrepiece of the portfolio you built in project 8.2.4 Portfolio. Put
it first. A hiring manager who opens your portfolio should land on the capstone's `README`,
see the product in one paragraph, see the six packs and the pitch, and be able to open any
one of them in a click. Everything else in the portfolio supports it.

Before you start, reread the two hundred words you wrote in lesson 0.1 about why you were
here and what you wanted to be able to do. Keep them open while you write your product
statement. The gap between the two is what this course did.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Decide: your earlier product, a fresh idea, or Restock. Write a one-paragraph product
   statement in plain words: who it is for, what problem it solves, what it is, and what you
   do not yet know. Under a hundred and fifty words. If you chose Restock, the statement is
   still yours to write; the case above is a brief, not a statement.
2. Create a `9-capstone` folder in your work repository with a `README.md` holding the
   statement and empty headings for the six packs and the pitch. Commit it. The empty
   headings are a promise to yourself.
3. Post the statement in the capstone thread in
   [Discussions](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/discussions) with the date you
   intend to post the pitch. Reply to two other statements with one question each. The
   questions people ask you now are your first discovery data.
4. Reread your two hundred words from lesson 0.1 and your project 2.4.5 Letter to your past
   self. Do not write anything. Just read them, then start project 9.2.
