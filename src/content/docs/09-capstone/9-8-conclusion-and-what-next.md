## Introduction

If you have reached this page having done the projects, you have produced more product
artifacts than many working product managers make in their first year. If you have reached
it having only read, you have a map and no territory, and the most useful thing this page
can tell you is to go back to Course 1 and do the first project.

This lesson takes stock of what you have made, asks you to look back at two things you wrote
at the very beginning, sets out how to keep learning once the course's structure is gone,
and explains how to give some of what you got back to the people coming after you. It ends
with an honest note on what a course can and cannot do.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The portfolio of artifacts you now have and what each proves.
- Two pieces of writing from the start of the course to revisit.
- How to keep learning without a syllabus: reading, listening, communities.
- How to contribute to this course.
- What the course cannot do for you.

## What you have made

Count them. Across the path you have produced, for one or more products:

- A role audit and a written position on where you fit.
- A decision journal and a hard memo recommending a cut.
- A letter to your past self.
- An audience map, five user interviews with a synthesis, and an opportunity solution tree.
- A product narrative with a system map, and a strategy pack with goals, a prioritised
  backlog and a roadmap.
- A PRD with wireframes, a sprint plan, a product loop and a launch pack.
- A metrics tree with a dashboard, and an experiment analysis with a ship decision.
- An AI opportunity audit, an AI PRD, an eval suite and a working prototype.
- A portfolio and three mock interview self-reviews.
- A capstone: six packs and a ten-minute pitch.

That is the evidence. Lesson 8.3.1 mapped each interview round to one of these. When
someone asks "tell me about a time you", you have thirty times to choose from, and each has a
link.

## Two things to reread

In lesson 0.1 you wrote two hundred words on why you were here and what you wanted to be
able to do. Find them now and read them slowly. Some of what you wanted you can now do.
Some of it you no longer want, because you understand the job better. Write two hundred
more words underneath, dated, on what changed.

In lesson 1.1.1 you wrote a definition of what a product manager does in under a hundred
words. Rewrite it now without looking at the old one, then compare. The gap between the two
is the course.

## Keeping learning

The structure that got you here is about to disappear. What replaces it is a habit: read
one serious thing a week, make one thing a month, and talk to one practitioner a quarter.
Here is where to start.

**Books that came up across the course, worth reading in full.** *The Mom Test* by Rob
Fitzpatrick, on talking to users. *Continuous Discovery Habits* by Teresa Torres, the modern
discovery method. *Inspired* by Marty Cagan, on empowered product teams. *Good Strategy Bad
Strategy* by Richard Rumelt and *Playing to Win* by Lafley and Martin, on strategy. *Measure
What Matters* by John Doerr, on goals. *The Design of Everyday Things* by Don Norman, on
design. *The Mythical Man-Month* by Fred Brooks, on why software is late. *Thinking, Fast and
Slow* by Daniel Kahneman, on your own judgement. *Thanks for the Feedback* by Stone and
Heen, on receiving it.

**Newsletters.** [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/) for the broadest
view of the craft and its interviews with practitioners.
[Silicon Valley Product Group](https://www.svpg.com/) for Cagan's essays.
[Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/) for Torres on discovery.
[Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/) for strategy and the business of technology.
[Aakash Gupta's newsletter](https://www.news.aakashg.com/) for AI product management.

**Communities.** [Mind the Product](https://www.mindtheproduct.com/) has local meetups in
many cities, including on the continent. [Product School](https://productschool.com/) runs
free events. And this course's own
[Discussions](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/discussions), where the people who
reviewed your work are a network already.

**Podcasts.** Most of the newsletters above have one. Pick the one whose written voice you
liked and listen while you walk.

## Giving back

This course is free because people gave their time to it. The ways to return the favour, in
order of how much they help:

1. **Write a lesson.** Some lessons still carry a Draft badge. If you have done the work in
   one of those areas, you are qualified to write it. Open an issue to claim it, follow the
   style guide, open a pull request. Your name goes in the history of a course other people
   will learn from, which is a better line on a CV than most.
2. **Review submissions.** Every project thread has learners waiting for feedback. Twenty
   minutes a week reviewing two submissions is the most direct help there is.
3. **Fix what is broken.** Links rot, examples date, a sentence is unclear. The "Edit page"
   link at the bottom of every lesson makes a fix a two-minute job.
4. **Tell one person.** The person you think of when you read this sentence.

The [contributing guide](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md)
has the details.

## What a course cannot do

A course can give you the vocabulary, the methods, the habit of writing things down, and a
portfolio that proves you can do the work. It cannot give you the thing that makes a
product manager good, which is accumulated judgement from decisions that had consequences.
That comes from the job, and only from the job: from shipping something that nobody used,
from being overruled and watching what happened, from saying no to the loudest person in the
room and being right, and being wrong.

What the course can do is make sure that when those moments arrive, you recognise them, you
have a method to reach for, and you write down what you learned so the next one goes
better. That is all any of us have. Go and get the job.
