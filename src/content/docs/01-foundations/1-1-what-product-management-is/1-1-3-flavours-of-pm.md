## Introduction

"Product manager" is a family of jobs, not one job. A growth PM at a consumer app and a
platform PM at a cloud provider share the sentence from lesson 1.1.1 and almost nothing
about their week. Knowing the flavours lets you pick the ones that fit you, read a job ad
correctly, and stop comparing yourself to people doing a different job.

This lesson cuts the role three ways: by what the product is, by what the PM is optimising
for, and by the stage of the company. Real jobs are a mix.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Flavours by product: consumer, B2B, platform and internal.
- Flavours by focus: core, growth, technical, data and AI.
- Flavours by company stage: startup, scale-up, enterprise.
- Why the mix matters more than the label.

## By what the product is

**Consumer.** Millions of users, none of whom you can call. Data is the main voice, design
is decisive, and small changes to a funnel can move more than a new feature. Your week leans
toward experiments, metrics and working closely with design. Examples: a music app, a
payments wallet, a social product.

**B2B.** Hundreds or thousands of customers, some of whom are large enough to matter
individually. Sales and customer success are in your ear daily, contracts shape the roadmap,
and a single enterprise deal can create a feature. Your week leans toward customer calls,
prioritisation fights and writing things down so that sales stops promising them. Examples:
accounting software, a logistics platform, a developer tool sold to companies.

**Platform.** Your users are other engineers, inside or outside the company. The product is
an API, an SDK, a set of services other products are built on. Reliability, documentation
and backwards compatibility are features. Your week leans toward technical design
discussions and the slow work of getting other teams to adopt what you build.

**Internal.** The customer is a department of your own company. No revenue, so you justify
work with time saved and errors avoided. Stakeholders are also users and sit nearby, which is
both a gift and a trap.

## By what you are optimising for

**Core product.** Making the main thing better for the people who already use it. The
default flavour and the one most of this course assumes.

**Growth.** Acquisition, activation, retention, revenue. Growth PMs run many experiments,
live in the data, and work on onboarding, pricing pages, referral loops and notifications
rather than the core feature set. Fast feedback, high measurement discipline, and a real
risk of optimising the funnel while the product underneath goes stale.

**Technical.** Infrastructure, performance, migrations, developer experience. Often a former
engineer. The user is frequently internal and the value is often invisible until something
breaks. Deep systems knowledge required; design skill less so.

**Data.** Products whose value is the data or the model: a recommendation engine, a fraud
detector, a reporting product. Overlaps heavily with the next flavour.

**AI.** Products whose core behaviour comes from a model. The PM must understand what the
model can and cannot do, how to measure it, how to design for it being wrong, and what data
it needs. Course 7 covers this in depth. It is the fastest-growing flavour and the one with
the fewest experienced practitioners, which is an opportunity.

## By company stage

**Early startup, under thirty people.** There may be no PM title at all; the founder does
it. If you are hired, you do everything from support to QA to writing the onboarding email.
You learn fast and broadly. You also learn whatever the founder's habits are, good and bad.

**Scale-up, thirty to a few hundred.** Teams exist, each with a PM. Process is being
invented as you go. This is where most PMs get their best years: enough structure to do the
job, not so much that the job is the structure.

**Enterprise, many hundreds or thousands.** You own a narrow area. Much of the work is
alignment, documentation and navigating the organisation. Decisions are slower and larger.
The craft is deep and the blast radius of a mistake is wide.

Each stage is a different job. People who thrive at one often hate another, and it is
nothing to do with ability.

## Why the mix matters

Every real role is a point in this three-way space. A "growth PM at a B2B scale-up" and a
"core PM at an enterprise consumer product" overlap in the sentence from lesson 1.1.1 and
diverge on nearly everything about the week. When you evaluate a job, work out its
coordinates:

- What is the product, and who is the user? That tells you whose voice is loudest.
- What is the team measured on? That tells you what you will spend your time on.
- How big is the company and how fast is it changing? That tells you what the job will feel
  like.

Then ask yourself which combination you want. Not which is most prestigious. Which one has a
week you would be glad to live.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Brandon Chu's
   [Ruthless Prioritization](https://medium.com/blackboxofpm/ruthless-prioritization-e4256e3520a9),
   fifteen minutes. A scale-up PM's account of what the job feels like at that stage: a
   daily stream of decisions about what not to do. A good contrast with the enterprise and
   startup descriptions above.
2. Read Paul Graham's [Do Things That Don't Scale](http://paulgraham.com/ds.html), twenty
   minutes. It is about founders, and it describes exactly what product work looks like at
   the earliest stage: manual, personal, unscalable on purpose.
3. Read the a16z post [16 Startup Metrics](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/), fifteen
   minutes. You do not need to memorise the metrics yet. Read it to see how differently a
   consumer product and a B2B product are measured, which is the clearest window into how
   differently their PMs work.
4. Take the three job ads from lesson 1.1.2 and plot each one on the three axes: product
   type, focus, company stage. Add a sentence on which of the three you would rather have
   and why. Keep it for the section project.
