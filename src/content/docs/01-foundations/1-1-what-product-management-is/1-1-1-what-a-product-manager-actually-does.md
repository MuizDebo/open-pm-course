## Introduction

Ask ten product managers what they do and you will get ten answers, most of them lists of
activities: write specs, run standups, talk to customers, prioritise the backlog. The lists
are true and they miss the point. The job is not the activities. The job is to make sure the
team builds the right thing, that it gets built, and that it does what it was supposed to do
for the people it was built for. Everything else is how.

This lesson pins down the job in a way that survives contact with real companies, where the
title covers everything from "person who writes tickets" to "person who decides what the
company makes next". It also clears up the two most common misunderstandings before they
cost you an interview or a first year.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The one-sentence definition of the job and the three questions behind it.
- The four kinds of work that fill a PM's week.
- What a PM is accountable for and what they are not.
- Two myths: the CEO of the product, and the person who has the ideas.
- How the job changes with company size and product type.

## The job in one sentence

A product manager is accountable for the team building something valuable, usable and
feasible, and for knowing whether it worked. Three questions sit inside that sentence, and a
PM is doing the job whenever they are working on one of them:

1. **What should we build, and why?** Discovery, strategy, prioritisation.
2. **Is it getting built well?** Definition, delivery, working with engineering and design.
3. **Did it work?** Measurement, learning, deciding what comes next.

If a week goes by and none of your time touched one of those three, you were doing something
other than product management, whatever your title says.

## The four kinds of work

Most of the activities in a PM's week fall into four buckets. Knowing which bucket you are in
tells you what good looks like.

**Discover.** Talking to users, reading data, watching support tickets, studying competitors.
The output is understanding: what problem is worth solving, for whom, and why now. Course 3.

**Decide.** Turning understanding into a choice. This is where strategy, goals and
prioritisation live, and where most of the conflict is, because every yes is a no to
something else. Course 4.

**Define.** Writing it down so that a team can build it: requirements, user stories, success
metrics, what is out of scope. And then staying close while it is built, because no document
survives the first week of engineering. Course 5.

**Deliver and measure.** Shipping, launching, watching the numbers, writing up what happened
and feeding it back into discovery. Courses 5 and 6.

A PM at a large company may spend a month in one bucket. A PM at a ten-person startup cycles
through all four before lunch. Both are doing the same job.

## Accountable, not in charge

The thing that makes product management strange is that you are accountable for outcomes you
do not control. Engineers report to an engineering manager. Designers report to a design
lead. Nobody reports to you, and yet when the feature ships late or nobody uses it, the
question is "what happened?" and the person being asked is you.

That is not a design flaw. It is the design. A PM who could order people around would get
compliance instead of judgement, and the whole point of the role is to make better decisions
than any one function would make alone. The tools you have instead of authority are
evidence, clarity, trust and persuasion. Course 2 is about living with that; Course 1 just
needs you to see it clearly.

What you are accountable for: the decision about what to build, the clarity of the
definition, the honesty of the measurement, and the learning that follows. What you are not
accountable for: whether the code works, whether the design is good, whether the deadline
was realistic. You care about all of those and you influence all of them, but each has an
owner, and it is not you.

## Two myths

**The CEO of the product.** The phrase comes from a famous essay and it has done damage ever
since. It was meant to describe the breadth of concern, not the scope of authority. A PM
does not hire, fire, set budgets or overrule engineering. Walk in acting like a CEO and the
team will quietly route around you. The essay is in the assignment so you can read what it
actually says.

**The person who has the ideas.** The PM is not the source of ideas. Ideas come from users,
from engineers who see what is possible, from designers who see what is confusing, from
sales who hear what loses deals, from data. The PM's contribution is deciding which ideas
are worth the team's time and being able to explain why. A PM who insists on their own ideas
is a bottleneck. A PM who can take the best idea in the room and turn it into a shipped,
measured product is doing the job.

## Same job, different shapes

The job's shape depends on where you do it.

- **Early startup.** You are the discovery, the definition, often the support desk and the
  QA. The founder may be the real PM and you are their hands.
- **Scale-up.** Teams have formed. You own one area, with real engineers and a designer,
  and your week is the four buckets in rotation.
- **Large company.** You own a slice of a slice. The decisions are narrower, the stakeholders
  are more numerous, and a lot of the work is alignment: getting twelve people to agree
  before anything is built.
- **B2B versus consumer.** In B2B, a handful of customers can shape the roadmap and sales is
  a constant voice. In consumer, no single user matters and data is the loudest voice.
- **Platform and technical.** Your users are other engineers. The job is the same; the
  vocabulary is APIs and reliability instead of screens and funnels.

Lesson 1.1.3 goes through these flavours properly. For now, notice that the sentence at the
top of this lesson holds in every one of them.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Ben Horowitz's
   [Good Product Manager / Bad Product Manager](https://a16z.com/good-product-manager-bad-product-manager/),
   ten minutes. Written in the 1990s and still the most-shared definition of the job. Read it
   for the contrast pairs, and notice that "CEO of the product" is about taking ownership,
   not having authority.
2. Read Martin Eriksson's
   [What, exactly, is a Product Manager?](https://www.mindtheproduct.com/what-exactly-is-a-product-manager/),
   ten minutes. The source of the business, technology and user-experience diagram you will
   see in every PM slide deck. Read it for why the PM sits in the overlap and not at the top.
3. Read Marty Cagan's
   [Behind Every Great Product](https://www.svpg.com/behind-every-great-product/), twenty
   minutes. The clearest account of what separates PMs on teams that ship great products
   from PMs on teams that ship features. Note the four risks he says the PM owns.
4. Watch Henrik Kniberg's
   [Agile Product Ownership in a Nutshell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=502ILHjX9EE),
   fifteen minutes. It is about the product owner role in Scrum, which is a narrower job,
   but it is the best short animation of what deciding, defining and measuring look like
   in practice.
5. Write down, in your own words and in under a hundred words, what a product manager does.
   Keep it. You will rewrite it at the end of Course 1 and again at the end of the course.

</div>
