## Introduction

Product management is a well-paid, high-leverage, intellectually varied job, and a lot of
people who get into it are unhappy within two years. The reason is rarely ability. It is
that the job's daily texture is nothing like the way it is described, and nobody told them.

This lesson is the telling. It is not meant to put you off. It is meant to let you choose
with your eyes open, and to give you a head start on the parts that catch people out. Course
2 goes much deeper into living with them. For now, you just need to know what they are.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Five things about the job that are true and not in the job ad.
- The traits that correlate with thriving, and the ones that do not.
- Common reasons people leave, and which of them are avoidable.
- A simple test you can run on yourself this week.

## Five things nobody puts in the job ad

**You will be interrupted all day.** The job is coordination, and coordination arrives as
messages. Engineers with questions, designers with options, sales with a customer on the
line, leadership with a new idea. Deep work happens early, late or not at all unless you
defend it. People who need long unbroken stretches to think well find this exhausting.

**You will be wrong in public.** You made the call, the feature shipped, nobody used it, and
the retrospective is on the calendar. There is no hiding in a PM role. If you take being
wrong as a judgement on you rather than information about the world, the job will grind you
down. If you can be wrong, say so, learn and move on, it will make you very good.

**Nobody has to do what you say.** Covered in lesson 1.1.1, but it bears repeating because
it is the thing people from engineering or management underestimate most. Your influence
runs on trust and evidence. When those run low, you are a person sending messages that
nobody answers.

**Most of the job is writing and talking.** Documents, updates, meetings, one-to-ones,
customer calls. If you got into technology to avoid that, product management is the wrong
door. If you like explaining things and are good at it, it is one of the few technical roles
where that is the core skill.

**You rarely get to finish.** Shipping is the midpoint, not the end. The moment one thing
is out, the next three are already being argued about. People who need closure to feel
satisfied struggle with a job that is a permanently open loop.

## Who tends to thrive

Not the loudest, and not the most technical. From watching a lot of PMs, the traits that
seem to matter:

- **Curiosity about people.** Genuinely wanting to know why someone did what they did, in
  a user interview or a planning meeting.
- **Comfort with ambiguity.** Being able to act with sixty percent of the information,
  because a hundred percent never arrives.
- **Low ego, high standards.** Caring intensely about the outcome and not much about
  whose idea it was.
- **Clear writing.** Not elegant. Clear. The ability to put a decision and its reasoning on
  one page that a tired engineer can act on.
- **Steady under conflict.** Able to hold a disagreement without needing to win it or
  flee it.

Traits that matter less than people think: being technical enough to code (useful, not
required outside technical PM roles), having a business degree, being extroverted, having
"vision".

## Why people leave

The common exits, roughly in order:

1. **The job was not what they thought.** Usually one of the five things above. Mostly
   avoidable by reading this lesson and lesson 1.1.3 honestly.
2. **The company does not actually do product management.** They were hired as a PM and
   used as a ticket-writer or a project manager. Often the company does not know the
   difference. Lesson 1.1.2's job-ad decoder helps here, and so does asking the right
   questions in interviews, which Course 8 covers.
3. **Burnout from the always-on loop.** Avoidable, but only with deliberate habits. Course
   2 has a lesson on it.
4. **They preferred making things to deciding things.** A real and respectable reason.
   Some of the best engineers and designers are ex-PMs who found out.

None of these is failure. The point of asking now is to make the first two unlikely and the
third survivable.

## A test you can run this week

Pick a product you use and are mildly annoyed by. Spend one hour doing the following:

1. Write down what the annoyance is and who else probably has it.
2. Guess why the company has not fixed it. Think about what they would have to give up.
3. Write a half-page memo to their PM arguing for a change, with the trade-off stated.
4. Show it to someone and ask them to argue the other side.

If that hour was energising, you have the core of the job. If it was tedious, that is
useful information too. The section project asks you to do a version of this at scale.

1. Read Josh Elman's [A Product Manager's Job](https://medium.com/@joshelman/a-product-managers-job-63c09a43d0ec),

   [Working with Product Managers as an Engineering Manager or Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/working-with-product-managers),

   what PMs look like from the other side of the table, including when they are bad at it.

   project.
4. Write a short, private note answering: which of the five "things nobody puts in the job
   ad" worries you most, and what would you do about it? There is no right answer, and you
   will revisit this in Course 2.
