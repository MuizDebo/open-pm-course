## Introduction

Product management is about ninety years old and has been reinvented at least four times.
Each reinvention left a layer behind, and companies today run on different layers without
knowing it. That is why one company's PM writes marketing plans, another's writes code, and
a third's writes tickets, and all three are called the same thing.

You do not need the history to do the job. You need it to understand why the job is defined
so differently from place to place, to decode what a job ad actually means, and to see which
way the role is moving now, which matters for Course 7 and Course 8.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The brand-management origin and what it left behind.
- The shift from marketing to engineering with software.
- The agile and lean reinvention of the 2000s.
- The modern product-team model and where AI is pushing it next.
- How to read a job description against this history.

## 1931: the brand man

The usual origin story starts with a memo at Procter and Gamble. A young marketer named Neil
McElroy argued that each brand should have one person responsible for it end to end: its
sales, its advertising, its problems in the field, its improvement. The company agreed, and
the "brand man" was born. The idea that one person owns the whole outcome of one product,
without owning the people who make it, comes straight from that memo and has never left.

What it left behind: the PM as the person who understands the customer and the market, and
who coordinates everyone else. In consumer goods companies the role is still called brand
management and still looks like this.

## 1980s to 1990s: software borrows the role

When packaged software became an industry, companies needed someone to decide what went in
the next release. Hewlett-Packard, then Microsoft, adapted the brand-manager idea. Microsoft
in particular split it: a "program manager" who worked with engineers on what to build and
how, and a "product manager" who sat in marketing and handled positioning and launch. Both
titles survive, and the confusion between them survives too.

What it left behind: the PM moved closer to engineering. Specs, schedules and feature
decisions became the job. The 1990s essay you read in the last lesson comes from this era,
and so does the "CEO of the product" phrase.

## 2000s: the internet and agile

Two things changed at once. Software started shipping continuously instead of in boxed
releases, so the annual spec became useless. And the agile movement argued that teams
should build in small increments and learn as they went. Scrum gave the team a "product
owner" who kept the backlog and answered the engineers' questions. In many companies the PM
simply became the product owner, and the strategic half of the job quietly disappeared.

At the same time, Google's associate product manager programme, started by Marissa Mayer in
2002, created a template for the technical, data-driven, generalist PM that most large tech
companies now hire. The modern PM job ad descends from that programme.

What it left behind: the backlog, the sprint, the user story, the idea that the PM is
embedded in the engineering team rather than in marketing. And the lean startup idea, from
the end of the decade, that the PM's main output is validated learning, not shipped features.

## 2010s: the empowered product team

By the mid-2010s the best-regarded companies had converged on a model: a small, durable team
of a PM, a designer and a few engineers, given a problem to solve and an outcome to hit,
rather than a list of features to deliver. Discovery became a daily activity. The PM's job
became to make sure the team solved the right problem, with the authority coming from
evidence rather than from the org chart.

This is the model this course teaches, because it is the one that produces the best
products and the one the strongest companies hire for. It is also, honestly, the model most
companies claim and fewer practise. Lesson 1.1.4 is about telling the difference.

## Now: AI and the collapsing distance to the build

Two forces are reshaping the role as you read this. First, products increasingly contain
models whose behaviour is probabilistic, which changes what a spec is, what quality means
and how you measure success. Second, AI tools let a PM prototype, analyse data and draft
documents that used to need other people. The distance between deciding and building is
shrinking.

The likely result is a PM who is more technical, more hands-on with data, and more
responsible for evaluation. Course 7 is that PM's training. It is not a side topic; it is
where the role is going.

## Reading a job description

Every job ad is a fossil record. Look for which layer it comes from:

- "Go-to-market", "positioning", "pricing", "launch" in the first paragraph: the marketing
  layer. Often B2B, often called product marketing elsewhere.
- "Write detailed requirements", "manage the backlog", "work with engineering to deliver":
  the product-owner layer. The role may be execution-focused with strategy held above you.
- "Own outcomes", "discovery", "experimentation", "metrics": the empowered-team layer. Ask
  in the interview how often they talk to customers, and whether the roadmap lists features
  or problems.
- "Technical PM", "platform", "API": the engineering-facing layer. Expect systems questions.
- "AI PM", "ML product": the newest layer. Expect questions about evals and data.

None of these is wrong. They are different jobs with the same name, and knowing which one
you are applying for is most of the battle.
