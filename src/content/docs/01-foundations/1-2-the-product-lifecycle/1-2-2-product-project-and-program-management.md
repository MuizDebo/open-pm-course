## Introduction

Product manager, project manager, program manager. Three titles, one shared word, and more
confusion than any other corner of the job. Companies mix them up when hiring. Candidates
mix them up when applying. And a lot of people with "product" on their badge are doing one
of the other two jobs without knowing it, which is the second most common reason PMs quit,
as lesson 1.1.4 noted.

This lesson draws the lines. Not to rank the jobs, which are all real and all needed, but
so that you can tell which one you are being asked to do and whether that is the one you
want.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What each of the three roles owns, in one sentence each.
- The question each role is answering.
- Where the roles overlap, and why small companies merge them.
- Where they must stay separate, and what breaks when they do not.
- How to tell which job a "product manager" role actually is.

## Three jobs in three sentences

**Product manager:** decides what to build and why, and is accountable for whether it
delivers value to users and the business. The question is *what and why*.

**Project manager:** gets a defined piece of work done on time, on budget and to scope,
coordinating the people and tracking the risks. The question is *when and how, within the
constraints*.

**Program manager:** coordinates several related projects or teams toward a larger goal
that none of them could hit alone, managing the dependencies between them. The question is
*how do all these pieces fit together*.

A product manager thinks about the next year of a product's life. A project manager thinks
about the next milestone. A program manager thinks about the six teams whose milestones
have to line up.

## What each role owns

| | Product manager | Project manager | Program manager |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owns | The problem, the priorities, the outcome | The plan, the schedule, the delivery | The dependencies, the cross-team plan |
| Starts with | A user problem or business goal | A defined scope | A set of related projects |
| Ends when | Never; the loop continues | The project ships | The program's goal is met |
| Measured on | Did the product work for users and the business | On time, on budget, to scope | Did the pieces land together |
| Says no to | Features that do not serve the goal | Scope creep and slippage | Projects that break the critical path |
| Lives in | Discovery, definition, measurement | Delivery | Across delivery of many teams |

The table is clean. Real companies are not, and that is the next section.

## Where they overlap

At a startup of fifteen people, one person does all three. The PM writes the requirements,
tracks the sprint, and chases the dependency on the payments provider. That is normal and
healthy at that size. The trouble comes when a company grows and never separates the roles,
so the PM is still running the schedule of forty engineers and has no time left to talk to a
user.

In many software companies today, the project-management work has been absorbed into the
team's own process. An agile team plans its own sprints, tracks its own work and manages its
own risks, with the PM and the engineering lead sharing what a project manager used to do.
That is why "project manager" has almost disappeared from software job boards while
"product manager" has multiplied. It does not mean the work went away. It means it got
spread around, and the PM often ends up holding more of it than they should.

Program management shows up when the company is large enough to have many teams working on
one thing: a platform migration, a new market launch, a compliance deadline. A good program
manager is a gift to a PM, because they take the cross-team coordination that would
otherwise eat the PM's week.

## Where they must stay separate

The one thing that must never blur is accountability for the outcome. A project manager is
accountable for delivering the scope. A product manager is accountable for the scope being
the right one. If one person is both, the incentives collide: it is always tempting to
protect the schedule by shipping what was planned, even after the evidence says it is
wrong. The project-manager half wins, the product ships on time, and nobody uses it.

The second thing to keep separate is where the authority comes from. Project and program
managers run on the plan: a schedule exists, people have committed, and the job is holding
them to it. Product managers run on evidence and persuasion, as lesson 1.1.1 said. A PM who
starts managing by the plan, chasing engineers for dates and treating the roadmap as a
contract, has quietly become a project manager and will be treated as one.

## Telling which job you are being offered

Job ads rarely use the right word. Read the responsibilities instead.

- "Define requirements, manage the backlog, track delivery, report status": mostly project
  management with a product title. Ask in the interview who decides what goes on the
  roadmap. If the answer is "leadership" or "the client", the PM is executing, not deciding.
- "Coordinate across teams, manage dependencies, run the release train": program
  management. A fine job. Not a product job.
- "Own outcomes, talk to customers, define strategy, prioritise": product management. Then
  ask how much of the week is also spent on the first two, because the honest answer is
  never zero.

None of these is a trap if you know what you are walking into. The trap is wanting one and
taking another.

   fraction of the listed responsibilities is product, project or program work. Write the
   three numbers next to each ad. You will probably find that at least one "product
   manager" role is mostly something else.
