## Introduction

Every product, from a two-person startup's first feature to a bank's mobile app, moves
through the same stages: someone notices a problem, someone decides what to do about it,
someone builds it, and then everyone finds out whether it worked. The names change from
company to company. The stages do not.

What does change, and what separates good product teams from the rest, is whether they
treat those stages as a line or a loop. A line ends at launch. A loop treats launch as the
point where you learn the most and start again. This lesson gives you the loop, the
vocabulary for each stage, and the reason the loop beats the line.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The four stages: discovery, definition, delivery, measurement.
- What each stage produces and who is involved.
- Why the stages form a loop, and what goes wrong when they form a line.
- The minimum viable product, what it is for, and how the idea gets abused.
- How the loop looks at different speeds.

## The four stages

**Discovery.** Finding out what is worth building. Talking to users, reading support
tickets, studying the data, looking at competitors, testing rough ideas before anyone writes
code. The output is a problem worth solving, for a known group of people, with evidence that
they have it and some idea of what a solution might look like. Course 3 is entirely about
this stage.

**Definition.** Deciding what to build and writing it down. This is where strategy and
prioritisation happen (Course 4) and where requirements, user stories and success metrics get
written (Course 5). The output is something a team can build from: clear enough that the
engineers and designer know what done means, loose enough that they can find a better way.

**Delivery.** Building, testing and shipping it. Mostly engineering and design work, with the
PM close by answering questions, cutting scope when reality arrives, and planning the
launch. The output is a working product in users' hands. Course 5.

**Measurement.** Finding out whether it worked. Did the metric move? Did people use it? What
did they say? What did we learn that we did not know before? The output is a decision:
keep going, change course, or stop. Course 6.

## Why it is a loop

Draw the four stages in a line and the picture tells you something false: that the work
is done when the product ships. Draw them in a circle and the picture is right. Measurement
is the start of the next discovery. What you learn from shipping version one is the best
evidence you will ever have for what version two should be.

Teams that work in a line show three symptoms. They treat the launch date as the goal, so
scope gets protected and learning gets skipped. They have no measurement stage worth the
name, so nobody knows whether the last thing worked. And their discovery is a one-off at
the start of a project rather than a habit, so the longer the project runs, the staler its
assumptions get.

Teams that work in a loop ship smaller things more often, measure every one, and are
constantly talking to users. They are not slower. They waste less, because they find out
earlier when something is not working.

## The minimum viable product

The lean startup movement gave the loop a name, build, measure, learn, and a tool, the
minimum viable product. An MVP is the smallest thing you can build that tests the riskiest
assumption behind an idea. Its purpose is learning, not revenue, and not a shippable
version one.

The idea gets abused in two directions. Some teams use "MVP" to mean "a bad version of the
full product", which tests nothing because nobody can tell whether users hate the idea or
the execution. Others use it to mean "whatever we can ship by the deadline". Neither is an
MVP. The test is: what assumption does this let us check, and what will we do differently
depending on the answer? If you cannot answer that, it is not minimum, viable or a product.
It is just small.

A useful mental picture: if you are trying to find out whether people want to travel faster,
do not build a wheel, then an axle, then a chassis. Build a skateboard. It is a complete,
usable, embarrassingly simple answer to the question, and you learn from it.

## The loop at different speeds

The same loop runs at very different speeds depending on the product.

- A consumer app with millions of users can run a discovery-to-measurement cycle in a week:
  interview on Monday, ship a small change on Thursday, read the numbers the week after.
- A B2B product with quarterly customer contracts might take a quarter per loop, because
  customers need to adopt and the data takes time to arrive.
- A hardware product or a regulated one, such as medical software, may take a year, with
  most of the loop spent in definition and delivery and most of the learning done in
  prototypes before the expensive build.

Fast is better, but only if each loop produces real learning. A team that ships daily and
measures nothing is running in a line very quickly.

## What this means for you

For the rest of this course, every project you do sits at a stage of the loop, and every
later course goes deep on one stage. Knowing where you are tells you what good looks like:
in discovery, good means evidence; in definition, clarity; in delivery, a working product;
in measurement, an honest answer and a decision. Lesson 1.2.4 asks you to trace one real
feature all the way round.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Teresa Torres's [Product Discovery Basics](https://www.producttalk.org/2021/08/product-discovery/),
   fifteen minutes. The clearest short explanation of what discovery is for and why it has
   to be continuous rather than a phase. Note the distinction between discovery and
   delivery; the whole lesson above hangs on it.
2. Read Marty Cagan's [Product Discovery](https://www.svpg.com/product-discovery/), ten
   minutes. A second angle on the same stage, with the four questions a team must answer
   before it commits engineering time. Compare with Torres: they agree on the stage and
   differ on the method.
3. Read Henrik Kniberg's
   [Making Sense of MVP](https://blog.crisp.se/2016/01/25/henrikkniberg/making-sense-of-mvp),
   fifteen minutes. The source of the skateboard picture and the best short treatment of
   how the MVP idea is misused. Read it for the phrase "earliest testable product".
4. Read the [principles page](https://theleanstartup.com/principles) of The Lean Startup,
   five minutes. It is a summary of the book, which is where build, measure, learn comes
   from. The book itself is worth your time later; the page is enough for now.
5. Pick a product you use. Write down, in one line each, what its team probably learned at
   each of the four stages for one recent feature you noticed. You are guessing, and that is
   fine. The project at the end of this section turns the guess into research.
