## Introduction

Beyond the product team sits the rest of the company, and most of it has opinions about what
you should build. Sales has a deal that needs one feature. Marketing has a launch date.
Support has the same complaint forty times a week. Legal has a regulation. Leadership has a
strategy, or a hunch. All of them are right about something, and the PM is the person who
has to weigh them against each other and against the user.

This lesson introduces each group, what they are measured on, what they want from you, and
what you need from them. The relationships are different from the ones in lesson 1.3.1:
these groups are not building the product with you, but they decide whether it succeeds in
the market and whether it is allowed to exist at all.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Sales and customer success: the voice of the deal.
- Marketing: the voice of the market and the launch.
- Support: the voice of the user who is already unhappy.
- Legal, compliance and security: the voice of what is allowed.
- Leadership: the voice of the company's direction.
- A principle for all of them: translate, do not relay.

## Sales and customer success

**Measured on** revenue closed and revenue retained. **Want from you** the features that
close the deal in front of them, a roadmap they can show prospects, and fast answers to
"can we do X?" **You need from them** what prospects actually say, what loses deals, and
discipline about not promising what does not exist.

Sales is the loudest voice a B2B PM hears, and the most useful if you treat it as evidence
rather than instruction. One lost deal is an anecdote. The same objection in fifteen lost
deals is a pattern. Build a habit of reading call notes and asking "why did we lose?" and
you will know the market better than any survey can tell you.

The failure mode in both directions: a PM who builds whatever the biggest deal asks for ends
up with a product shaped like one customer. A PM who ignores sales ends up with a beautiful
product nobody can sell. The middle path is a clear roadmap, explained to sales in terms
of the problems it solves, and a clear process for the exceptions.

## Marketing

**Measured on** awareness, pipeline, launches landed. **Want from you** a product with a
clear story, firm dates, and early sight of what is coming so that they can prepare.
**You need from them** positioning, which is the single sentence that explains who the
product is for and why it is different, and the market context you do not have time to
gather yourself.

Product marketing, where it exists as a role, is your closest partner here: they turn what
you built into what the market hears. The sharpest point of friction is dates. Marketing
needs certainty to plan a launch; engineering cannot give it. Your job is to be honest early
and often about confidence, and to plan launches around what is actually ready rather than
what was promised.

## Support

**Measured on** tickets resolved, time to resolve, satisfaction. **Want from you** fewer
tickets, which means fixing the things that cause them, and warning before a change lands
so that they are not surprised by the users it confuses. **You need from them** the single
richest source of discovery evidence in the company, and usually the most ignored.

Support hears what users actually struggle with, in the users' own words, every day. A PM
who reads support tickets weekly, or better, takes a shift on the support desk, learns
things no dashboard shows. A PM who treats support as downstream of product, a place to send
the problems, has cut themselves off from the truth.

## Legal, compliance and security

**Measured on** risk avoided. **Want from you** to be involved early, before the design is
fixed, and to be told the truth about what the product does with data. **You need from
them** a clear picture of what is allowed, so that you do not discover a regulation after
building something.

The instinct is to see this group as the department of no. The reality is that most of
them are trying to find a way to yes, and the earlier they are involved the more ways
there are. Privacy, accessibility, financial regulation and AI-specific rules are all
product constraints now, and Course 7 returns to this in depth. For now: bring them a
problem in discovery, not a finished design in delivery.

## Leadership

**Measured on** the company's results and the board's confidence. **Want from you** to
know what is going on without having to ask, to see how your work connects to the strategy,
and to not be surprised. **You need from them** the strategy itself, in enough detail to
make decisions against, and air cover when your evidence disagrees with their instinct.

The hardest moment in the relationship is when a senior leader wants something the evidence
says is wrong. The phrase for the person with the highest salary winning the argument is
"HiPPO", the highest-paid person's opinion, and it is the single most common way good
product decisions die. The PM's tool is not confrontation. It is bringing the evidence,
proposing a cheap test, and being willing to be proved wrong. Course 2 covers this in detail;
lesson 1.3.3 gives you the start.

## Translate, do not relay

The principle for all five groups is the same. Each speaks in its own terms: sales in
deals, marketing in launches, support in tickets, legal in risks, leadership in strategy.
Your job is to translate each into the user problem or business outcome underneath it, weigh
it against the others, and decide. A PM who relays, who takes the sales request straight to
engineering as a ticket, has stopped doing the job. A PM who translates, who says "three
customers hit the same gap, here is the problem, here is why it matters more than the other
things on the list", is doing exactly the job.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Marty Cagan's
   [Product Management vs. Product Marketing](https://www.svpg.com/product-management-vs-product-marketing/),
   ten minutes. The cleanest statement of the boundary between the two roles and why both
   need the other.
2. Spend twenty minutes on April Dunford's site at [aprildunford.com](https://www.aprildunford.com/),
   starting with whatever she has most recently written on positioning. Positioning is the
   one marketing concept every PM must own, and she is its clearest teacher.
3. Read the chapter on support in Basecamp's [Getting Real](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal),
   titled "Feel the Pain", ten minutes including finding it. The argument that the people
   who build the product should answer its support requests is twenty years old and still
   right.
4. Read the Harvard Business Review classic [Managing Your Boss](https://hbr.org/2005/01/managing-your-boss),
   twenty minutes. Written for any employee, and the best short treatment of the leadership
   relationship a PM has to manage. Note the idea that it is a mutual dependence.
5. For the product you tore down in lesson 1.2.4, write one line for each of the five groups:
   what that group probably wanted from the feature, and whether the shipped version
   suggests they got it.
