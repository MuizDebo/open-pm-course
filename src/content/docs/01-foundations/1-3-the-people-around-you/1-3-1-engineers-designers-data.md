## Introduction

A product team is a PM, a designer, some engineers and, increasingly, someone who owns the
data. None of them report to you. All of them can make your week wonderful or miserable, and
you can do the same to theirs. This lesson is about the three crafts closest to you: how
each thinks, what each needs from a PM, and the habits that make PMs welcome or unwelcome
in their work.

The short version is the same for all three: respect the craft, bring the problem not the
solution, and never pretend to know their job better than they do.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- How engineers, designers and data people each see the product and the PM.
- What each needs from you to do their best work.
- The things PMs do that each finds exhausting.
- The shared principle: own the problem, share the solution.

## Engineers

**How they see it.** Engineers turn decisions into working systems, and they carry the
consequences of bad decisions longer than anyone: in bugs, in on-call pages, in code that
has to be maintained for years. They have usually seen several PMs come and go. They value
clarity, consistency and being told the truth about why.

**What they need from you.**

- *The problem and the constraints, before the solution.* "Users abandon checkout when the
  delivery estimate is missing" lets an engineer find the cheapest fix. "Add a delivery
  estimate widget to step three" forecloses it.
- *Priorities that hold.* Nothing burns trust faster than a priority that changes mid-sprint
  without explanation. If it must change, say why, in person.
- *Decisions when they ask for them.* An engineer blocked on a question for a day has lost a
  day. Answer fast, even if the answer is "I'll know by Thursday".
- *Protection from noise.* Part of your job is being the person stakeholders talk to so that
  engineers can work.

**What exhausts them.** Specs that change after they have started. Requirements that were
never discussed with them before being committed to a customer. Being asked "how long will
it take?" before anyone has said what "it" is. Technical debt treated as something they
made up to avoid feature work. Hearing about a bug from a customer before hearing about it
from you.

A useful habit: sit in their design discussions and ask questions, not to decide but to
understand. You will learn what is cheap and what is expensive, and that knowledge makes
every later prioritisation call better.

## Designers

**How they see it.** Designers hold the user's experience end to end, across the whole
product and not just the feature in front of you. They think in flows, states and edge
cases, and they can see inconsistency the way engineers can see a bug. Many have had their
work overruled by someone with an opinion and a title, and they remember.

**What they need from you.**

- *The user and the goal, not the screen.* "First-time users need to understand what the
  app does within ten seconds" is a brief. "Put a carousel on the home screen" is a
  solution, and probably a bad one.
- *Time to explore.* Good design comes from trying several things. If the schedule only
  allows one, say so up front rather than asking for options and then rejecting them for
  lack of time.
- *Feedback about outcomes, not taste.* "I'm worried users won't notice this button" is
  useful. "I don't like the blue" is not, unless you can say why it matters for the user.
- *A seat in discovery.* Designers who hear users directly produce better work than ones
  who get a summary. Bring them to interviews.

**What exhausts them.** Being handed wireframes by the PM and asked to "make them pretty".
Being brought in after the requirements are fixed. Design reviews where every stakeholder's
opinion counts equally. Being asked to justify spacing decisions to people who would never
be asked to justify a database choice.

The craft of giving design feedback has a name, critique, and Course 5 covers it properly.
For now: ask what problem the design is solving and whether it does, and leave the
aesthetics to the person trained in them.

## Data

**How they see it.** Analysts and data scientists know what the product actually does, as
opposed to what everyone thinks it does. They have seen metrics defined three ways in three
dashboards and watched teams celebrate numbers that meant nothing. They value precise
questions and are wary of being asked to find evidence for a decision already made.

**What they need from you.**

- *A decision the answer will change.* "Should we keep the onboarding tour? We'll remove it
  if completion is under 40 percent" is a question worth their time. "Can you pull some
  numbers on onboarding?" is not.
- *Instrumentation planned before launch, not after.* If the events are not logged, no
  amount of analysis afterwards can tell you what happened. Ask "how will we measure this?"
  in definition, not in measurement.
- *Honesty about uncertainty.* When they say the result is not significant, that is the
  answer, not an obstacle.
- *Credit.* Data work is invisible when it goes well. Say who found the insight.

**What exhausts them.** Requests with no decision attached. Being asked for the same metric
five different ways until one looks good. Dashboards nobody opens. Being handed a feature
after launch and asked to prove it worked when nothing was logged.

Course 6 teaches you enough analysis to ask good questions and read the answers critically.
It will not make you the data person, and it should not.

## The shared principle

Across all three: the PM owns the problem and shares the solution. You decide what is worth
solving and why. Engineers decide how to build it. Designers decide how it should work and
look. Data decides what the numbers can and cannot tell you. When a PM crosses those lines,
work gets worse and trust gets spent. When a PM holds the problem clearly and lets the
experts solve it, the team produces things the PM could never have specified.

This is the practical form of "accountable, not in charge" from lesson 1.1.1, and it is the
foundation for lesson 1.3.3 on influence.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Gergely Orosz's [The Product-Minded Software Engineer](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/the-product-minded-engineer/),
   fifteen minutes. It describes the engineers you most want on your team, and in doing so
   shows exactly what a good PM gives them room to do. Read it as a picture of the
   partnership from the engineering side.
2. Read Julie Zhuo's [How to Work with Designers](https://medium.com/the-year-of-the-looking-glass/how-to-work-with-designers-6c975dede146),
   ten minutes. Written by a design leader for the people who work with designers. Her list
   of what not to do is the one to memorise.
3. Read Cassie Kozyrkov's [What on earth is data science?](https://hackernoon.com/what-on-earth-is-data-science-eb1237d8cb37),
   ten minutes. A clear account of what the data roles actually do and where the boundaries
   between analyst, scientist and engineer fall, so that you ask the right person the right
   question.
4. Talk to one person in each craft, if you can find them: an engineer, a designer, someone
   who works with data. Ask one question: "What does the best PM you've worked with do that
   others don't?" Write down the three answers. If you cannot find all three, search for
   the question online and collect answers that way. Keep the notes for the section
   project.

</div>
