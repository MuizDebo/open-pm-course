## Introduction

Everything in this section comes down to one fact: nobody has to do what you say. The
engineers can build it differently. The designer can push back. Sales can promise something
else. Leadership can overrule you. And yet the product has to get built, well, and you are
accountable for it.

The skill that closes that gap is influence without authority. It is not charm, and it is
not politics in the bad sense. It is a small set of habits that make people want to work
with you and trust your judgement when it counts. This lesson introduces them. Course 2
develops them. You will practise them for the rest of your career.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Why authority would not help even if you had it.
- The four sources of influence a PM actually has: trust, evidence, clarity and reciprocity.
- Practical habits for building each.
- Disagree and commit: how to lose an argument well.
- What influence is not.

## Why authority would not help

Imagine you could order the team around. You would get compliance. You would not get the
engineer saying "there's a cheaper way to do this", the designer saying "users won't find
that", or the analyst saying "that metric is misleading". You would get what you asked for,
and what you asked for would be wrong more often than you think, because one person's
judgement is always worse than a team's.

The lack of authority is what forces the PM to persuade, and persuading forces the PM to
have reasons, and having reasons is what makes decisions good. Treat the constraint as the
design, not the bug.

## Four sources of influence

**Trust.** People do what you ask when they believe you are competent, honest and on their
side. Trust is built slowly, through hundreds of small interactions, and spent fast. You
build it by doing what you said, by telling people bad news early, by giving credit away and
taking blame on, and by never surprising anyone in a meeting with something you could have
told them beforehand.

**Evidence.** The PM's substitute for rank. "I think we should" loses to "I'd prefer not
to". "Twelve of the fifteen users we interviewed hit this, and here are the recordings" is
very hard to argue with. Evidence works best when it is specific, when it is shared before
the decision rather than produced to defend it, and when you are visibly willing to change
your mind if it points elsewhere.

**Clarity.** A clear written explanation of a problem, the options and a recommendation
does more persuading than any meeting. Most disagreements are people solving different
problems without realising it. Writing it down exposes that. A one-page document that
states what we are trying to do, why, what we are not doing and how we will know it worked
is the most powerful influence tool a PM has, and Course 2 teaches you to write it.

**Reciprocity.** Help people with their problems and they help with yours. Shield engineers
from a stakeholder and they take your word next time scope has to be cut. Give the designer
a real seat in discovery and they fight for your priorities in design review. Make sales look
good in front of a customer and they stop promising features you have not built. None of
this is cynical. It is how teams work.

## Habits that build them

- **Hold regular one-to-ones** with the engineering lead and the designer, even when nothing
  is wrong. Especially when nothing is wrong. The relationship you need in a crisis is built
  in the quiet weeks.
- **Pre-wire decisions.** Before a meeting where something will be decided, talk to the
  people who will be in it. Hear objections in private, where people can change their minds
  without losing face. Meetings should confirm decisions, not make them.
- **Write before you meet.** Send the one-pager the day before. People who have read the
  reasoning argue about the substance; people who have not argue about whatever they
  heard first.
- **Say what you do not know.** "I'm not sure, here's how we could find out" earns more
  trust than a confident guess that turns out wrong.
- **Give credit specifically and in public.** "This shipped because Amara found the
  cheaper approach" costs you nothing and buys you a great deal.
- **Ask for the other side.** "What would make this a bad idea?" Asked sincerely, it
  surfaces the objection before it becomes a blocker and shows that you want the truth more
  than you want to win.

## Disagree and commit

You will lose arguments. Sometimes the evidence is not enough, sometimes a leader decides
against you, sometimes the team simply sees it differently. The skill is losing well.
Disagree and commit means: make your case fully, with evidence, in the right room. If the
decision goes the other way, say so clearly once, and then support the decision as if it
were your own. No relitigating in the corridor. No "I told you so" when it goes wrong.

This is not weakness. It is what makes people willing to let you win next time, because
they know that if they lose, you will not make them pay for it. It also keeps the team
moving, which is worth more than most individual decisions. Course 2 has a project that asks
you to write a disagree-and-commit memo. Lesson 1.1.4's note about being wrong in public is
the other half of the same skill.

## What influence is not

Influence is not manipulation. If you find yourself framing things to get a result rather
than to show the truth, stop. People notice, and once they do, you have no influence at
all, because trust was the whole foundation.

It is not avoiding conflict. A PM who agrees with everyone has no influence either, because
nothing they say carries information. Holding a position under pressure, kindly and with
reasons, is part of the job.

And it is not a performance. The PMs with the most influence are rarely the most visible.
They are the ones whose documents get read, whose estimates people believe, and who the
engineers go to when something is wrong.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read Robert Cialdini's
   [Harnessing the Science of Persuasion](https://hbr.org/2001/10/harnessing-the-science-of-persuasion),
   twenty minutes. The six principles of influence, from the researcher who defined them.
   Reciprocity, consistency and social proof are the ones a PM uses daily. Read it also as a
   warning: the same principles are how you get manipulated.
2. Read Lara Hogan's [Questions for our first 1:1](https://larahogan.me/blog/first-one-on-one-questions/),
   five minutes. Written for managers, and exactly right for a PM starting with a new
   engineering lead or designer. Steal the questions.
3. Read Rands's [The Update, The Vent, and The Disaster](https://randsinrepose.com/archives/the-update-the-vent-and-the-disaster/),
   fifteen minutes. The best thing written about one-to-ones: what they are for and how to
   tell which kind you are in. You will hold a lot of them.
4. Read Amazon's [Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles),
   ten minutes, specifically "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit". It is the source of the
   phrase and a compact statement of what it demands: the backbone part as much as the
   commit part.
5. Think of the last time you needed someone who did not report to you to do something and
   it did not go well. Write half a page: which of the four sources of influence was
   missing, and what you would do differently. Private, but keep it.

</div>
