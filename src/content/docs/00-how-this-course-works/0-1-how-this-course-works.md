## Introduction

This is a free, open-source course for becoming a product manager. It is built the way
[The Odin Project](https://www.theodinproject.com/) teaches web development: a single path of
lessons and projects, curated from the best material already on the web, with your own work
done in public and reviewed by other learners.

It exists because the best product management resources are scattered across GitHub
read-mes, blog posts, university syllabi and podcasts, and none of them tell you what order
to read them in or make you practise. This course does both. It also covers the parts most
PM resources skip: what the job does to you, how to be accountable for outcomes you do not
control, and how to build products with AI in them.

By the end you should be able to walk into a product team and do the work, and walk into a
PM interview and show the artifacts that prove it.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- Who this course is for and who it is not for.
- How the path is shaped: courses, sections, lessons, projects.
- The difference between a lesson and a project, and why projects matter more.
- How to pace yourself and what a realistic finish looks like.
- Why the course is open source and what that means for you.

## Who this is for

You fit if any of these are true:

- You want to become a product manager and do not know where to start.
- You are already a PM, learned on the job, and want to fill the gaps you can feel but
  cannot name.
- You are an engineer, designer, analyst or founder who keeps doing PM work and wants to do
  it on purpose.
- You want to work on AI products and need the product side, not another model tutorial.

You will struggle if you want a certificate, a guarantee of a job, or a course you can finish
by reading alone. There is no certificate. The proof of completion is the portfolio of
artifacts you build along the way, which is what hiring managers actually look at.

## The shape of the path

The path has ten courses. Each course is made of sections, and each section is made of
lessons and projects. You go in order. Later courses assume the vocabulary and artifacts of
earlier ones.

| Course | What it covers | Weeks |
|---|---|---|
| 0 How this course works | This course. Tools, submissions, getting help. | 0.5 |
| 1 Foundations | What a PM does, the product lifecycle, the people around you. | 3 |
| 2 The PM mind | Accountability, decisions under uncertainty, communication, the hard parts. | 3 |
| 3 Discovery and research | Framing problems, talking to users, sizing markets. | 3 |
| 4 Strategy and prioritisation | Vision, narrative, goals, roadmaps, saying no with a framework. | 3 |
| 5 Definition and delivery | Specs, working with engineering and design, shipping. | 4 |
| 6 Data and metrics | Metrics that matter, experiments, dashboards. | 2 |
| 7 AI product management | How models work, where AI belongs, designing for uncertainty, evals. | 5 |
| 8 Career | Ladders, portfolios, networking, interviews, negotiation. | 3 |
| 9 Capstone | One product, end to end, every artifact. | 3 |

Thirty weeks at eight to ten focused hours a week. The full map, with every lesson and
project named, is in
[CURRICULUM.md](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/blob/main/CURRICULUM.md) in the
repository.

## Lessons and projects

A **lesson** is something you read, watch and think about. Every lesson ends with an
Assignment: three to five things to read, watch or do, each with a sentence on why. The
assignment is the lesson. The text above it is the map; the links are the territory.

A **project** is something you make. An audience map, a product requirements document, a
roadmap, an eval suite, a recorded pitch. Projects are the artifacts a working PM produces,
and they are the only part of this course that anyone else will ever see. You submit them in
the repository's Discussions, and you review other people's. Lesson 0.3 explains how.

If you are short on time, skip lesson reading before you skip a project. A PM who has done
five user interviews and written one PRD is further along than one who has read fifty
articles about them.

## Pacing

The course is self-paced, which is a polite way of saying nobody will chase you. Three
things make people finish:

1. **A fixed weekly block.** Two or three sessions of three hours beat ten scattered
   half-hours. Put it in your calendar before you start Course 1.
2. **Public commitment.** Introduce yourself in Discussions and say when you plan to finish
   each course. Other learners are the accountability structure.
3. **Finishing projects, not lessons.** Count progress by artifacts produced. The sidebar
   will show you where you are.

If you are already a PM, you may move through Courses 1 and 3 to 6 quickly. Do not skip
Course 2 or the projects. They are where experienced PMs most often find the gaps.

## Why open source

Every lesson in this course is a Markdown file in a public repository, licensed so that
anyone can copy, improve and redistribute it. That is deliberate.

- **You can fix it.** Every page has an "Edit page" link. A broken link, an unclear
  paragraph, a better resource: open a pull request. Lesson 0.4 explains how.
- **It stays honest.** Sources and their licences are listed. Where a framework is
  contested, the lesson says so. There is no product to sell you.
- **It outlives its author.** The curriculum map is the source of truth. If the author
  disappears, the course does not.

The reading lists are curated from the strongest open resources on GitHub, including
[Open Product Management](https://github.com/ProductHired/open-product-management),
[Awesome Product Management](https://github.com/dend/awesome-product-management) and
[Awesome AI PM](https://github.com/aakashg/awesome-ai-pm). The structure and the project
sequence are modelled on Cornell Tech's product management studio course. The writing in
between is original and yours to improve.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Skim the full [curriculum map](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/blob/main/CURRICULUM.md).
   Do not read the detail. Get a feel for the size and the order, and note the two or three
   projects that look most useful to you right now. Those are your motivation when the
   middle weeks drag.
2. Read The Odin Project's own
   [How this course will work](https://www.theodinproject.com/lessons/foundations-how-this-course-will-work)
   lesson, ten minutes. This course borrows its model on purpose, and their explanation of
   why reading alone does not work applies here word for word.
3. Watch Barbara Oakley's talk
   [Learning how to learn](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O96fE1E-rf8), eighteen minutes.
   Focused and diffuse modes, chunking and the illusion of competence explain why the
   projects in this course exist and why re-reading a lesson feels productive but is not.
4. Write two hundred words on why you are here and what you want to be able to do when you
   finish. Keep it somewhere you will see again. You will be asked to reread it in the
   capstone.
