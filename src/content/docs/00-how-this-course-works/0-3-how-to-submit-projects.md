## Introduction

Every project in this course ends the same way: you put the artifact in your work
repository, post a link in the project's thread in the course's GitHub Discussions, and
review two other submissions. This lesson explains that loop, why it matters, and how to
give and take feedback without it going badly.

There is no grader. The feedback you get comes from other learners, and the feedback you
give is part of your own learning. Reading five other people's PRDs teaches you more about
PRDs than writing one.

## Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The four steps of a submission.
- What a good submission post looks like.
- How to review someone else's work usefully.
- How to handle work that uses confidential material.

## Why submit at all

Nobody is checking. You could do every project privately and finish the course. Three
reasons not to:

- **Feedback is the fastest way to improve.** You cannot see what is unclear in your own
  roadmap. A stranger can, in thirty seconds.
- **Deadlines you announce are deadlines you keep.** Saying "submitting this by Sunday" in a
  public thread works on most people.
- **The thread is the course's memory.** Twenty submissions for the same project, with
  comments, are a better teaching resource than any lesson text. Yours makes it better for
  the next person.

## The four steps

1. **Finish the artifact** as the project's Assignment describes. Done means every numbered
   requirement is met. Extra credit is extra.
2. **Put it in your work repository**, in a folder named by the project number, as set up in
   lesson 0.2. If the artifact is a Google Doc or a Figma board, the folder holds a
   `README.md` with the link and sharing set to "anyone with the link can comment".
3. **Post in the project's thread.** Open the course's
   [Discussions](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/discussions), find the thread for
   that project (the project page links to it), and reply using the template below.
4. **Review two other submissions** in the same thread, ideally the two most recent ones
   without replies. Use the review guide below.

## The submission template

Copy this into your reply and fill it in. Short is fine.

```markdown
**Project:** 3.2.5 Five interviews
**Link:** https://github.com/yourname/pm-course-work/tree/main/3-2-5-five-interviews
**Time spent:** about 6 hours
**What I'm least sure about:** whether my synthesis is insights or just a summary of what people said
**Feedback I'd most like:** is the opportunity I picked actually supported by the notes?
```

The last two lines are the important ones. They tell reviewers where to look and make it
easy to be useful in five minutes. Submissions that say "any feedback welcome" get less of it.

## How to review

A useful review takes five to ten minutes and answers the submitter's question first. Then,
if you have more to say:

- **Say what worked, specifically.** "The segment table made the wedge obvious" is useful.
  "Nice work" is not.
- **Ask before you assert.** "Did you consider X?" and "What made you rule out Y?" are
  questions a PM will hear in every review for the rest of their career. Practise asking
  them well.
- **One or two things to change, not ten.** Pick the ones that would most improve the
  artifact. Leave the rest.
- **Critique the work, never the person.** The product manager's job is to hear hard
  feedback on their work and not take it personally. Model that for each other.

If a submission is clearly rushed or incomplete, say so plainly and kindly, and name the
missing requirement. That is more respectful than a vague "looks good".

## Taking feedback

You will get feedback you disagree with. Reply with what you are going to change and what
you are not, and why. Do not defend every point. Do not go silent. "Thanks, I'll update the
sizing but I'm keeping the wedge because the interviews pointed there" is a complete PM
response. Course 2 goes deeper on this. For now, practise it.

## Confidential material

Many learners are already working PMs and will want to use real problems from their job.
Good. Just do not publish anything your employer would object to. The rule: if you would
not paste it into a public tweet, do not put it in a public repository.

Options, in order of preference:

1. **Sanitise.** Change the names, round the numbers, remove the identifying detail. The
   structure of your reasoning is what gets reviewed, and that survives sanitising.
2. **Pick a different product.** Every project can be done on a product you merely use. A
   bank app, a bus ticketing system, a recipe site.
3. **Submit privately.** Post in the thread that you completed the project and what you
   learned, without the link. You lose the review, but you keep the accountability.

Never post customer data, internal metrics, unreleased plans or anything under a
non-disclosure agreement, sanitised or not.

## Assignment

<div class="assignment">

1. Read GitHub's short
   [About discussions](https://docs.github.com/en/discussions/collaborating-with-your-community-using-discussions/about-discussions)
   page, five minutes, so you know what threads, categories and marked answers are.
2. Go to the course's [Discussions](https://github.com/MuizDebo/open-pm-course/discussions)
   and introduce yourself in the General category: who you are, why you are here, and
   which course you plan to have finished in a month. This is your first public
   commitment.
3. Reply to at least one other introduction. Ask one real question. This is a dry run of
   the review habit before any project is at stake.
4. Read [How to ask good questions](https://jvns.ca/blog/good-questions/) by Julia Evans,
   fifteen minutes. It is about technical questions, and every principle in it applies to
   asking for feedback on product work: state what you tried, say what you expected, and
   make it easy to answer.

</div>
